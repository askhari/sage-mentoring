## Additive and subtractive mixing (color18a.html; color14.html; color18b.html)
- Additive mixing describes how the eye responds to overlapping lights, not a physical blending of light; any light colour can be matched by at most three lights. Heading "Additive Color Mixing / Additive Mixtures Occur In The Eye" (color18a). → additive-mixing
- Example: overlapping yellow and blue light give white, not green. "Additive Color Mixing" (color18a). → additive-mixing
- The RGB lights are a convention; the "true" additive primaries are the invisible L, M, S cone responses. "The True Additive Primaries Are Invisible" / "How Do We Choose the RGB Lights?" (color18a). → additive-mixing
- The geometric colour-wheel method works well for mixtures of light but is much less precise for paint. "mixing with a color wheel" (color14). → additive-mixing
- Paint mixing is subtractive in the sense that the mixture absorbs more light than either component; mixtures darken and dull. "Subtractive Mixtures Always Increase Light Absorptance" (color18a). → subtractive-mixing
- The page calls subtractive theory only an approximation, not a rigorous theory; the mixture's colour results from the pigments' reflectance curves. "Subtractive Color Mixture" (color18a). → subtractive-mixing
- Paint mixing follows subtractive mixing, but "substance uncertainty" shapes the practical gamut. "The Behavior of Paint Mixtures" (color18b). → subtractive-mixing
- Modern subtractive primaries are cyan, yellow, magenta; traditional red-yellow-blue was limited by the pigments historically available. "The CYM Subtractive 'Primary' Colors" (color18a). → subtractive-mixing
- Note for the caller: in the visible text MacEvoy does NOT say paint mixing is "not subtractive"; he treats it as subtractive but only approximately predictable by simple subtractive models (see pigment-mixing-behavior). → subtractive-mixing

## Primary colors (color18a.html; color13.html; color18b.html)
- Primaries used to explain colour mixture are imaginary: quoted as "all imaginary" (misconceptions in traditional color theory, item 2, color18a). → primary-colors
- Real pigments are always imperfect, so no three material primaries can mix all colours; ideal subtractive primaries stimulate two cone types strongly and the third weakly. "True additive primaries are invisible" section (color18a); also "always imaginary or imperfect" (color14). → primary-colors
- Three paints matching the subtractive primaries are violet-red (magenta), yellow and green-blue (cyan). "Primary Color Palettes" (color18b). → primary-colors
- Artists choose a primary triad for three reasons: large gamut, analytical mixing recipes, desirable harmonies. "Limited Palette" claims, color13 (heading "Primary color wheel"). → primary-colors
- Gamut is the range of hues/saturations a palette can mix; plotting the pigments and joining them shows it, and a larger enclosed area means more mixtures. "Gamut" (color14). → primary-colors
- A primary triad may fail to mix a dark neutral visually equal to black. "Four Palette Limitations" (color18b). → primary-colors
- Conclusion line: "the color wheel is not a color theory". color13. → primary-colors

## Limited palettes (color13.html; color18b.html; color14.html; mix.html)
- Six paints (primary triad plus three secondaries) give the most chroma per paint; the secondary palette is called the most efficient. "More is less? A gamut comparison" (color13). → limited-palette
- Almost all the mixing potential of watercolours can be reached with six paints. "Six Color Palettes" (color18b). → limited-palette
- The split-primary palette is a 19th-century attempt to widen the triad gamut while keeping 18th-century primary theory; it gives a smaller gamut biased along warm/cool and needs more awkward mixing than the six-paint palette. "Six Color Palettes" (color18b). → limited-palette
- A four-paint method is called needlessly complicated and wasteful; three paints can do the job more reliably. "Four-paint method" (mix). → limited-palette
- Any colour can be mixed from three suitable paints, usually a close-hued pair plus a third to adjust saturation/value. "Triads" (color14). → limited-palette

## Complementary colors (color13.html; tech17.html; mixtable.html; color12.html)
- Visual, mixing and afterimage complements often differ; one hue can have several mixing complements, so they cannot all sit at one wheel point. "Mixing Complements" (color13). → complementary-colors
- Opposite wheel colours mixed pull toward gray or black. "Complements" (color14). → complementary-colors
- Three paints forming a triangle around the neutral point make a gray; two-paint complements are more convenient and often give darker neutrals. "Mixing complements" (tech17). → complementary-colors
- Most mixing complements of blues and blue-greens are warm colours; yellow rarely works as one. "Mixing complements" (tech17). → complementary-colors
- Some mixing complements contradict colour-wheel logic, so they cannot be shown on a wheel and must be learned per paint on your palette. (tech17). → complementary-colors
- Test: 21 cool blue/green paints against 50 warm paints, 1500+ mixtures measured by spectrophotometer; pure gray = chroma <= 2, near gray = chroma <= 5; 170+ pairs gave pure or near grays. "Results and Method" (mixtable). → complementary-colors
- Neutralizing pairs are scattered irregularly, so a consistent mixing wheel is impossible; complements must be defined by specific pigments, and the results apply to watercolour (acrylic/oil may differ). (mixtable). → complementary-colors
- Warm/cool hue pairs (e.g. red-orange and green-blue) are described as "metacomplementary". "Color temperature" (color12). → complementary-colors
- Near neutrals: next to a saturated colour, an analogous near neutral makes it look more saturated and a complementary one makes it more distinct. "Near neutrals and color design" (tech17). → complementary-colors
- Burnt sienna and venetian red are workhorse neutralizers; raw umber is useful for reddish blues. (tech17). → complementary-colors

## Tints, shades, tones (color12.html; color11.html; color13.html)
- Tint: mixing with white gives pastels and never gives the darker unsaturated colours (brown, ochre). "Unsaturated color zones" (color12). → tints-shades-tones
- Mixing with black lowers both lightness and chroma and yields the unsaturated colours (brown, ochre, green-gold). (color12). → tints-shades-tones
- Hue shift: adding white shifts oranges and deep yellows toward yellow and reds toward violet; black or gray can shift hues otherwise. (color12). → tints-shades-tones
- "Colors lose saturation in shades"; for shadows add a near-neutral dark (neutral tint, ivory/lamp black). "Hue, lightness and saturation" (color11). → tints-shades-tones
- Gray steps can be made by mixing black and white paint; mixing error limits a painter to about eleven usable values. "Painting values" (color11). → tints-shades-tones
- Carbon blacks are really dark grays of reflectance about 20; a white paint "cannot" be mixed. (color11; color13). → tints-shades-tones
- Diluting paint or adding dark neutral changes lightness and chroma; dilution dulls saturated mixtures. "Tints and Shades" (color13). → tints-shades-tones
Not found: no formal definition of "tone" and no claim that black plus yellow gives green in the pages read (the page itself does not define tints/shades).

## Pigment mixing behavior (color14.html; color13.html; color18a.html; color18b.html)
- Light mixtures fall on straight lines in a chromaticity diagram, but "paint mixing lines are erratic" and often curve on a wheel. "Mixing Lines" (color14). → pigment-mixing-behavior
- Complementary-pair lines (a*b* plane) fan out or skew; a mixing wheel cannot accurately place pigments. "Mixing lines" (color18a). → pigment-mixing-behavior
- Mixing paints far apart on the wheel costs saturation ("saturation costs"). (color13; color14 headings). → pigment-mixing-behavior
- Subtractive-primary mixing lines bow outward (less chroma loss than expected); additive-primary (orange, green, violet) lines bow inward with larger chroma and lightness losses. "The Relative Effects of Color Mixtures" (color18b). → pigment-mixing-behavior
- Additive-type primaries (red-orange, green, blue-violet) darken strongly and can mix a synthetic black. "Primary Color Palettes" (color18b). → pigment-mixing-behavior
- Paints with the same visual colour can have different reflectance curves and mix differently; visual colour does not predict the mixture. Reflectance also changes wet/dry, thick/thin. "Substance Uncertainty" (color18a). → pigment-mixing-behavior
- Two yellows with near-identical reflectance can mix differently because refractive index differs. "Particle size, refractive index and hiding power" (color18a). → pigment-mixing-behavior
- Mixtures change on drying; mark shifts with arrows; let swatches dry at least 10 min. (color13; tech17). → pigment-mixing-behavior

## Optical (partitive) mixing and glazing (color18a.html; color18b.html; mix.html)
- Partitive mixing: when small dots fuse visually the eye averages their reflectance rather than adding light; fused red and green ink dots look dull and dark. "Partitive Mixture" / "Don't Confuse Additive & Subtractive Mixtures" (color18a). → optical-mixing
- Printers use overprinted cyan, yellow, magenta inks, mixing subtractively within a dot, for finer texture. (color18a). → optical-mixing
- Glazing = painting one paint over a dried layer; the shift is larger when darker over lighter, cooler over warmer, or more saturated over less saturated. "The Relative Effects of Color Mixtures" (color18b). → optical-mixing
- Glaze order matters: cadmium yellow over phthalo green gives a lighter, less saturated result than the reverse. "Other Factors in Material Mixtures" (color18a). → optical-mixing
- Recommended glaze order: light to dark, warm to cool, opaque to transparent. "Wet-in-wet and glazing" (mix). → optical-mixing
- Pointillism is not named in the visible text of any page read. → optical-mixing

## Target-colour mixing (mix.html)
- Use the wheel as a directional guide, not a calculator; the 8-step method: (1) locate hue (clock position) and chroma (distance from centre) of the target; lightness is adjusted separately by water, white or black. → target-color-mixing
- (2) Choose paint pairs whose mixing line passes outside the target or near the centre: hue-shift pairs for saturated targets, chroma-shift pairs for dull ones. (3) Add a third paint forming a triangle around the target if needed. → target-color-mixing
- (4) Two mixing colours set hue/texture; the "adjusting colour" (usually the paint farthest from the target) tunes chroma and value. Start more saturated than the target because paints dull when dry. → target-color-mixing
- (5) Aim for the hue the third paint will shift toward, and account for tinting strength. (6) Dilute the weaker paint on the palette to cover the whole area. → target-color-mixing
- (7) Add the dominant paint gradually, test on paper, let dry; (8) add the adjusting colour sparingly; discard excess rather than chasing the colour. → target-color-mixing

## Later domains (pigmt1.html; mixtable.html; mix.html)
- Smaller particles raise tinting strength (more surface area per mass); very strong pigments such as phthalocyanines may need extra vehicle/filler. "Tinting Strength" (pigmt1). → tinting-strength
- A stronger-tinting paint dominates a mixture beyond its proportion. "Estimate proportions" (mix); "Particle size, refractive index and hiding power" (color18a). → tinting-strength
- Smaller particles and higher tinting strength allow thinner application, hence more transparency; diluting makes even opaque paints more transparent. "Transparency" (pigmt1). → transparency-opacity
- Cadmium/phthalo mixtures can look cooler than they are because cadmiums sink: judge by dry swatch. (mixtable). → transparency-opacity
- Masstone and undertone are not covered on pigmt1 (not found on pages read). → masstone-undertone
- Colour Index names are published by the Society of Dyers and Colourists (e.g. PY3 for arylide yellow 10G); the name does not guarantee quality or lightfastness, and one pigment is sold under many trademarks. "Colour Index (C.I.) Names" (pigmt1). → colour-index-names
- The mixing-complements table lists each pigment with its C.I. name and manufacturer, and flags pigments whose hue varies by maker. "How to use the table" (mixtable). → colour-index-names

### Paint composition (pigmt1; pigmt2)
- Paint is pigment dispersed in a liquid vehicle that suspends it and binds it to the paper when dry. "Paint Composition" (pigmt1). → paint-composition
- Gum arabic is the traditional watercolour binder (since the 18th century); a few brands use synthetic glycol. It is a weak binder, redissolvable in water after drying. "Paint Composition" (pigmt1). → paint-composition
- Other vehicle parts: glycerin as plasticizer, sugar syrup or honey as humectant, dextrin as thickening filler; ox-gall-type dispersants speed milling (and make wet-in-wet diffuse more); preservatives stop mould. "Paint Composition" (pigmt1). → paint-composition
- Tube paint contains about 15% water by volume; binder is about 65% of the vehicle volume (pigment-to-vehicle ranges are labelled illustrative). "Paint Composition" (pigmt1). [data, approximate] → paint-composition
- Brand quality is judged on pigment, binder, additives and handling; cheaper lines tend to use less pigment, substitute pigments, more dispersant and added fillers/brighteners, and "price is no indication of product quality". Overview and "Paint composition" (pigmt2). [opinion based on his tests] → paint-composition
- Single-pigment paints are preferred; convenience mixtures usually reduce purity and lightfastness (share of single-pigment paints varies by brand, e.g. 86% Daniel Smith vs 46% Lukas). "Pigment" (pigmt2). [data + opinion] → paint-composition
- Fillers and brighteners may degrade colour, and brighteners may compromise lightfastness. (pigmt1). [opinion] → paint-composition
- Particle size affects staining and hue: finer particles stain more and, within one pigment, are lighter and less saturated (e.g. cobalt teal blue: grayish fine particles on top, greener coarser ones underneath). (pigmt1). → paint-composition

### Colour Index names (pigmt1; pigmt2; palette1)
- Pigments are identified by Colour Index generic codes (e.g. PW6 titanium white, PY3 arylide yellow 10G, PB15:3 phthalo blue GS), published by the Society of Dyers and Colourists. "Colour Index Names" (pigmt1). → colour-index-names
- Paints carrying the same C.I. name can differ in lightfastness, depending on particle size and manufacturer. (pigmt1). → colour-index-names
- He treats clear disclosure of C.I. names on the tube as a mark of quality; labels vary (generic name, vague chemical class, only a lightfastness rating) and some are wrong (he cites Lukas for at least two misidentifications). "Colour Index" (pigmt2). [opinion/his findings] → colour-index-names
- Marketing names are unreliable: one name such as "indian yellow" can cover several different pigments, and behaviour varies by brand. "Choosing a limited palette" (palette2). → colour-index-names
- Pigments fall in four classes (natural inorganic, synthetic inorganic, natural organic, synthetic organic); nearly all in use are synthetic, mostly organic; pigments within one chemical family (quinacridones, naphthols) usually share lightfastness and colour traits. "Pigment properties" (palette1). → colour-index-names

### Transparency and opacity (pigmt1; palette1; palette2; pigmt8)
- Smaller particles generally make a more transparent watercolour; higher tinting strength allows thinner application, also more transparent; diluting even an opaque paint makes it more transparent. "Transparency" (pigmt1). → transparency-opacity
- Transparency is rated by the hiding power of the masstone over black lines on white paper; few pigments are truly transparent and most become transparent when diluted. "Criteria" (palette2). → transparency-opacity
- Opacity often comes from small particle size or heavy pigments that settle. (palette2). → transparency-opacity
- His pigment-guide ratings use a 0-4 scale (4 = pure form of the attribute), e.g. transparency Tr 2 for Daniel Smith raw sienna vs Tr 4 for W&N raw umber (as printed in the examples). "Ratings" (pigmt8). [data, scale as reported] → transparency-opacity
- ⚠️ [opinion] The "transparency fetish" is overblown, since almost no modern pigment is transparent as painters assume, and opaque paints "get a bum rap"; he rejects Kosvanec's rule against mixing staining and opaque pigments. "Criteria" (palette2). → transparency-opacity
- Example lists: transparent (hansa yellow light, quinacridone magenta, ultramarine, phthalo blue) vs opaque (cadmium yellow, cadmium red, cerulean, Chinese white). "Example palettes" (palette2). → transparency-opacity

### Tinting strength (pigmt1; pigmt8; palette2)
- Staining is an effect on the paper surface, different from tinting strength, which is an effect in the liquid; the two are often confused. "Criteria" (palette2); "Tinting strength" (pigmt8). → tinting-strength
- Value range, tinting strength and dilution together decide how dominant a paint is in a mixture: a dark, strongly tinting, little-diluted paint has the largest effect. (pigmt8). → tinting-strength
- Strong pigments (phthalocyanines, dioxazine violet) are cut with vehicle or filler to match other paints in a line; expensive high-tinting organics can still be economical because little is needed. "Tinting Strength" (pigmt1). → tinting-strength
- He gives no formal definition or numeric tinting-strength rating there. (pigmt8). → tinting-strength

### Masstone and undertone (pigmt8; pigmt2)
- Masstone = the paint's full-strength colour; he measures it just below the transparency test lines and uses it for hue angle and value range because it is close to typical paint concentration and usually has the highest chroma. "Masstone" (pigmt8). → masstone-undertone
- Undertone = the colour of a diluted wash (tint). Hue shift = undertone hue angle minus masstone hue angle on the CIELAB plane; positive means counterclockwise. "Undertone" (pigmt8). [data definition] → masstone-undertone
- Ratings examples include hue shift (HS) values such as +13 for Daniel Smith raw sienna and +5 for W&N raw umber. "Ratings examples" (pigmt8). [data] → masstone-undertone
- Usage in brand review: masstone means full-strength paint as opposed to diluted tints (e.g. Art Spectrum minerals "whitish bronze" in masstone). (pigmt2). → masstone-undertone
- Lightfastness differs between masstone and tint for some pigments (see below). (pigmt9). → masstone-undertone

### Lightfastness (pigmt1; pigmt9; pigmt8; pigmt2)
- Lightfastness is determined by the pigment's molecular structure; organics tend to fade or vanish, inorganics gray or darken; modern synthetic organics are far more durable than natural ones; smaller particles are generally less lightfast, especially in synthetic organics. (pigmt1; pigmt3 "lightfastness testing"). → lightfastness
- Blue wool scale: eight dyed reference strips, 1 (most fugitive) to 8 (most permanent); a paint's rating is the last reference not yet changed when the paint changes. "Lightfastness ratings" (pigmt9). → lightfastness
- ASTM D5383 categories as he lists them (blue wool equivalents): I superior 7.1-8.0, II good 6.1-7.0, III fair 5.5-6.0, IV inferior 2.2-5.4, V fugitive 0-2.1; III and below unsuitable for art. ASTM D5398 uses: no change / small change / large change / bleached white; only "no change" suitable. (pigmt9). → lightfastness
- His guide rates each paint by the blue wool level at which significant change appeared, as two numbers (tint, full strength): 8 and 7 lightfast, 6 marginal, 4-5 impermanent, 2-3 fugitive, 1 very fugitive; he calls the ratings advisory and advises readers to run their own tests. "Lightfastness" (pigmt8). → lightfastness
- Quick test: 1:8 diluted swatches on cold-pressed paper (300 gsm+) under glass, half masked with aluminium tape, in an unshaded south window for four months (May-October); any visible tape shadow means unsuitable. (pigmt9). → lightfastness
- Comprehensive test: alizarin crimson (PR83, about 1:12) is the exposure meter; expose other samples twice as long as it takes alizarin to fade (four times for high confidence); paint each swatch as a stroke from masstone to tint; view under daylight/5000-7500K light, CRI 89+. (pigmt9). → lightfastness
- Use soda-lime glass or Acrylite OP-4, not UV-protective glass; best season about 15 April-15 September; alizarin typically fails in 4-6 weeks under clear skies in most of the USA. (pigmt9). [data/experience] → lightfastness
- Limits of standards (his critique): judging contrast is subjective (light yellows/oranges vs dark wool strips), some paints darken or gray (Chinese white whitens), fade rates vary (steady, collapse, or stop), and he argues ASTM's dilution (about 40% reflectance, calculated) is unlike painting practice. (pigmt9). [mix of data and opinion] → lightfastness
- Observed: PBr7, PR101, PR108, PY35, PY42, PY184 discolour in concentrated samples while looking lightfast diluted; aureolin grays in masstone and fades in tints. (pigmt9). [data from his tests] → lightfastness
- ⚠️ [opinion] "Wisdom of experience": diluted paints are usually less lightfast. (pigmt9). → lightfastness
- Fugitive pigments exist in commercial lines; he retested lightfastness in 2004; Dr. Ph. Martin's Radiant dyes are called "equally fugitive". (pigmt2; pigmt5). → lightfastness
- palette1 tiers: "highly recommended" (lightfast, versatile, handle well), reliable, unreliable/fugitive (e.g. alizarin crimson), discontinued. [opinion] → lightfastness

### Limited palette (palette1; palette2)
- palette1 indexes 100+ watercolour pigments in 27 colour categories on a wheel, high-chroma on the rim, earth colours inside, with one representative paint per category; it gives no limited-palette recipe. (palette1). → limited-palette
- Choose paints by handling (transparency, opacity, staining, granulation) and by dry appearance (saturation, texture, value). "Criteria" (palette2). → limited-palette
- Suggested approach: a core of general-purpose paints plus a few special-effect paints, with pigments of similar or harmonizing traits. "Choosing a limited palette" (palette2). → limited-palette
- Categories he lists: transparent, opaque, nonstaining (cobalt blue, naples yellow), staining (phthalo blue, prussian blue, dioxazine violet), saturated, earth (raw/burnt sienna, ochre), textured/granulating (ultramarine, cerulean, viridian). (palette2). → limited-palette
- Granulation scale 0-4 (liquid to coarse); staining 0-4 from a wet-swab wipe test; staining depends on paper and sizing, not just pigment. (pigmt8; palette2). [data scales] → limited-palette
- ⚠️ [opinion] "Style and technique trump almost every other consideration"; earths are among his favourites; beginners overchoose saturated colours; "choose what gives you pleasure". (palette2). → limited-palette
- Pigment-family examples (palette1 bold = highly recommended): cadmium yellow/orange/red, quinacridone red, ultramarine, cobalt, cerulean, phthalo green, viridian, sienna/umber earths, lamp black. The bold list is a reader's inference, not his stated palette. (palette1). → limited-palette
- For his mixing-based six-paint palettes see "Limited palettes" above (color13, color18b). → limited-palette
