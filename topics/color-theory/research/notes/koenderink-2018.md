## Optical mixing / pointillism
- Pointillists and Divisionists replaced tonal (value-based) palettes with chromatic ones and relied on "optical mixture", which the authors describe as a mental blend of separately visible dots (Abstract; Introduction) → optical-mixing
- They call the mixing one done "by the mind, not the light", and say the dots should remain visible rather than fuse (Introduction, chromatic vs. tonal contrast) → optical-mixing
- Before French Impressionism painting relied on tonal values; later styles suppressed tonal contrast and used chromatic modulation (Introduction) → optical-mixing
- Seurat's early work is described as looking drab from afar and vivid up close, which led later artists to space dots further apart (Introduction) → optical-mixing
- Authors claim vision science has added little to the artists' empirical discoveries, and that optical mixture needs colour-description methods beyond standard colorimetry (Abstract; Introduction) → optical-mixing
- Pointillist dots may cross region borders, which unites regions instead of dividing them (Introduction, edges and regions; Fig. 1 Signac, *La Rivière*) → optical-mixing
- Cited art sources include Signac (1899); Chevreul and Rood were not found in the portion read → optical-mixing
- Main finding: polychromatic colour is seen "by area rather than by edge"; distinctiveness of a transition depends mainly on the colours of the adjoining regions, hardly on edge sharpness (hard vs soft edges differed little) (Abstract; Discussion; Conclusions) → optical-mixing
- Observers compare regions (macchie) more than transitions, so Pointillism works through colour regions unless edges are treated specially, e.g. Signac's Cornsweet-style edge (Discussion, Fig. 25) → optical-mixing
- Stimulus: random Voronoi dots of cardinal hues (Y,G,C,B,M,R) in two 9°x9° squares at 57 cm viewing distance; the authors did not test varying viewing distance (Methods, Figs. 3-7) → optical-mixing

## Additive / averaging
- All three tested models (CIE luminance, CIEDE2000, RGB-cube distance) use patch averages. Equal-average patches then predict invisible transitions, but dithered-texture counterexamples (Fig. 22) show this is incomplete: dot statistics matter, not only the mean → additive-mixing
- Euclidean distance in the uncorrected RGB cube best predicts perceived distinctiveness of transitions; luminance is the worst predictor, CIEDE2000 in between (Results; Discussion, Figs. 19-23; Conclusions) → additive-mixing
- The authors describe cyan as green plus blue etc. for the cardinal colours, and call the perceptual blending of varicoloured dots an assimilation effect, not a physical mixture (Methods, colour) → additive-mixing
- ⚠️ The paper does not claim optical mixture is strictly additive; it says standard colorimetric averaging is insufficient (Abstract; Fig. 22) → additive-mixing

## Assimilation
- Assimilation is described as the generalising capacity of visual awareness that blends varicoloured textures into a single impression (Methods/Discussion) → assimilation

## Equiluminance / luminance vs chromatic edges
- Pointillist edges are defined mainly by chromatic differences, not luminance contrast; chromatic contrast can match luminance contrast in making regions distinct (Abstract; Introduction scope) → equiluminance
- Observers matched the Michelson contrast of a grey pair to equal distinctiveness with a chromatic pair; settings mostly 36-55% (Methods, Results, Fig. 10) → equiluminance
- Cardinal colours differ about fivefold in luminance (Y about 100 vs B about 20); an equiluminant display would need to attenuate yellow about fivefold; luminance models predict equiluminant transitions invisible, yet they are seen (Discussion) → equiluminance
- Monochrome renderings of chromatic paintings (Fig. 1) are compromises, not faithful (Introduction; Conclusions) → equiluminance
