## Aim and limits of accelerated testing (lightfastness)
- Accelerated-aging tests serve three aims: rank materials quickly, predict long-term service, and clarify the degradation mechanism; the author lists them in order of increasing difficulty (p. xvi) → lightfastness
- Fundamentally, accelerated tests only rank materials relative to each other under the conditions used; any prediction of years of service is an educated guess (p. 8) → lightfastness
- "Lifetime prediction is in its infancy"; the book states it is too early for firm test recommendations, perhaps possible in future for artists' pigments, dyed textiles and paper (pp. 1-2) → lightfastness
- Stability (chemical resistance to light, heat, oxygen, moisture) is distinguished from durability (physical resistance to stress); chemical changes are held to underlie physical failure (p. 1) → lightfastness
- Correlation between natural and artificial aging tends to be worse the greater the acceleration; suggested acceptable speed-ups are about 5-10x, and UV not above about 3x the solar level (Brown 1991; Kockott 1989; Minsker 1982, quoted p. 97) → lightfastness
- No fixed acceleration factor can be assigned to a device: it depends on the material, the light source, temperature and humidity, and on inhibitor systems (p. 98) → lightfastness
- Cited speed-ups: a xenon-arc Fade-ometer at sunlight intensity is about 5x Florida and 7.5x central Europe; mirror-concentrated sunlight (EMMA) about 8-10x; summary says 7-9x over normal outdoor exposure (pp. 97, 114) → lightfastness
- Results of exposure tests are hard to compare unless the severity is known: one study of fugitive pigments used only about 0.8 million lux hours, another (alizarin) 350 h of xenon arc, at least 20 times more (pp. 5-6) → lightfastness
- Very stable and very fugitive materials cannot be tested with one standard procedure: fugitive pigments and dyes can fade in a few weeks under fluorescent lamps, while stable coatings would need a high-power xenon arc and hotter, wetter conditions (p. 5) → lightfastness
- Small ranking differences between very stable samples may be unimportant in practice, and ranking at harsh conditions may not hold at normal conditions; the example of an acrylic that cross-linked in the hot Fade-ometer (above 60 C) but not under cool fluorescent lamps (28 C) shows this (pp. 8-9) → lightfastness
- Logic of severity: a material that survives very severe conditions better than a proven control will probably also survive milder ones; failing a severe test does not prove failure under mild conditions (p. 100) → lightfastness
- Run several severity levels at once (e.g. Fade-ometer or outdoor rack, plus ordinary or UVA fluorescent lamps) with controls, to sort materials as fugitive, excellent or intermediate (p. 100) → lightfastness
- Test samples vary a lot in time to failure (sometimes by a decade or more), so an average time to failure is of little value; a probabilistic statement (a fraction of samples survives to a given time) is preferred (p. 9) → lightfastness

## Classes of stability and what "permanent" means
- Feller (1975) proposed three classes of photochemical stability for conservation materials: Class C unstable (serious degradation in under 20 years of normal museum use), Class B intermediate (20-100 years), Class A excellent (at least 100 years); plus Class T for temporary-contact materials (under about 6 months) (pp. 6, 11) → lightfastness
- The classes were defined objectively against the fading of the ISO R105 blue-wool standards: roughly blue wool 3 or less = Class C, between 3 and 6 = Class B, greater than 6 = Class A (p. 6; Table 1.1, p. 7, labelled there as BS 1006 class) → lightfastness
- ⚠️ Table 1.1 (p. 7) labels the reference scale "BS1006" while the text (p. 6) says "ISO R105"; the book treats them as the same blue-wool scale without explaining, so quote the numbers (3 and 6) with care → lightfastness
- The text suggests a possible Class A1 (over 500 years) and A2 (over 100 years), shown with question marks in Table 1.1; no firm definition (p. 7) → lightfastness
- Reference exposure figures (Thomson 1967): average annual exposure in the National Gallery, London, about 1.5 million lux hours (Mlxh); 100 years on an average gallery wall is about 155 Mlxh; a fugitive colorant that fades considerably in 20 years would fade seriously at about 30 Mlxh (p. 6) → lightfastness
- Same source: gallery walls receive about 1.5% of outdoor daylight (stated as 1.55% on p. 98); outdoor annual exposure on a horizontal surface in Rome, New York and Madrid is about 160 Mlxh (pp. 7, 98) → lightfastness
- A coating that lasts 10 years outdoors in Washington D.C. receives about 1,600 Mlxh, about 1,000 times a gallery wall's annual dose, without counting heat and moisture (pp. 6-7) → lightfastness
- Class C is easy to identify in short tests and Class A with harsh tests; the real difficulty lies with Class B (pp. 7, 10) → lightfastness
- The book says there has been little formal discussion of what "permanent" or acceptable lifetime/decline should mean for conservation materials; the question is left open (p. 11) → lightfastness

## Blue wool and other reference materials
- The ISO R105 blue-wool fading standards, Paraloid B-72 and butyl methacrylate polymers were suggested as material standards for photochemical tests (Feller 1978, cited p. 42) → lightfastness
- Alizarin lake mixed with titanium white often shows lightfastness equal to ISO blue wools 4 to 5 and served as a handy control, but it counts only as a secondary standard because it varies with the manufacturer (Levison 1976, cited p. 42) → lightfastness
- Controls should include very poor, intermediate and very stable materials; well-characterized materials act as primary standards and less well defined ones as secondary controls (p. 42) → lightfastness
- Other exposure monitors named: AATCC standard fading cloth XRF-1 (xenon and carbon arc), Rhodamine B dyed filter paper (Tokyo), a UV dosimeter, and yellowing of polystyrene reference white (mostly UV-sensitive) (pp. 42-43) → lightfastness
- Light test for UV in a lamp: expose samples with and without a Plexiglas UF-4 UV filter; Prussian blue and alizarin lake in dilute mixture with titanium white are suitable controls because UV influences their fading (p. 113) → lightfastness
- Blue-wool cloth No. 7 is cited as a case where the fading is not driven by the dye's own absorption (McLaren 1956) (p. 63); and slight differences in fading of AATCC and ISO blue wools occur under different filter systems, so the filter system used must always be reported (p. 92) → lightfastness
- If blue wool standards are used to monitor museum exposures, the wool substrate itself yellows or bleaches differently with wavelength (Appendix A, p. 176) → lightfastness

## Mechanism: what fading is, light dose, reciprocity
- Only radiation that is absorbed can cause a photochemical change (Grotthus-Draper law); but the absorbing species may not be the visible colorant: a trace impurity or another component can absorb and pass energy on (photosensitized fading), so the colorant's absorption curve may not show which wavelengths damage it (p. 45; also p. 63) → lightfastness
- Near-UV and visible light rarely break bonds directly (photolysis); more often it excites molecules and starts oxidation chains, with oxygen and water involved in secondary steps. Light mainly speeds the initiation, so it often shortens the induction period rather than the later rate (pp. 47, 51, 55-56) → lightfastness
- Quantum yield of fading is low: about 0.002 for bleaching of certain dyes (two molecules per thousand photons absorbed) and 10^-4 to 10^-5 for plastics and drying-oil vehicles (p. 50) → lightfastness
- Photochemical damage is irreversible: faded colorant fragments diffuse away and cannot be reassembled, nor can broken-down linseed oil be rebuilt (p. 53) → lightfastness
- Reciprocity principle: net damage is proportional to intensity x time, so 100 lux for 1 hour equals 1 lux for 100 hours (100 lux hours); it is a principle, not a strict law (p. 53) → lightfastness
- Apparent reciprocity failures are often practical: strong light heats samples and dries them, and fading depends on moisture; also some reactions depend on the square root of intensity (e.g. radicals on titanium white surfaces causing chalking) (pp. 53-54) → lightfastness
- Alternating light and dark gives a different result from continuous exposure at the same dose; Levison found alizarin lake and other artists' oil paints faded more per footcandle hour with light/dark cycles (his routine: 8 h on, 4 h off) because samples regain moisture in the dark (p. 111-112; p. 53) → lightfastness
- There is no threshold of intensity below which photochemical damage stops; low light only slows it. Museum exposure limits in lux hours are not damage-free but tolerable damage, e.g. Krochmann's limit is a just perceptible change of delta E = 1 (pp. 50, 76-77) → lightfastness
- Wavelength matters: shorter wavelengths carry more energy, and for many materials the damage rises roughly exponentially as wavelength falls (NBS/Harrison damage factors; Krochmann's slopes of about 0.010-0.0125 per nm for watercolors, oil paints, papers and textiles, based on delta E* = 1) (pp. 73-76) → lightfastness
- Sunlight reaching the earth has little below about 290 nm, and through window glass little below 315 nm; for museum-related tests, wavelengths under about 310-315 nm and especially 254 nm should be avoided because they trigger different chemistry (pp. 78-82, 91, 114) → lightfastness
- Fluorescent lamps emit about 2-6% UV (xenon arc with Pyrex about 6% of total irradiance), and little IR, so samples stay near 25-28 C; xenon arc filtered like sunlight is the most widely accepted high-intensity source (pp. 91-94) → lightfastness
- Xenon-arc intensity falls with lamp age and filters solarize; modern instruments therefore regulate exposure by radiant dose at 340 or 420 nm rather than by clock hours (p. 104) → lightfastness
- Heat effects: use covered controls next to exposed samples to separate heat from light; temperature has little effect on the primary photochemical step but the secondary dark reactions depend on heat and moisture (pp. 54-55, 108) → lightfastness
- Higher humidity generally speeds fading: six fugitive watercolor colorants in gum arabic faded 3.1x faster between 25 and 65% RH at 20 C; a humidity index for carmine lake, gamboge, alizarin lake and Vandyke brown in PVAc was 11, 0, 22 and 11% per 10% RH (pp. 123-124) → lightfastness
- Fading rate for dyes/pigments depends on seven factors: dye photochemistry, physical state, substrate chemistry, substrate structure, foreign substances, atmosphere, illumination (Giles and McKay; Appendix B, p. 177) → lightfastness
- Fading can occur by oxidation or reduction; the observed lightfastness of a colorant depends strongly on the substrate or vehicle, their chemical affinity and the dispersion/aggregation of the colorant, so rankings may change with the medium (pp. 178, 185) → lightfastness, paint-composition
- Some color changes are reversible in the dark or on removal from exposure (e.g. Prussian blue fading can be reversible), so measurement timing matters (pp. 105, 183) → lightfastness

## Binder and other components (paint-composition)
- Titanium white under UV generates free radicals in the vehicle, leading to binder destruction and chalking; photoactivity of TiO2 is also needed to be monitored because it requires some moisture (pp. 15, 54, 124) → paint-composition
- Titanium white and zinc white pigments, and cadmium sulfide, are photosensitive and can produce hydrogen peroxide that attacks neighbouring materials; undyed fibers placed next to such samples can be degraded (Appendix B, p. 180) → paint-composition
- A dye or pigment can sensitize the breakdown of its binder or substrate (photo-tendering), by chain breaking or cross-linking (Appendix B, p. 180) → paint-composition
- A source rich in very short UV (about 290 nm) exaggerates binder degradation and hides the pigment's photoactivity; results for TiO2 in an alkyd differed from those in a more durable thermoset acrylic (Blakey 1985, cited p. 80) → paint-composition
- Lead white plus UV-activated titanium white: Ti(III) reduces lead ions to metallic lead, darkening a paste of the two pigments; the darkening rate in glycerine was used as an indirect test (p. 15) → paint-composition
- Fading of the same colorant depends on vehicle: gamboge in poly(vinyl acetate) was insensitive to RH (p. 121); the artists' pigment tests used PVAc and acrylic emulsion vehicles (pp. 59-60, 124) → paint-composition
- Film systems: practical materials are mixtures (vehicle-pigment-volatile solvent), where several components age and opposing reactions (yellowing/bleaching, cross-linking/chain breaking) may run at once (p. 14) → paint-composition
- Lightfastness of dyes tends to rise with their concentration in the substrate, and large particles fade more slowly than finely dispersed ones; the rate of fading varies with 1/a^2 (radius a) for large particles, tending to 1/a for smaller ones (pp. 181-182) → paint-composition, tinting-strength

## Depth of fading, hiding power, tints
- Photochemical damage in a paint film is mainly a surface phenomenon; light is absorbed with depth (Beer-Lambert) and shorter wavelengths penetrate less (e.g. 320 nm penetrates 1/10 as deep as 350 nm in one polyester) (p. 56) → lightfastness
- In alizarin lake/titanium white paints (xenon arc), the faded zone is only about 4 to 40 micrometers deep and gets shallower as the alizarin content rises (Johnston-Feller 1986; Fig. 5.4, p. 59) → lightfastness, transparency-opacity
- The faded depth also increases as the amount of scattering white (TiO2 pigment volume concentration) decreases: light goes deeper in a paint at 3.5% PVC than at 7 or 14% (Table 5.1, pp. 59-60) → transparency-opacity
- In paler tints (low colorant concentration) the boundary between faded and unfaded paint is diffuse; with high colorant concentration it is sharp, because light is attenuated faster in the more strongly colored film (pp. 59-60) → tinting-strength
- Pigmented coatings gave almost complete protection to the underlying coating from UV in Renz et al.'s tests (p. 65) → paint-composition
- Because fading is usually limited to the upper layer, a faded paint may keep unfaded material below; cross-sections can show whether fading occurred and may reveal the original colorant (p. 61) → lightfastness
- Deterioration depth also depends on oxygen diffusion, so thick vs thin films behave differently (pp. 61, 105) → lightfastness
- Dilute mixtures with titanium white (e.g. Prussian blue, alizarin lake) are used as sensitive UV-dependent fading controls (p. 113) → tinting-strength

## Not covered / caveats
- Not covered: blue-wool grades 1-8 individual lightfastness values, ISO 105-B02 procedure details, lux-hour doses per blue wool grade, ASTM I-III pigment ratings. The book names blue wools only in relation to classes and controls.
- Not covered: explicit definitions of transparency/opacity, tinting strength or hiding power; only the indirect findings above on depth of fading versus TiO2 PVC and colorant concentration. The "tint" claims come from alizarin/TiO2 films, not from general oil paint.
- Concept coverage requested: lightfastness (covered), paint-composition (partly), transparency-opacity (indirectly, 2 bullets), tinting-strength (indirectly).
- Source level: specialist (conservation science monograph, 1994). Standards cited (ISO R105, BS 1006, AATCC) are from that period and have been revised since.
