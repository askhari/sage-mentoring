## photoreceptors
- Rods are slim, rod-shaped, fill the spaces between cones; cones are conical with cell bodies in one row below the outer limiting membrane (A, "Light microscopy and ultrastructure of rods and cones"). → photoreceptors
- Peripheral-retina inner segment diameter: rods about 2 µm, cones about 6 µm; central foveal cones about 1.5 µm, thinner than the average rod (A, same section). → photoreceptors
- Rod discs detach and float free in the outer segment; cone discs stay attached to the membrane (A, "Outer segment generation"). → photoreceptors
- Visual pigment = opsin plus 11-cis retinal in the outer-segment discs; light closes cGMP-gated channels, hyperpolarizing the cell and reducing glutamate release (A, "Visual pigments and visual transduction"). → photoreceptors
- Cones adapt over about 7-9 log units of intensity, rods over about 2 log units (about 5 with network adaptation) (A, same section). → photoreceptors
- Peak sensitivities: rods about 500 nm (dim/night vision); L-cones about 560 nm; M-cones about 530 nm; S-cones about 420 nm (other studies 430 and 445 nm) (A, "Different types of cone photoreceptor"). ⚠️ Chapter C gives different values corrected for pre-retinal light loss: S about 440, M 545, L 565 nm (C, "Trichromacy"/Theories of Colour Vision); chapter B's Figure 22 caption mentions 419, 509, 610 nm. Peaks differ by method; state which. → photoreceptors
- Primates are trichromatic, most other mammals dichromatic (A, same section). → photoreceptors
- Distribution: cone density highest in the foveal pit, drops quickly outside, then roughly even in periphery; rods peak in a ring about 4.5 mm (about 18°) from the fovea (A, "Densities of rods and cones in the human retina"). → photoreceptors
- S-cones: 3-5% of cones in the foveal pit, up to 15% on the foveal slope (about 1° away), about 8% elsewhere. Human L:M ratio varies widely between people, from about equal to as much as 16:1 (A, same section). Chapter B (§9, "The Cone Mosaic of Trivariant Color Vision") also reports large L:M variability between subjects and a constant S-to-L/M ratio; ⚠️ its text and figure-caption ratios are noted as not fully consistent. → photoreceptors
- Rods mediate night vision, respond to single photons, and their signals arrive up to about 0.1 s later than cone signals under mixed lighting (A, "Rods and Night Vision"). → photoreceptors
- Rods: very sensitive but slow, saturate at light levels where cones work well; cones: less sensitive, fast, almost impossible to saturate (B, §2 "The Photoreceptors"). → photoreceptors
- The Photoreceptors chapter does not use the words "scotopic"/"photopic" (A); the Light and Dark Adaptation chapter was not read. Scotopic/photopic definitions are not backed by this note. → photoreceptors

## trichromacy
- Colour vision starts with three cone classes S, M, L; described as "trivariant or trichromatic" (C, Theories of Colour Vision). → trichromacy
- Three primaries can match almost any colour and matching is linear: a test colour equals a weighted sum of three primaries, weights = tristimulus values (C, "Colour Matches"). Grassmann's laws describe the matching data under fixed brightness, adaptation and field size (C, same). → trichromacy
- Matching is affected by macular pigment (central 4-5°), chromatic aberration, rod intrusion, failure of Abney's law (Helmholtz-Kohlrausch effect) and the Bezold-Brücke hue shift (C, "Colour Matches"). → trichromacy
- Young proposed three principal receptors in 1802; Helmholtz developed it (1866 in C; 1852 in B, §18 "Hering's Theory"). ⚠️ date discrepancy between chapters. Maxwell (1872) supported it (B, §18). → trichromacy
- Evidence for it: Rushton 1963 densitometry, Marks et al. 1964 microspectrophotometry (three pigments), Nathans et al. 1986 genetics (C, Young-Helmholtz). The theory alone cannot explain the four unique hues or why dichromats see white and yellow (C, same). → trichromacy
- Univariance: a cone responds only to the energy it absorbs (attributed to Maxwell 1872); equal absorbed energy gives equal response at any wavelength, so a single cone type is colour blind (B, §2 "Univariance; Divariance"). Therefore at least two cone types ("divariant": L and S) are required to compare wavelengths; three give trivariant colour vision (B, §2, §8). Chapter C does not discuss univariance. → trichromacy
- Evolution claim: the L opsin gene duplicated, giving M and a longer-wavelength L; the new L pigment split the yellow region into red and green percepts (B, §8 "Trivariant Color Vision"). → trichromacy
- Under ideal conditions at least a million colours can be perceived (B, §16). → trichromacy
- Chromatic contrast needs comparison of different cone types over an area; achromatic borders can be resolved with about two neighbouring cones (B, §3). → trichromacy

## opponent-process
- Hering (1872 in C; 1874 in B §18) proposed opponent pairs: C lists three channels red-green, blue-yellow, black-white; B mentions blue-yellow and red-green pairs. His idea of opponency at the photoreceptor level was incorrect (B, §18). ⚠️ date discrepancy. → opponent-process
- Hurvich and Jameson (1957) gave quantitative support with hue cancellation (C, Opponent Process). → opponent-process
- Neural evidence: Svaetichin 1956 (fish retinal horizontal cells), De Valois et al. in the LGN (1966 in C; 1958 in B §18 ⚠️), Gouras 1968 in primate ganglion cells (C). Gothlin (1944) first suggested opponency arises from neural excitation/inhibition; Granit 1947 dominator/modulator theory (B, §18). → opponent-process
- Modern "stage" model: three photopigments at the receptor stage, then opponent processing starting as early as the horizontal-cell level (C, Opponent Process). Retinal "colour-opponent" cells are now explained as cone-opponent (B, §18). → opponent-process
- Circuitry: horizontal cells give antagonistic feedback to cones (H1 contacts L only; H2 contacts S and L) (B, §4). L-cone midget bipolar/ganglion cells and bistratified S-cone-ON ganglion cells are the main colour channels (red-green and blue-yellow respectively) (B, §5-6). S cones connect only to ON bipolar cells (B, §5). → opponent-process
- Parasol (magnocellular) ganglion cells: achromatic, similar L and M input, no S input (B, §10). LGN: parvocellular layers (midget cells), koniocellular layers (S-cone blue-yellow), magnocellular layers (achromatic) (B, §11). → opponent-process
- Cortex: double-opponent cells (found by Michael in monkey striate cortex) use spectral contrast for borders and are linked to colour constancy (Land's model) (B, §7, §13-14). Colour-selective blobs in striate cortex hypercolumns (B, §12). → opponent-process
- Short wavelengths can look reddish, attributed to the beta band of the L-cone opsin (B, §17). → opponent-process

## color-vision-deficiency
- Classes: monochromasy, dichromasy, anomalous trichromasy (C, "Colour Vision Deficiencies", Table 1). → color-vision-deficiency
- Prevalence, males / females (C, Table 1): overall about 8% / 0.5%; protanomaly 1% / 0.01%; deuteranomaly 5% / 0.4%; protanopia 1% / 0.01%; deuteranopia 1.5% / 0.01%; tritanopia 0.008% / 0.008%; tritanomaly, rod and cone monochromasy rare; atypical monochromasy very rare. → color-vision-deficiency
- Genetics: red-green deficiencies and blue cone monochromacy are X-linked recessive (genes OPN1LW, OPN1MW); males need one altered copy, hence far more affected. Blue-yellow deficiencies are autosomal dominant (OPN1SW), one altered copy suffices. Congenital tritanopia was once thought not to exist; family studies and molecular genetics confirmed it (C, "Genetics"). → color-vision-deficiency
- Chapter B (Gouras) has no protan/deutan/tritan classification, prevalence or genetics (checked). → color-vision-deficiency

## light-spectrum
- UV is absorbed by the anterior eye; infrared photons may carry too little energy to activate opsins (B, §2). Mice and rats have UV-sensitive cone opsins (B, §2). Photopigment peaks: see photoreceptors above. No visible-range numbers (e.g. 380-700 nm) were found in the chapters read. → light-spectrum

## Color Perception chapter (Kalloniatis & Luu)
Re-checked chapter C (and B for constancy/contrast) in a second pass. Chapter C turns out to cover almost none of the requested perceptual-phenomena concepts; the usable material comes from chapter B (Gouras), cited by section/figure. Via the same automatic summarizer; re-check before quoting.

- Chapter C mentions colour opponency "as revealed by coloured after images" only as part of what Hering's theory was meant to explain (C, "Opponent Colour Theory"); it gives no discussion of successive contrast or adaptation mechanisms. → afterimages
- Afterimage example: after fixating a black spot on a blue/white figure and then looking at a neutral scene, a yellow afterimage appears where the object was (B, §18, Fig. 38, as summarized). → afterimages
- Gouras uses the afterimage to argue that S-cone ganglion-cell activity signals that S cones absorbed light, not that the light is "blue" (it could be white or gray) (B, §18). → afterimages
- Gouras says Hering's opponency theory was right in its analysis but wrong in placing opponency in the photoreceptors (B, §18). → afterimages
- Adaptation is shown with coloured adapting fields: red and blue adapting fields (B, §8, Fig. 17), yellow adapting light (B, §8, Fig. 18), blue adapting light (B, §17); "transient tritanopia" is mentioned (B, §6). The summaries give no explicit afterimage-by-adaptation explanation. → afterimages
- Problem of constancy: colours look stable under very different illuminants (e.g. fluorescent vs tungsten), while identical surface reflectances can look different depending on the whole scene (B, §7 "Color Constancy and Double Opponency"). → color-constancy
- Land's model: cone signals are normalized across the entire scene before being compared locally to give object colour (B, §7). "Retinex" appears only in the reference list (B). → color-constancy
- Shadow demonstration: a shadow cast on a white-plus-yellow screen looks blue, although raw absorption predicts yellow; normalizing the screen to 100% raises the relative S-cone effect (B, §7, Fig. 11). → color-constancy
- Proposed mechanism: mutual inhibition between S- and L-cone channels could implement the normalization (B, §7, Fig. 12). → color-constancy
- Double-opponent cells (found by C. Michael in monkey striate cortex, after Daw's goldfish work) separate spectral contrast from energy contrast and are linked to constancy (B, §7, §13-14). → color-constancy
- If surround regions enhance local spectral contrast, the result is simultaneous colour contrast; the idea is stated briefly, not as a separate topic (B, §7, Fig. 12). → simultaneous-contrast
- Double-opponent logic: a region looks blue with short wavelengths and no long wavelengths, yellow for the reverse; activation of the surround by the opposite colour strengthens this (B, §7). → simultaneous-contrast
- Cortical cell responding to spectral contrast but not energy contrast: no response to red or green bars on black, large response to a green bar on a red background where luminance contrast is minimal (B, §13, Fig. 26). → equiluminance
- Orientation- and direction-selective complex cells respond strongly to red/green spectral contrast without energy contrast (B, §14, Fig. 28); simple cells likewise give strong, out-of-phase responses to red-green and green-red bars (B, §14, Fig. 27). → equiluminance
- Double-opponent simple/complex/hypercomplex cells parallel the energy-based cells; both likely contribute to form vision, and energy-based processing gets more cortical area because of finer spatial resolution (B, §14, Fig. 29). → equiluminance
- Parasol cells (achromatic, motion) are discussed separately (B, §5, §10); the chapter does not test isoluminant motion or depth. → equiluminance
- In C, "isoluminant" appears only as a design property: the colours of the Panel D-15 test are chosen from the CIE diagram to be isoluminant (C, "Diagnostic Colour Vision Tests", Fig. 23). → equiluminance
- C lists the Helmholtz-Kohlrausch effect and failure of Abney's law as luminance-additivity problems in colour matching, without explaining them (C, "Colour Matches"). → equiluminance
- C says trichromatic theory fails to account for the four unique colours (red, green, yellow, blue), and that complementary wavelengths cancel for all wavelengths except the four unique hues (C, Theories of Colour Vision; Fig. 15, Hurvich and Jameson data). Not a treatment of naming or categories. → color-categories
