## paint-composition
- Pigments are the particles that give paint its hue; they are classed as organic (synthetic, carbon-based) or inorganic (from minerals/ores) (B, "Pigment vs. Binder"). → paint-composition
- Acrylic binder is an emulsion of acrylic polymer particles in water, with a surfactant helping water and polymer combine; as water evaporates the particles fuse into a continuous film (B, "Pigment vs. Binder"). → paint-composition
- Each pigment's own properties influence lightfastness, grind and drying time (A, "Pigment"/"Dry Time"). → paint-composition
- Organic pigments: high chroma, strong tinting power, high transparency; inorganic: lower chroma and tinting power, moderate to high opacity, Zinc White being an exception (B, "Transparency and Opacity"). → paint-composition
- Acrylic's clear film can make transparent colours such as Phthalo Blue dry noticeably darker; test a small swatch first (B). → paint-composition
- Golden says pigment load is about the same across nearly all its lines: as high as possible without weakening the paint film (C). Golden's own products. → paint-composition
- Williamsburg oils are milled in small batches, so grind varies on purpose from smooth/glossy to textured (A, "Grind"). Golden/Williamsburg products. → paint-composition
- Oil and acrylic are tested with different primers on the aluminium panels, and watercolour on filter paper, because the media differ (JP1, "Sample Preparation"). → paint-composition

## colour-index-names
- The C.I. Name and Number are internationally recognised codes for a colorant, issued by the Society of Dyers and Colourists and AATCC (A, "Color Index Name"). → colour-index-names
- The name combines type (pigment/dye), general hue and a serial number; example PB 60 = Anthraquinone Blue (A; also D, "Pigment Codes"). → colour-index-names
- The C.I. Number is a five-digit reference based on chemical structure, independent of use (D, "Pigment Codes"). → colour-index-names
- Golden's technical charts list the C.I. names of the pigments in each colour, plus an organic/inorganic/mixed classification (D, column definitions). → colour-index-names
- Chart abbreviations: C.P. = concentrated cadmium pigment, G.S./B.S./R.S./Y.S. = green/blue/red/yellow shade (D). Golden-specific. → colour-index-names
- Reader comment in JP1 cites a pigment by its CI code (PY73) when questioning its ASTM I rating; shows buyers use CI codes to look up a rating (JP1, comments). → colour-index-names
- The Color Mixing Guide itself names colours by trade names, not C.I. codes (B). → colour-index-names

## transparency-opacity
- No industry standard exists for measuring opacity/transparency; ratings are relative, comparing similarly prepared samples (A, "Opacity / Transparency"). → transparency-opacity
- Transparent pigments can look opaque at full strength from the tube (e.g. Phthalo Blue); thin application, gels or mediums reveal the transparency (A). → transparency-opacity
- Golden's charts use an 8-point scale (1 most opaque, 8 most transparent) with labels opaque / semi-opaque / semi-transparent / transparent (D). Golden-specific. → transparency-opacity
- Organic pigments transmit light almost undisturbed (transparent); adding Titanium White raises coverage, while Zinc White adds little opacity and suits tinted glazes (B). → transparency-opacity
- Small particle size goes with excellent transparency, e.g. quinacridones, hansa and phthalo colours (C). → transparency-opacity
- Golden's test samples are drawdowns (films of fixed thickness) on panels or cards: 10 mil for acrylics, 6 mil for oils (JP1, "Sample Preparation"). → transparency-opacity

## tinting-strength
- Tint strength = how much a colour changes the character of another; Golden tests each colour mixed with an equal amount of Titanium White; weak tinters give light pastels, strong tinters darker mixes (A, "Tint Strength"). → tinting-strength
- Golden's tints on its charts are 1 part colour to 10 parts Titanium White (C, "Strong and Weak Colors"). ⚠️ conflicts with A's "same amount"; the two pages describe the test differently. → tinting-strength
- Very light/weak tints usually indicate larger pigment particles (exceptions exist); dark tints usually indicate very small particles (C). → tinting-strength
- Phthalo Blue and Phthalo Green are very strong, so use them in much smaller amounts than a yellow when mixing (B, mixture ratios). → tinting-strength

## masstone-undertone
- Masstone: the colour of paint applied thickly enough to hide what lies beneath; thick Phthalo Blue looks near black (B, "Masstone"). → masstone-undertone
- Undertone: what shows when paint is spread or scraped very thinly over white, or thinned with medium/water (B, "Undertone"). → masstone-undertone
- Transparent organics such as quinacridones and phthalos can have an undertone noticeably different from masstone (B). → masstone-undertone
- ASTM lightfastness ratings are always based on the tint, not the masstone, because pigments are generally more vulnerable in a tint (JP1, "Sample Preparation"). → masstone-undertone
- Golden adds masstones to its own tests for a new pigment, supplier or formulation change; in the outdoor example the Pyrrole Red masstone held while the tint lightened, and Hansa Yellow Medium darkened in masstone but bleached in tint (JP1, "Number of Samples" and photo caption). → masstone-undertone

## lightfastness
- JP1 gives the ΔE bands for the first three ASTM categories: ΔE 1-4 = I "Excellent", 4-8 = II "Good", 8-16 = III "Fair"; one ΔE is in theory the smallest change a normal observer can distinguish (JP1, "Evaluating the Results"). The article does not list IV and V. → lightfastness
- Golden's sunburst label rating comes from ASTM D4303 testing: films exposed to light and colour change measured; I best, II strong, III-V progressively more fading (A, "Lightfastness"). → lightfastness
- Some labelled ratings come from pigment-maker data or tests not exactly following ASTM (A); D shows "N/A" in such cases. Acrylic chart ratings cite ASTM D 5098 (D, "Lightfastness"). → lightfastness
- Tint level: oils/acrylics are mixed with enough white (watercolour with enough water) to reach 35-45% reflectance at the wavelength of maximum absorption (JP1, "Sample Preparation"). → lightfastness
- ASTM requires 7 identical tints per colour: 1 unexposed control, 3 outdoor, 3 accelerated indoor (JP1, "Number of Samples"). → lightfastness
- Golden adds extras beyond ASTM: masstones for new pigments/formulations, paler 20:1 tints, and areas coated with UV-filtering varnish; only the original tints count for the ASTM rating (JP1, "Number of Samples"). Golden-specific. → lightfastness
- Outdoor exposure: under glass at 45 degrees in South Florida or Arizona, October-March, until 1260 MJ/m2 total solar irradiance (about 3 months); that dose was chosen by ASTM because it matched fading of pigments like Alizarin Crimson and Rose Madder in century-old paintings (JP1, "Outdoor and Accelerated Indoor Xenon Light Exposure"). → lightfastness
- Accelerated indoor: xenon arc chamber simulating sunlight through window glass, same UV dose in 410 hours (JP1, same section). → lightfastness
- Colour is read by spectrophotometer in three places before and after, averaged, and the change is reported as ΔE (JP1, "Sample Preparation"/"Evaluating the Results"); CIE Lab is the colour space required (JP1, comment reply). → lightfastness
- Accelerated results are not meant to be predictive of a specific result after a specific number of years; they only rank colorants (JP1, comment reply by the author). → lightfastness
- Formulation-specific testing: ASTM lets a manufacturer assign ratings from a lookup table of pigments tested decades ago (mostly 30+ years, often one pigment source or brand); Golden argues this is inadequate because pigment quality varies, and tests its own products regularly (JP1, "Going Beyond What Is Required"). → lightfastness
- Other Golden tests: unglazed outdoor exposure in South Florida read at 6/12/24/36 months, and QUV chamber (UV-A fluorescent bulbs) in 400 h cycles, 3 cycles roughly matching outdoor/xenon (JP1, "Other Lightfastness Testing"). → lightfastness
- A reader comment (not Golden's article) says home testing with a blue wool card helps estimate exposure; Golden's own suggestion for home tests is a south-facing window sample vs a drawer control, compared at 3, 6 and 12 months (JP1, comments). Blue wool is not part of the article body. → lightfastness
- Golden's reply says fluorescents fade fast, especially in thin glazes, and that UV is the most damaging factor (JP1, comments). → lightfastness
- Effect of white/tints on fading: JP1 states only that pigments are generally more vulnerable in tints (above). The detailed white-pigment study is in JP2, which I could not read. → lightfastness

## limited-palette
- Golden proposes an 8-colour acrylic palette: Benzimidazolone Yellow Medium, Quinacridone Magenta, Phthalo Blue (GS), Phthalo Green (BS), Naphthol Red Light, Titanium White, Zinc White, Yellow Ochre (B, "Limited Palette"). Golden products. → limited-palette
- Mixing primaries are the yellow, magenta and phthalo blue; black is omitted because Phthalo Green + Naphthol Red Light gives a near-black (B). → limited-palette
- Example mixtures: turquoise = equal Phthalo Blue (GS) + Phthalo Green (BS); Light Red = 1 Quinacridone Magenta : 5 Benzimidazolone Yellow Medium; Permanent Green Light = 1 Phthalo Blue (GS) : 10 yellow (B). → limited-palette
