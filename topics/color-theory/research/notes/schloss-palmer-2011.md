## Definitions and framing
- The authors separate three judgments about colour pairs: pair preference (liking the pair as a whole), pair harmony (how much the colours "go together", independent of liking) and figural preference (liking the figure colour seen against a coloured ground). Confusing them explains much contradiction in the literature (Introduction) → harmony-research
- They argue by music analogy: Mozart is more consonant than Stravinsky, yet people differ in which they prefer; likewise harmony is not preference (Introduction; instructions in Exp. 2) → harmony-research
- Classic theories (Chevreul, Itten, Munsell, Ostwald, etc.) disagree so much that, pooled, almost any pair would count as harmonious; Chevreul used "preference" and "harmony" as equivalents and claimed harmonies of both analogous and contrasting colours (Introduction) → harmony-research
- Earlier empirical work is also inconsistent: Granger (1955) found "harmony" rising with hue difference, Allen & Guilford (1936) found no clear effect, Ou & Luo (2006) found harmony rising with hue similarity. The authors attribute this to ambiguous tasks/definitions (Granger's task may in fact measure figural preference; Chuang & Ou's "pleasing" definition mixes harmony and preference) (Introduction; Exp. 2 intro) → harmony-research

## Method and limits (important for interpreting the results)
- Berkeley Color Project: 48 participants (24 M, 24 F), screened for colour deficiency, rated all 992 figure-ground pairs from 32 chromatic colours: 8 hues (R, O, Y, chartreuse, G, cyan, B, purple) x 4 "cuts" (saturated, light, muted, dark) (Exp. 1, Methods) → harmony-research
- Stimuli: small square centred in a larger square on a neutral grey, shown on a monitor (not paint or real objects, no meaning/context). The authors admit generalisation to other spatial layouts and to 3+ colours is only supported by preliminary data (Exp. 1 Displays; GD) → harmony-research
- ⚠️ The participants were Berkeley students; the authors suggest the blue-yellow up-tick in harmony may be a school-colours (blue and gold) artefact (Exp. 2). Population is therefore narrow, and non-Western or older samples were not tested (inferred from Methods; the source itself only raises the school-colours point) → harmony-research
- Colours were from the Munsell system (rendered on a monitor); results in the hue-step plots relate to 8 hue steps only, a coarse sampling of the wheel (Exp. 1, Methods) → harmony-research
- The authors state their data say little about the causes of preferences; the causes they give (ecological valence, co-occurrence in natural images, fluency, simultaneous contrast) are conjectures still under test (GD) → harmony-research

## Hue similarity, analogous vs contrasting hues
- Pair preference is highest when the figure and ground have the same hue (differing in lightness/saturation) and falls monotonically as the hue difference grows (Exp. 1) → color-schemes
- Pair harmony does the same: highest for same hue, falling monotonically with hue difference. This agrees with Chevreul's "harmony of analogous colours" (Exp. 2) → color-schemes
- No upturn for maximally contrasting hues: no increase in preference or harmony for complementary pairs. Held for perceptual complements (R-G, Y-B) and for paint complements (Y-purple, B-orange, R-G) (Exp. 1, Exp. 2) → color-schemes
- Paint-complement pairs were rated significantly less harmonious than the same hues paired with the neighbours of their complement. The only exception for preference was orange-blue, which was preferred over its neighbours; the only upturn in harmony was blue ground/yellow figure (Exp. 1, Exp. 2) → color-schemes
- Hue similarity is the main shared driver of preference, harmony and similarity ratings, so low-contrast-hue (analogous-like) pairs win on average. Chevreul's "harmony of contrast" holds for hue only in figural preference (see below) (GD) → color-schemes
- ⚠️ These conclusions concern two-colour averages across many people. They do not test triads or whole palettes; the authors give only preliminary, unreported results that triple preference is predicted by its pairs (GD) → color-schemes
- Pair preference also depends on which hues: cooler (bluer) pairs are liked and judged harmonious more, yellower/warmer ones less; saturated red produces particularly disliked, disharmonious pairs. Grounds with preferred hues get a larger boost from same-hue figures (Exp. 1, Exp. 2) → color-schemes

## Effect sizes (harmony vs preference)
- Average pair preference vs average harmony r = +.79 (62% shared variance). Ou et al. earlier reported r = +.85 on a binary task (Exp. 2; Introduction) → harmony-research
- Harmony agreement across observers is higher (mean r with group = .51) than preference agreement (.36); similarity is highest (.75) (Exp. 2, Exp. 3) → harmony-research
- Preferences for the two component colours explain 21.7% of pair preference variance (ground 15%, figure 6.7%) but only 1.4% of harmony variance; the authors read this as showing that harmony instructions can isolate harmony from liking (Exp. 1, Exp. 2) → harmony-research
- Munsell relational factors: best 3-factor model explains 53.5% of preference variance (cooler, similar hue, contrasting value); for harmony 67.3% (similar hue, cooler, less saturated, similar coolness) (Exp. 1, Exp. 2) → harmony-research
- Harmony ratings are the best single predictor of pair preference (62.3%); best model with harmony + ground preference + figure preference + lightness difference reaches 80.8% (Exp. 2) → harmony-research
- Beyond their shared variance, preference additionally depends on liking of ground/figure colours and large lightness difference, whereas harmony additionally depends on hue similarity (13.7%) and low total saturation (6.4%). Harmony pairs are lighter and less saturated; preferred pairs contain more dark and saturated colours (Exp. 2) → harmony-research
- Harmony and similarity are strongly correlated (r = +.83), but harmony is not mere similarity: harmony predicts preference better (r .79 vs .55) and similarity adds nothing once harmony is included (Exp. 3) → harmony-research
- Individual differences: the individual correlation between liking and harmony ranged from .75 to -.03. People with moderate formal colour training preferred harmonious pairs most; those with the most training (artists, designers) and those with none less so. The authors speculate the experts learn to go beyond the rules (⚠️ interpretation, not tested) (Exp. 2) → harmony-research
- Albers (cited) argued that dissonance can be as desirable as consonance (Introduction); the authors' data show that, for some observers, preference does not follow harmony → harmony-research

## Lightness contrast (value structure)
- More preferred pairs have contrasting Munsell value (lightness); lightness difference adds 4.5% to preference beyond harmony (Exp. 1 regression, Exp. 2) → value-structure
- Pair preference depends more on lightness contrast than harmony does, and harmony more than similarity does (GD, Exp. 3 summary) → value-structure
- Pairs that are more preferred than harmonious tend to have high lightness contrast; those more harmonious than preferred have low lightness contrast (Exp. 2, Fig. 8 description) → value-structure
- For harmony the effect of lightness contrast is small but positive (r = +.10 with lightness difference); for similarity it is negative (r = -.23). That is the main difference between harmony and similarity (Exp. 3) → value-structure
- Dark yellow/orange (browns, olives) pairs are disliked but still judged harmonious when combined with light colours of similar hue (Exp. 2) → value-structure
- Proposed reasons for the lightness-contrast preference: processing fluency (high figure-ground contrast is easier to perceive) or dislike of near-isoluminant boundaries; both untested conjectures (GD) → value-structure
- Hue contrast raised figural preference mainly when lightness contrast was minimal; the authors conclude hue contrast is preferable only when lightness contrast is low (Exp. 4, summary) → value-structure
- Light figures were preferred on dark backgrounds and dark figures on light ones (Exp. 4, Fig. 13) → value-structure

## Figure vs ground, accent
- Ground colour counts more than figure colour: ground preference explains 15% of pair-preference variance vs 6.7% for figure. The authors suggest this is because the ground covers more area (⚠️ speculation) (Exp. 1) → dominance-and-accent
- Swapping figure and ground roles has small effects: warmer figure on cooler ground and lighter figure on darker ground are slightly preferred (r = +.13 and +.14; together 4% of variance). Lighter-on-darker was also slightly more harmonious (r = +.13); the warm/cool asymmetry was not significant for harmony (Exp. 1, Exp. 2) → dominance-and-accent
- Experiment 4 (figure colour rated on 32 backgrounds, scale 1-9): figural preference increased as hue similarity to the background decreased, the opposite of pair preference, harmony and similarity (Exp. 4) → dominance-and-accent
- After removing single-colour and pair preference, warm figures were preferred on cool backgrounds and cool figures on warm backgrounds; figures were generally preferred on backgrounds of contrasting lightness; saturated figures are most preferred, saturated grounds least (Exp. 4, GD) → dominance-and-accent
- Interpretation: Chevreul's "harmony of contrast" is better understood as contrast making the figure colour itself look better, not as harmony of the pair. Suggested cause is simultaneous contrast raising the figure's apparent saturation (untested conjecture) (Exp. 4, GD) → dominance-and-accent
- ⚠️ Stimuli were equal-sized small squares (a "figure" of 100x100 px on 300x300 px ground or 8 squares on a full-screen ground), not true small accents in a large field or real paintings, so "accent" is only indirectly addressed (Exp. 1, Exp. 4 Methods) → dominance-and-accent
- Colours seen on contrasting backgrounds are generally perceived as more saturated (Lotto & Purves cited by the authors); preference for saturated colours is from their earlier paper (Exp. 4 intro) → dominance-and-accent
