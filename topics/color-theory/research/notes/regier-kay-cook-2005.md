## Background
- The long-held view: colour categories in the world's languages are organised around universal focal colours (prototypes), and speakers tend to pick these as best examples (Abstract; Introduction) → color-categories
- Berlin and Kay (1969) found that best examples of colour terms in 20 languages clustered in colour space; later work showed the most reliable clusters match the six Hering primaries: white, black, red, yellow, green, blue (Introduction) → color-categories, hue
- The relativist challenge (Roberson and coworkers, Berinmo of Papua New Guinea): categories are mainly set by cultural experience and drawn at boundaries that vary by language; best examples would only be secondary category centres (Introduction) → color-categories
- Roberson et al.'s evidence: failure to replicate Heider's privileged-focus findings in Berinmo; diffuse best examples for some Berinmo terms; differing Berinmo/English boundaries influence non-linguistic colour memory (Introduction) → color-categories
- Berinmo has five colour terms, focused at or near black, white, red, yellow and green; the green-focused term also covers blue and purple, and the yellow one includes yellowish greens (Study 1, Fig. 3) → color-categories
- The original Berlin and Kay data came largely from written languages of industrialised societies, so their regularities could have reflected the spread of industrialisation (Introduction) → color-categories

## Method: World Color Survey
- The World Color Survey collected naming data from 110 unwritten languages of non-industrialised societies, on average 24 native speakers per language (Study 1) → color-categories
- Each speaker named each of 330 Munsell chips and marked the best example (focus) of each term (Study 1, Fig. 1) → color-categories
- The array has 10 Munsell value levels by 40 equally spaced Munsell hues (R2.5 to RP10), each at about the maximum available chroma, plus an achromatic column (Fig. 1 caption) → color-categories

## Results
- Pooled best-example choices peaked at the lightest and darkest achromatic chips (white 2,048 hits, black 1,988), matching English white and black (Study 1) → color-categories
- The other best examples clustered near English red, yellow, green and blue; the authors take the peaks as universal foci (red 668, yellow 752, green 351, blue 253 hits) (Study 1, Fig. 2) → color-categories
- Berinmo best examples, apart from achromatic ones, also peak near the WCS red, yellow and green foci (Study 1, Fig. 3) → color-categories
- Measured in CIELAB, best examples cluster more tightly across languages than the centres of category extensions (paired t test, t(109) = 10.25, P < 0.0001), as predicted if foci are universal and boundaries vary (Study 2) → color-categories

## Conclusions and limits
- Named colour categories tend to be built around favoured regions of colour space in both industrialised and non-industrialised societies (Discussion) → color-categories
- Why those regions are favoured (colour appearance, surface or illuminant statistics, shape of perceptual colour space, social negotiation) cannot yet be decided; possibly several factors (Discussion) → color-categories
- The authors accept that language-specific category boundaries can affect non-linguistic colour cognition (Whorfian effects), provided the boundary variation is itself constrained by universal forces (Discussion) → color-categories
- Unsettled: how far foci vary across languages and whether they are points or irregular areas (Discussion) → color-categories
