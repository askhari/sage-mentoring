## Learner, "Modern Paints: Uncovering the Choices" (keynote)

- Main binders in modern paints: drying oil, acrylic solution (polymer dissolved in solvent, e.g. Magna), acrylic emulsion (polymer dispersed in water with surfactant and other additives), alkyd, PVA emulsion, nitrocellulose (Learner, pp. 5-10) → paint-composition
- Acrylic emulsion binder is a copolymer of methyl methacrylate with ethyl acrylate or n-butyl acrylate; since the late 1980s most artists' brands use the butyl acrylate type, which is tougher and more hydrophobic (Learner, p. 6) → paint-composition
- The binder's glass transition temperature is chosen just below room temperature (about 10-15 C) so the film stays flexible but does not get tacky and pick up dirt (Learner, p. 6) → paint-composition
- Typical additives in an artists' acrylic emulsion and their purpose: surfactant (disperses the polymer in water), antifoam, thickener (buttery consistency), freeze-thaw agent, coalescing solvent (temporarily softens polymer particles during film formation), pH buffer, biocide, pigment dispersant, wetting agent (helps spread over pigments, especially organic ones) (Learner, Table 2, p. 7) → paint-composition
- Dry acrylic emulsion cannot be redissolved in water, because the polymer spheres coalesce into a continuous film once the water evaporates; this permits wet-on-dry painting (Learner, p. 7) → paint-composition
- Modern artists' oil paints: linseed is still common, but safflower or poppy oil is used in whites and light colours because it yellows less; Winsor & Newton blends linseed and safflower, balancing faster drying (more linseed) against less yellowing (more safflower). Driers, stabilizers and dispersing agents are usually added (Learner, p. 8) → lightfastness
- Same passage on oil paint additives: driers, stabilizers and dispersing agents are probably present in modern oil paint (Learner, p. 8) → paint-composition
- Water-miscible oils are oil paints with added surfactant so they can be thinned with water (Learner, p. 8) → paint-composition
- Alkyd paints are oil-modified polyesters (polyol plus polybasic acid, typically glycerol or pentaerythritol with phthalic anhydride); house alkyds are usually "long oil" (above 60 percent oil). They dry by oxidative cross-linking, faster than oil (Learner, p. 8) → paint-composition
- The oil part, not the polyester, has the strongest effect on how much an alkyd yellows; soybean oil is now preferred over linseed because it yellows less (Learner, p. 8) → lightfastness
- Appearance does not reliably identify the binder. An alkyd can dry matte and textured if the solids, particularly extender, are raised relative to the binder (Frank Stella, Hyena Stomp). Acrylic can blend wet-in-wet, and oil can show air bubbles (Learner, p. 11) → paint-composition
- Organic pigments have such high tinting strength that they are added in small amounts, so their infrared peaks are often masked by binder or extender (Learner, p. 13) → tinting-strength
- Reasons artists choose a given paint: optical (gloss, transparency, texture), practical (drying time, diluent), economic, symbolic (Learner, p. 12) → paint-composition

## Croll, "Overview of Developments in the Paint Industry since 1930"

- Paint is a composite of polymer, pigments and additives. A model exterior acrylic latex house paint has about 45 percent pigment volume concentration in the dry film, which gives a probably matte finish (Croll, p. 18) → paint-composition
- Model formulation (Table 1, p. 19): water, polyacrylate dispersant, biocide, nonionic surfactant (wetting agent), titanium dioxide, silicate extender, calcined clay extender, acrylic latex (about 60 percent solids), coalescent, hydroxyethylcellulose thickener, glycols (antifreeze, coalescent), defoamer, ammonium hydroxide for pH (Croll, p. 19) → paint-composition
- An artists' latex paint would be much like the house paint but with more intense colour, less extender and higher viscosity from more thickener (Croll, p. 18) → paint-composition
- Same passage: less extender in artists' paint means higher colour intensity, since extenders replace pigment or polymer volume to cut cost (Croll, pp. 18, 23) → tinting-strength
- Extenders are inorganic minerals (silica, clay, calcium carbonate) whose refractive index is close to the binder's (about 1.56 against 1.5), so they scatter very little light. They are often impure and can carry coloured impurities (Croll, pp. 21-23) → transparency-opacity
- White pigments work by scattering light, not absorbing it. Scattering grows with the refractive index difference between pigment and binder (Fresnel). Table 2 gives titanium dioxide 2.76, white lead 2.0, zinc oxide 2.0, lithopone 1.84, extender 1.56, polymer 1.5. This is why rutile titanium dioxide became the preferred white and hides well even in thin films (Croll, pp. 21-22) → transparency-opacity
- Paint dried above the critical pigment volume concentration (CPVC) contains air voids, which scatter light and give cheap opacity, but weaken the film mechanically and chemically (Croll, pp. 21, 24) → paint-composition
- Same passage: air voids scatter light and make titanium dioxide scatter better, so opacity rises as pigment volume concentration passes the critical value (Croll, p. 24) → transparency-opacity
- Titanium dioxide particles packed too closely scatter less efficiently each ("dependent scattering"), so paints keep titanium dioxide below that concentration (Croll, p. 22) → transparency-opacity
- Anatase titanium dioxide forms free radicals under UV that degrade the surrounding binder; rutile is the later, better form. Surface treatments of silica and alumina reduce this. All titanium dioxide absorbs UV and shields easily degraded binders such as alkyds (Croll, pp. 22-23) → lightfastness
- Coloured pigments absorb particular wavelengths and are made with particles small enough to scatter little, so the colour is not diluted. Many 20th-century organic pigments are degraded by UV, heat or acid pollution, and their bleaching rate falls roughly exponentially as the labile chromophores are used up (Croll, p. 23) → lightfastness
- Traditional mineral colours (iron oxide, cobalt blue) are very lightfast but not intense. Hansa (arylide) yellows are fairly light stable, copper phthalocyanine (1935) is very lightfast, and quinacridone reds (1955) are very lightfast. Intensity and lightfastness are costly, so much commercial paint was made with colours that fade, especially yellows, reds and oranges (Croll, p. 23) → lightfastness
- Some surfactants are intrinsically yellow and can affect colour when they concentrate on drying; some modern biocides may cause measurable yellowing under UV. Surfactant at the surface also gives haze and low gloss (Croll, pp. 20-21) → lightfastness
- White lead and lithopone were the main whites before latex paint and fell away in the 1950s. White lead darkens in polluted air (black lead sulfide) and catalyses oil and alkyd curing (Croll, p. 21) → paint-composition
- Latex film formation: water evaporates, particles pack, coalesce and interdiffuse, helped by a slow-evaporating coalescing aid that temporarily lowers the glass transition temperature (Croll, p. 24) → paint-composition
- Old styrene-butadiene latex binders had chalking and yellowing problems; all-acrylic emulsions are best for exterior use because they resist UV, moisture and pollution (Croll, pp. 25-26) → lightfastness

## Foster, "Artisan Water Mixable Oil Colour" (Winsor & Newton)

- Artisan water-mixable oil is a conventional oil paint (linseed-type oil binder) that contains no water. Components compatible with both oil and water allow thinning with water, and these additives are volatile and do not stay in the dried film. It dries by oxidation like normal oil (Foster, pp. 53-54) → paint-composition
- Foster expects pigment stability to be the same as in ordinary oils: all the pigments have a good lightfastness record in oil-based media (Foster, p. 54) → lightfastness
- Winsor & Newton found Artisan drying times similar to its Artists' Oil and Winton (student grade) oil, color for color (about 1-14 days depending on pigment), with Griffin Alkyd drying in under a day. Films kept getting glossier with age, except alkyd (Foster, p. 55) → paint-composition
- Artisan films were softer and tackier than the other oils, possibly because surfactants migrate to the surface; the film stopped hardening after year one, while the other oils continued. Films with titanium white (PW6) were harder and less tacky (Foster, pp. 55-56) → paint-composition
- Artisan films showed full resistance to water and solvents after two years, including after UV exposure equal to 30 years (Foster, p. 57) → lightfastness

## Hayes, Golden and Smith, "From Formulation to Finished Product" (acrylic emulsion paints)

- Acrylic emulsion paint is a balance of many additives around a hydrophobic polymer dispersed in water: binder plus emulsifier/surfactant, adhesion promoter, initiator, buffer, pigment, wetting agent, dispersing agent, thickener, freeze-thaw stabilizer, coalescent, biocide, pH buffer, defoamer (Hayes et al., Table 2, p. 59) → paint-composition
- Formulation also has to cover UV resistance, colour development, surface clarity, flexibility and drying rate (Hayes et al., Table 1, p. 59) → paint-composition
- The emulsifier stays in the dry film, migrates to the surface and crystallizes, which reduces clarity (cloudiness). It acts as a plasticizer, and washing it off raises the glass transition temperature by about 5 C within an hour (Hayes et al., pp. 59-60) → paint-composition
- Golden raised the glass transition temperature of its line in 1994, when binder makers switched from ethyl acrylate to butyl acrylate copolymer; Hansa Yellow Medium went from 13 C to over 15 C, giving less tack (Hayes et al., p. 60) → paint-composition
- Experiments at Golden with alternative binders (polyurethane, silicone, cross-linking polymers) were rejected for a loss of colour acceptance or for yellowing in the dry film (Hayes et al., p. 61) → lightfastness
- Waxes added as functional additives produced yellowness in the film. Matting solids gave dramatic gains in block resistance but loss of clarity, yellowing, and less brilliance of colour in matte paint lines (Hayes et al., p. 61) → paint-composition
- Surfactant-free polymers increased water sensitivity and caused colour-acceptance problems. Most of the surfactant comes already in the polymer dispersion bought from the supplier (Hayes et al., p. 62) → paint-composition
- Golden's advice to artists: wipe the dried surface lightly with a damp lint-free cloth to remove surfactant, which may remove some colour. To avoid losing colour, apply an isolation coat of the same composition as the colours but without pigment (Hayes et al., p. 62) → paint-composition
- Hayes et al. report that acrylic paints have fared very well over fifty years, with no major deterioration as a group (Hayes et al., pp. 62-63) → lightfastness

## G. Smith, "Aging Characteristics of a Contemporary Acrylic Emulsion"

- Unpigmented films of Rhoplex AC-2235 (n-butyl acrylate/methyl methacrylate dispersion marketed for artists' paint), aged under simulated sunlight through window glass: colour measurement showed initial yellowing that rapidly bleached out, leaving largely colourless films (G. Smith, abstract, p. 236) → lightfastness
- Same study: the dominant long-term chemical change was cross-linking, and the glass transition temperature stayed roughly constant, so flexibility was retained (G. Smith, abstract, p. 236) → lightfastness
- The primary emulsifier, an octylphenol polyethoxylate surfactant (Triton X-405), exudes to the film surface and remains throughout ageing. A 16-year-old naturally aged commercial gel also had significant surfactant on its surface (G. Smith, abstract, p. 236) → paint-composition
- Earlier work cited by Smith: Learner and colleagues found acrylic paints quite resistant to yellowing compared with other artists' media under visible light; Whitmore and Colaluca found strong UV-B caused chain scission, loss of haze and colour and rising solubility (G. Smith, pp. 236-237) → lightfastness
- Yellowing measured as the change in the b* (yellow-blue) CIELab axis. Longest exposure (1196 h) is equivalent to about 200 museum years, assuming 2950 h/year at 150 lux (G. Smith, pp. 238-239) → lightfastness
- Unpigmented acrylic medium was thickened with 1.5 percent hydroxyethylcellulose for film casting; the dispersion is expected to contain a trace biocide (G. Smith, p. 237) → paint-composition

## Lomax, Schilling and Learner, "Identification of Synthetic Organic Pigments by FTIR and DTMS"

- A reference set of 162 dry synthetic organic pigments from manufacturers was analysed. They cover azo and non-azo classes used in house, car and artists' paints, inks, plastics and textiles (Lomax et al., abstract and p. 106) → colour-index-names
- Pigments are named by Colour Index generic name and number (e.g. PY3, PR3, PB15:1, PV19, PO48). Classes covered: monoarylide, diarylide, pyrazolone, disazo condensation, benzimidazolone, beta-naphthol, Naphthol AS, BONA, perinone, perylene, DPP, quinacridone, phthalocyanine, triarylcarbonium (Lomax et al., p. 106) → colour-index-names
- Colour Index names can be ambiguous in practice. One sample labelled PBr23 by its maker was mostly calcium carbonate filler by infrared; the pure pigment from the same maker was needed (Lomax et al., pp. 108-109) → colour-index-names
- Synthetic organic pigments have high tinting strength, so they appear in low amounts in paint; this makes them hard to detect by XRD, XRF, SEM/EDX and light microscopy (Lomax et al., p. 105) → tinting-strength
- Synthetic organic pigments are carbocyclic, ring-containing, manufactured pigments; the quinacridones are described as extremely lightfast violets, reds and oranges (Lomax et al., pp. 105, 108) → lightfastness
- Phthalocyanine blue is noted as the main synthetic blue/green class. Partial chlorination is used to increase stability against flocculation (Lomax et al., pp. 108, 110) → colour-index-names
- Quinacridones PV19, PR206, PO48 and PO49 differ in the ratio of quinacridone to its oxidized quinone form (mass 312 vs 342). More quinone gives a more yellow/orange-brown shade; pure PV19 is red-violet to bluish red (Lomax et al., p. 111) → colour-index-names
- Pigment quality varies: azo pigments that exist as lakes or salts (e.g. PR48, PR49, PR53, PY100) are historically widespread but were poorly handled by the method (Lomax et al., p. 111) → colour-index-names
- Colour Index numbers are cited in the paper for pigments in museum works, e.g. PY1, PY3, PO5, PR1, PR3, PR4 in Pellan (Lomax et al., p. 105) → colour-index-names

## Schilling, Mazurek and Learner, "Studies of Modern Oil-Based Artists' Paint Media by GC/MS"

- Three classes of modern oil-based paint: processed drying/semi-drying oils (linseed, safflower, sunflower, tung, castor), alkyds, and water-mixable oils. The oils are heat-, air- and chemically treated to adjust viscosity, drying and colour (Schilling et al., pp. 129-130) → paint-composition
- Table 1 gives fatty acid compositions of common oils; linseed is rich in linolenic acid (about 47 percent), safflower and sunflower in linoleic acid (about 65-69 percent) (Schilling et al., p. 130) → paint-composition
- A paint maker's oil may not be a single product; suppliers combine oils or fatty-acid fractions for cost and seasonal supply (Schilling et al., p. 130) → paint-composition
- Alkyd resin classes: drying (autoxidize), non-drying (dry by solvent evaporation, e.g. coconut oil based) and modified. By oil length: short 35-45, medium 46-55, long 56-70, very long more than 70 percent; most artists' and house alkyds are long-oil (Schilling et al., pp. 130-131) → paint-composition
- Water-mixable oil paints (Winsor & Newton Artisan, Holbein, Grumbacher, Royal Talens) use polyoxyethylene nonionic surfactants with HLB 8-18, with unsaturated fatty chains that can cross-link into the oil, plus amine soaps and castor-oil thixotropic stabilizers; the paints lose water solubility when dry (Schilling et al., p. 132) → paint-composition

## Posters

- Bannerman, UV ageing of acrylic paint films: tested titanium white, gesso, phthalocyanine green and acrylic resin; humidity and light both shift the brittle-to-ductile transition temperature, and water's plasticizing effect makes films more vulnerable to light ageing (Bannerman, p. 271) → lightfastness
- Del Hoyo-Melendez and Domenech-Carbo: linseed oil mixed with synthetic organic pigments (PR5, PY74, PR170, PG7, PV19, PR83, PY110) at 10, 25 and 50 percent was aged by heat and light. Some pigments seemed to inhibit long-term ageing of the oil (carboxylic acid shoulder behaviour differed by pigment) (del Hoyo-Melendez and Domenech-Carbo, p. 277) → lightfastness
- Gates et al., Magna (acrylic solution) paint of Morris Louis: wax-like long-chain alkanes were found only in red and orange paints containing cadmium sulfoselenide, so wax may have been added to those pigments to improve stability or dispersion (not confirmed) (Gates et al., pp. 277-278) → paint-composition
- Cross (Trevelyan poster): a black gloss paint was judged commercial because of its relatively high amount of extenders (Cross, p. 276) → paint-composition
