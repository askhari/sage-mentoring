Quotes of critics are the author's translations from French/Spanish.

## Definition and the common belief
- Abstract: optical mixing is defined as splitting a colour into its components and having the tone rebuilt in the viewer's retina. The paper argues the belief that the Impressionists did this is a myth, born when Seurat and Signac showed at the 8th Impressionist exhibition (1886). (abstract, p. 301 / PDF p. 2) → optical-mixing
- The common formulation of the "myth": Monet, Renoir, Sisley and Pissarro hardly mixed on the palette. They put separate strokes of a few basic colours on the canvas so the eye fuses them at a suitable distance. (p. 303) → optical-mixing
- The supposed payoff of this belief: colours more intense and luminous than physical pigment mixing, because retinal mixtures would behave like rays of light. (p. 303) → optical-mixing
- The author says nobody has pointed to a specific Impressionist painting where specific colours, applied in a specific way, give a specific tone when mixed at a specific distance. (pp. 303-304; author's claim) → optical-mixing
- The author sees the basic error as confusing two incompatible phenomena: optical fusion and vibration by contrast. (p. 304) → optical-mixing
- The same point appears as a footnote on Lecomte: optical mixing and tonal contrast are opposed by definition, because fusion cancels contrast, and if contrast is perceived then fusion is not taking place. (n. 4, p. 323) → optical-mixing
- Mixing and contrast excluding each other: Fénéon is also criticised for thinking chromatic vibration is compatible with optical mixing, when they exclude each other. (n. 3, p. 323) → simultaneous-contrast

## Did Seurat/Signac (divisionists) aim for optical mixture? Did the Impressionists?
- The divisionists (Neo-Impressionists) did pursue optical mixing programmatically, and the term "division of tone" had been in Neo-Impressionist criticism since the mid-1880s. (p. 316; author's interpretation) → optical-mixing
- Fénéon (1887), the divisionists' theorist, described Seurat's innovation as "scientific division of tone". Instead of mixing paints on the palette, the painter places separate strokes (local colour, light quality, reflections from neighbours, complementaries of ambient colours) and "the colours are composed on the retina: an optical mixture". (quoted p. 306-307) → optical-mixing
- The same Fénéon quote lists the complementaries of the surrounding colours as one of the components placed as separate strokes in Seurat's method. (p. 307) → complementary-colors
- Fénéon's four-point summary of the "spirit of Impressionism" (1887) does not mention optical mixing. (p. 306) → optical-mixing
- Signac (De Eugène Delacroix au néo-impressionnisme, 1899) states that Neo-Impressionists developed the "division" technique using the optical mixture of tones and tints as the means of expression. (p. 319) → optical-mixing
- Signac's three-stage scheme: Delacroix mixes on the palette and optically with scientific purpose; the Impressionists mix on the palette and only occasionally on the retina, by instinct and so crudely; the Neo-Impressionists use only optical mixing. (p. 320; the author's reading of Signac) → optical-mixing
- Signac criticised the Impressionists' hasty work for physically fusing opposing colours on the canvas into a dull "pigmentary" grey, which dims the splendour. (Signac 1941, p. 51, quoted p. 320) → subtractive-mixing
- The Neo-Impressionist avoids "soiling" the purity of colours and the meeting of opposed elements on the support. Slow, careful divided strokes keep colour clean on the palette and the canvas. (p. 320) → optical-mixing
- Signac was already aware that a very atomised touch, as in Seurat's "Les Poseuses" (1886-88), was more a drawback than a virtue for the brilliance of the fused effect. (p. 320, citing Cachin 2000) → optical-mixing
- Signac was beginning to see by 1897 that retinal fusion would have spoiled the chromatic effect, as material mixing would to a greater degree. (p. 322; author's interpretation) → optical-mixing
- The author states the paper does not settle how much the divisionists themselves actually used optical mixing; he defers that to future work. (p. 322) → optical-mixing

## What the Impressionists themselves said / did (author's evidence)
- Pissarro in a 1886 letter to Durand-Ruel described the Neo-Impressionist method as replacing pigment mixture by optical mixture, "because optical mixture gives much more intense luminosities than pigment mixture". (Pissarro 1978, pp. 71-72, quoted p. 308) → optical-mixing
- The author reads that letter as implying the earlier Impressionists were practising pigment mixing. In Jan 1887 Pissarro also criticised Monet's dull tonality and praised the advantage of "colours without mixture" in his own canvas. The author concludes Monet apparently still mixed on the palette until at least 1887. (p. 308; author's interpretation) → optical-mixing
- Duret (1904): the new procedure of putting unmixed colours side by side as dots appeared in 1886 and was unknown to the first Impressionists. Vollard (1937) ties the "Neo-Impressionists" to Chevreul's discoveries and says for them "the eye does the mixing", so pure tones go on the canvas. (quoted p. 307) → optical-mixing
- Renoir, asked by Vollard whether Watteau's brushwork anticipated Monet's division of tones, replied that under a magnifying glass Watteau shows only mixed tones. (Vollard 1944, quoted p. 311) → optical-mixing
- Monet, in 1900, said that in 1867 he was far from adopting the "division of colours"; the author reads this as an ambiguous phrase that critics confused with optical mixing. (Mirbeau & Thiébault-Sisson 2011, p. 69, discussed p. 316-318; author's interpretation) → optical-mixing
- In the author's reading, Monet's "division of colours" meant giving up uniform colour and blended strokes for fractured touches and vibration by proximity. This does not exclude mixing on the palette for each tint. At some distance a degree of optical fusion is "undeniable and inevitable", but it is doubtful it was the conscious aim. (pp. 317-318; author's interpretation) → optical-mixing
- Beyond a certain viewing distance the touches start to fuse and the picture looks noticeably duller, though this is mitigated by saturated local colours and large coloured masses. (p. 318; author's interpretation) → optical-mixing
- The author's thesis: Impressionists never made conscious use of optical mixing, and the false link arises after the 1886 exhibition. (conclusion, p. 321; author's claim, argued from critics and letters) → optical-mixing
- Francastel (quoted p. 314): Monet's colours are not pure spectral colours, his strokes are superimposed not just juxtaposed, and he always mixed two or three colours to get the tone. (Francastel 1970, pp. 316-317) → optical-mixing
- Fragmented colour that looks "dull and brownish" if physically mixed on the palette is part of why critics find optical mixing plausible. (p. 317) → subtractive-mixing

## Origins of the idea; named authorities
- The myth was spread by critics (Adam 1886, Sizeranne 1900, Marcel 1905, Souriau 1913, Lamandé 1925) and by Signac's pamphlet. The author argues it persisted through "the principle of authority". (pp. 305-314, 319; author's interpretation) → optical-mixing
- Marcel (1905) claimed Impressionists put yellow and blue strokes side by side so the eye fuses them into green. Albers (Interaction of Color, 1963) repeats it, and the author calls this a flagrant confusion. (quoted pp. 312, 315) → optical-mixing
- Albers is quoted as saying that instead of green paint from mechanical mixing of yellow and blue, painters put yellow and blue dots that mix only in perception. (Albers 1999, p. 47, quoted p. 315) → subtractive-mixing
- Lamandé (1925): colours in nature arise by juxtaposition of vibrations, so painters supposedly juxtapose the seven prism colours to fuse in the retina. (quoted p. 313) → optical-mixing
- Souriau (1913): hues obtained not by real pigment mixing but by optical mixing of dissociated tones; ideal is only pure spectral colours giving all intermediate tints by fusion at a distance. (quoted p. 312) → optical-mixing
- Domínguez (1900) claimed the Impressionist creed was to avoid mixing on the palette because tones go dull and cold when mixed there. Sizeranne (1900) said Impressionists banned neutral colours and mixing to avoid dulling brightness. (quoted pp. 309-310) → subtractive-mixing
- Chevreul: appears only through Vollard's remark (neo-Impressionists adapting Chevreul's discoveries) and through Roque's book title. The paper does not discuss Chevreul's law in detail. (p. 307, 324-325) → optical-mixing
- Rood and Helmholtz are not mentioned in the paper. (whole text) → optical-mixing

## Additive vs subtractive (limited coverage)
- Footnote 3: Fénéon wrongly identifies optical synthesis with additive synthesis, according to the author. No further explanation of additive/subtractive mixing is given. (n. 3, p. 323) → additive-mixing
- The idea that retinally mixed colours "behave like light rays" while pigment mixing darkens (the belief the paper describes, not the paper's own account). (p. 303) → additive-mixing
- Pigment mixing is repeatedly described in the quoted sources as dulling or darkening ("necessarily darker mixing on the palette", Lecomte quoted p. 308; "dull and brownish", p. 317). The paper reports this as belief and does not explain the physics. (pp. 308, 317) → subtractive-mixing

## Complementary colours and simultaneous contrast
- Lecomte (1890, cited by Roque) said Monet and Pissarro brought back from Turner the "law of complementaries" and division of tone; optical reconstitution of divided complementary colours supposedly gave them luminous clarity. (quoted p. 308) → complementary-colors
- Francastel quotes the myth as claiming Monet put pure spectral colours side by side following "the law of complementaries and simultaneous contrast of colours" to obtain the tone required, and then rejects it. (p. 314) → complementary-colors
- Same Francastel passage links juxtaposition to the "law of ... simultaneous contrast". The paper does not describe Chevreul's law itself, only that contrast vibration differs from optical fusion. (p. 314, p. 304) → simultaneous-contrast
- Footnote 1: Preckler says the Impressionists, applying Chevreul's discoveries, used only the six primary and secondary colours. The author says she is unclear whether secondaries came from optical mixing of the primaries or were just tube colours. (n. 1, p. 323) → complementary-colors
