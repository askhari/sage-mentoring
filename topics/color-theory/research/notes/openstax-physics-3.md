## light-spectrum

- Maxwell's equations predict EM waves; the chapter frames radio, infrared, visible, ultraviolet and X-rays as kinds of the same radiation (Vol 3, ch. 1 intro). → light-spectrum
- Light is a form of electromagnetic radiation; Maxwell's predicted EM waves travel at the speed of light, which Hertz confirmed, using v = fλ (Vol 2, §16.1). → light-spectrum
- c = fλ holds for all EM waves: higher frequency means shorter wavelength (Vol 2, §16.5). → light-spectrum
- Visible light spans about 400 to 750 nm (figure labels violet 400 nm, red 700 nm). Red has the longest wavelength and lowest frequency, violet the shortest and highest. The seven rainbow colors lie between them, with no sharp boundaries (Vol 2, §16.5). ⚠️ Vol 3 §1.1 only says the visible wavelength is under a micron, and gives no range. → light-spectrum
- Infrared and ultraviolet are named for lying just below red and above violet (Vol 2, §16.5). → light-spectrum
- Approximate band order, long to short wavelength: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet (about 400 to 10 nm), X-rays, gamma rays (Vol 2, §16.5). → light-spectrum
- Dispersion is the spreading of white light into its full spectrum of wavelengths (Vol 3, §1.5). → light-spectrum
- The index of refraction depends on wavelength and rises as wavelength falls, so violet bends more than red (Vol 3, §1.5). → light-spectrum
- A glass prism refracts white light at both surfaces and separates it into a red-to-violet sequence (Vol 3, §1.5). → light-spectrum
- Crown glass: n about 1.512 for red (660 nm) and 1.530 for violet (410 nm) (Vol 3, §1.5, Table 1.2). In the worked example, red and violet rays are about 0.3° apart inside the glass (§1.5). → light-spectrum
- A rainbow looks like six colors but is a continuous distribution of wavelengths. Raindrops refract, reflect at the back, then refract again. The back reflection does not disperse light, because reflection does not depend on wavelength. The secondary rainbow, from two internal reflections, shows the colors in reverse order (Vol 3, §1.5). → light-spectrum
- Laser light is nearly a single wavelength, so it disperses little (Vol 3, §1.5). → light-spectrum
- Refraction table values of n are given for 589 nm light in vacuum (Vol 3, §1.1, Table 1.1). → light-spectrum

## light-matter-interaction

- Light slows down in matter because it interacts with atoms. n = c/v, with n ≥ 1 (Vol 3, §1.1, §1.3). → light-matter-interaction
- Refraction is the change of direction of a ray passing between media of different n, because the speed changes. Snell's law is n1 sinθ1 = n2 sinθ2. Entering a higher-n medium bends the ray toward the normal (Vol 3, §1.3). → light-matter-interaction
- Law of reflection: the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence (Vol 3, §1.2). → light-matter-interaction
- Specular reflection occurs on surfaces smooth relative to the wavelength (mirror). Diffuse reflection occurs on rough surfaces (paper, clothes, leaves), which scatter light in many directions so the object is visible from any side (Vol 3, §1.2). → light-matter-interaction
- Light scattered by air is partially polarized. The electric field of the light drives electrons in air molecules, which re-radiate light polarized perpendicular to the original ray. Smoke and dust also polarize scattered light (Vol 3, §1.7). → light-matter-interaction
- Visible light passes through the atmosphere to the surface. The atmosphere absorbs about 23% of incoming solar energy, mostly infrared and UV. Ozone absorbs most UV-B and all UV-C (Vol 2, §16.5). → light-matter-interaction
- NOT covered: absorption and transmission by materials as a cause of object color, and why objects appear colored. Neither §1.2 nor §1.1 discusses absorption or color of objects. → light-matter-interaction

## illuminant-color

- A blackbody is a perfect absorber, approximated by a cavity with a small hole. In thermal equilibrium good absorbers are also good emitters (Vol 3, §6.1). → illuminant-color
- Wien's law: λmax·T = 2.898×10⁻³ m·K. Hotter bodies peak at shorter wavelengths (Vol 3, §6.1). → illuminant-color
- A body that heats up glows dull red, then orange, then spreads across the whole visible spectrum, as in an incandescent filament (Vol 3, §6.1). → illuminant-color
- Approximate peaks (Fig. 6.3): 2000 K far infrared, 3000 K near infrared, 4000 K red part of the visible range, 5000 K green part. A body near 300 K radiates mostly infrared (Vol 3, §6.1). → illuminant-color
- The Sun's surface (about 5000 to 6000 K) peaks near 560 nm (Vol 3, §6.1). → illuminant-color
- Betelgeuse (red) is cooler than Rigel (blue), by Wien's law (Vol 3, §6.1, Ex. 6.1). → illuminant-color
- Sunlight's blackbody spectrum peaks in the visible range but is stronger in red than violet, so the Sun looks yellowish (Vol 2, §16.5). ⚠️ This is a different statement from the 560 nm peak in Vol 3 §6.1. The two sections give different framings and temperatures (Vol 2 Fig. 16.17 links visible emission to bodies around 10,000 K), so check before combining. → illuminant-color
- Visible light is emitted by vibrations and rotations of atoms and molecules and by electronic transitions. Thermal motion also emits across the spectrum (Vol 2, §16.5). → illuminant-color
- The text does not use the term "color temperature" and does not discuss lamp spectra such as LED or fluorescent. The unanswered exercises ask which flame is hotter (yellowish candle or bluish Bunsen) (Vol 3, §6.1). → illuminant-color
