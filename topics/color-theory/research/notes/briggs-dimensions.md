## Attributes (Google Sites page "1 Attributes of colour perceptions"; site pages 013-018.php, 1117.php)
- The CIE vocabulary (e-ILV) recognises six attributes of perceived colour: hue, brightness, lightness, colourfulness, saturation, chroma; some systems add blackness and its inverse, brilliance (Google Sites p. 1; 1117.php) → saturation-and-colorfulness
- Hue is defined by similarity to red, yellow, green, blue arranged in a closed ring; the four unique hues form two opponent pairs (red/green, yellow/blue), following Hering's opponency, which also underlies NCS (Google Sites p. 1; 073.php "hue circle, opponent") → hue
- Briggs compares a generic opponent hue scale, the Munsell hue scale and Itten's traditional wheel and notes the traditional wheel is perceptually uneven (Google Sites p. 1) → color-wheel, color-wheel-models
- Lightness is the scale from black through greys to white, judged relative to a similarly lit white; only related colours (seen in context) have lightness (013.php; Google Sites p. 1) → value
- Chroma is the departure of a colour from a grey of the same lightness (013-015.php glossary description; Google Sites p. 1; 082.php) → chroma
- Brightness is perceived amount of light, with no upper limit; lights are "bright/dim", surfaces "light/dark" (016.php; Google Sites p. 1) → saturation-and-colorfulness
- Colourfulness is how chromatic an area appears and increases with stronger lighting; in 1117.php brightness and colourfulness describe the light coming from parts of an object, while lightness and chroma describe the colour attributed to the object itself → saturation-and-colorfulness
- Formal CIE saturation is colourfulness judged relative to brightness (colourfulness is described as saturation times brightness); Briggs warns the word is used loosely in digital systems: in HSB/HSV "S" is relative saturation, in HLS it is effectively relative chroma (017.php, 093.php "HSB, HSV"; Google Sites p. 1) → saturation-and-colorfulness
- Artists often use "saturation" to mean chroma (Google Sites p. 1) → saturation-and-colorfulness, chroma
- For painters, chroma is the perceived strength of a surface colour; it is zero at white and black and peaks at intermediate values; maximum chroma differs by hue (yellow reaches higher chroma than blue) (082.php "chroma", as excerpted) → chroma
- Colour constancy example ("Orange Cube"): lightness and chroma are perceived as belonging to the object, while brightness/colourfulness variations across faces are attributed to the lighting (Google Sites p. 1) → value, chroma, saturation-and-colorfulness
- Lightness-based spaces (Munsell, CIELAB's L*Ch, RAL D4) are the framework many artists and designers use (Google Sites p. 1) → munsell-system, cielab-basics

## Colour wheels and complements
- The RYB "artists' colour wheel" is built on the three historical primaries red, yellow, blue, with complements red-green, yellow-violet, blue-orange; it rests on a theory overturned in the second half of the 19th century but is still the base of much art/design teaching (072.php "traditional colour wheel") → color-wheel-models, primary-colors, complementary-colors
- Munsell's original hue circle used opposite pairs chosen because they mixed to neutral grey on spinning discs, i.e. as visual complements; the renotated Munsell system is still used in science and industry and by a smaller group of painters (112.php "modern colour theory", as excerpted) → munsell-system, color-wheel-models, complementary-colors
- Additive complements: cyan, not green, complements red, and magenta, not red, complements green (043.php, 074.php) → color-wheel-models, complementary-colors
- Briggs states that paint-mixing complements agree more closely with the additive pairs than with the RYB wheel (074.php/063.php, as excerpted) → color-wheel-models, complementary-colors
- The most direct paint-mixing complement of most paints lies roughly opposite on the Munsell hue circle, except near the yellow to blue-purple axis, where mixing paths curve through middle-chroma greens (063.php "paint mixing, complementary") → complementary-colors, pigment-mixing-behavior
- Painters' psychological primaries sit near Munsell 5R, 5Y, 5G, with a middle blue near cobalt (5PB); two traditions disagree on the warm/cool polarity of blue (112.php, as excerpted) → primary-colors
- Mixing a paint with its complement usually lowers both value and chroma, at a rate that depends on the pigments (063.php) → complementary-colors, pigment-mixing-behavior, chroma

## Munsell and CIELAB for painters
- Painters mostly work with relative hue, value and chroma rather than absolute Munsell notation, though Briggs sees practical benefits in an absolute value scale (112.php/081.php, as excerpted) → munsell-system, value
- Munsell value 10 refers to a perfect diffuse reflector while CIE L* refers to a near-perfect white standard, so Munsell value 10 corresponds to L* above 100 (081.php "lightness, value") → munsell-system, cielab-basics, value
- Most black paints measure about value 2 in the modern Munsell system; Frank Reilly's scale has eleven levels with black and white paint at 0 and 10 (081.php, as excerpted) → value, munsell-system
- Gamut: the highest-chroma acrylic mixtures sit close to the top of the Munsell solid; the zero-blackness point lies just above the top of the solid and sits at different values depending on hue and chroma (pages on gamut of mixtures, as excerpted; exact URL not captured) → munsell-system, chroma
- Maximum chroma for green-to-blue paints is about 12 Munsell units at optimal white content, versus 14-16 for many reds, oranges, yellows (same gamut pages, as excerpted) → chroma, munsell-system, pigment-mixing-behavior
- 083.php is titled "hue chroma lightness spaces" and 093.php covers HSB/HSV; the excerpts do not show their content (title only) → munsell-system, cielab-basics

## Painter practice
- To mix a target colour: choose paints on either side of the target in hue, raise or lower each to the target's value, mix them, then reduce chroma if needed (109.php "Applying the Principles in Paint", as excerpted) → value, chroma, pigment-mixing-behavior
- High-chroma colours tend to look lighter than their stated value (a glow/brilliance effect), so people overestimate their lightness; squinting reduces the glow and helps compare value (081.php/013.php, as excerpted) → value, chroma
- Shading series (101.php): in a series where chroma falls as value falls, saturation stays constant; black pigment dulls a mixture faster than it lowers lightness (as excerpted; second claim's location uncertain) → tints-shades-tones, pigment-mixing-behavior
- A tint is an intermediate between white and a pure colour (excerpt of a 1117.php/related page) → tints-shades-tones
- Blackness, whiteness and chromaticness underlie the popular tints/shades/tones classification and are used in NCS and Ostwald (Google Sites p. 1) → tints-shades-tones
