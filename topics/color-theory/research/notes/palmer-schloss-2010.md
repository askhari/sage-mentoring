## color-meaning

- Ecological valence theory (EVT): colour preference arises from people's average affective (liked/disliked) responses to objects associated with each colour; e.g. blues liked via clear sky and clean water, browns disliked via faeces and rotten food (p. 1, abstract; p. 2). → color-meaning
- EVT is adaptive in premise: being attracted to objects whose colours "look good" and avoiding those that "look bad" helps survival, if colour appeal tracks how advantageous the typical objects of that colour are (p. 1). → color-meaning
- Unlike purely evolutionary accounts (Humphrey; Hurlbert & Ling), EVT also allows learning during an individual's lifetime, by analogy with taste preferences having genetic and learned parts (pp. 1-2). → color-meaning
- EVT links to the colour-emotion theory: the emotional outcomes of lifetime experiences with coloured objects provide the feedback that shapes colour liking (p. 2). → color-meaning
- Method for the associations: 74 people wrote down objects characteristically of each colour; answers were reduced to 222 object categories, rated for appeal by 98 others, and a further 31 rated how well each object matched each colour (p. 3; p. 5-6). WAVE (weighted affective valence estimate) for a colour = mean over its associated objects of (colour-object match x object valence) (p. 3). → color-meaning
- Evidence is correlational, but the authors argue causation runs from object to colour preference: if colour drove object liking, chocolate and faeces (similar colour) would be equally appealing (p. 3). → color-meaning
- Possible universality: the basic hue pattern agrees with earlier studies and infant looking biases, so some preferences may be universal (blue/cyan = sky, water; brown/olive = faeces, rot); whether innate or learned is left open (p. 4). → color-meaning
- Cultural variation (preliminary, Japan vs US): within-culture WAVEs predicted preferences better than across-culture ones (US-US r = 0.89 vs US WAVE to Japan r = 0.74; Japan-Japan 0.66 vs 0.55). Mexico, India, Serbia were under study (p. 4). → color-meaning
- Individual variation: two groups of Americans with similar preferences (n = 17 and 12) were predicted better by their own WAVEs (r = 0.77, 0.83) than by the other group's (0.47, 0.64) (p. 4). → color-meaning
- Institutional associations change preference: Berkeley students' school spirit correlated positively with liking Berkeley blue/gold and negatively with Stanford red/white; the reverse for Stanford students (preliminary) (p. 4). → color-meaning
- Experimental manipulation: showing pictures of desirable red (or green) objects raised preference for red (or green) significantly; decreases after undesirable objects were not reliable (preliminary) (pp. 4-5). → color-meaning
- Colour can also influence object preference (e.g. paint, clothes, furniture); EVT predicts self-reinforcing colour preferences until boredom, new circumstances or fashion change them (p. 5). → color-meaning
- Misfit cases of WAVE: it underpredicts dislike of dark orange (chocolate, coffee are appealing) and the liking for dark red (blood is unappealing) (p. 3). → color-meaning
- Colour-emotion theory (Ou et al.) defines colour-emotions as feelings evoked by colours or combinations, but does not explain how they arise or why some predict preference better (p. 1). Compared with it, EVT is argued to be the more causal explanation (p. 3). → color-meaning

## harmony-research

- Sample: 48 participants (mean age 32, equal sexes, non-colour-deficient, Bay Area) rated how much they liked each of 32 chromatic colours (8 hues x 4 "cuts": saturated, light, muted, dark) on a -100 to +100 scale, on a grey background (p. 2; p. 5). → harmony-research
- Colours were chosen from Munsell samples spanning eight hues (four unique hues R, Y, G, B plus orange, chartreuse, cyan, purple) and rendered on a calibrated monitor (p. 5). → harmony-research
- Pattern: saturated, light and muted colours gave roughly parallel hue curves with a broad peak at blue and a narrow trough at chartreuse; saturated colours were preferred over light and muted, which did not differ (p. 2). → harmony-research
- Dark colours showed a different hue pattern: dark orange (brown) and dark yellow (olive) were less liked than other oranges/yellows; dark red and dark green were more liked than other reds/greens (p. 2). → harmony-research
- WAVE alone correlates r = +0.893 with mean preference, 80% of variance, with no free parameters (p. 3). → harmony-research
- Model comparison on the same data: cone-contrast (Hurlbert & Ling extended) 37%; colour-appearance (blue-yellow 34%, saturation +19%, light-dark +7%) 60%; colour-emotion 55%; WAVE 80% with fewer predictors (p. 3). → harmony-research
- The cone-contrast model explained 70% in Hurlbert & Ling's narrow-gamut data and 64.4% on 8 analogous colours here, but dropped to 37% with the full 32-colour gamut (p. 1; p. 3). → harmony-research
- Cone-contrast components: violet preferred over yellow-green (S axis, 21%), lighter, more saturated, and (small) more blue-green preferred (p. 3). Hurlbert & Ling's sex difference (women redder, men bluer-green) is described as the basis of their evolutionary hypothesis (p. 1). → harmony-research
- Colour-appearance model fits well but fails to predict the interaction in the dark cut and only describes, not explains (p. 3). → harmony-research
- WAVE captures the blue peak, chartreuse trough, preference for saturated colours and the minimum at dark yellow (p. 3). → harmony-research
- Limitations: sample is modern Americans from Berkeley, group data is mostly preliminary (small n, 29 for clustering), evidence correlational, innate vs learned open (pp. 3-4). Valence-consistency bias in object listing is argued not to explain the result, because both groups rated the same 222 objects (p. 4). Preferences are for single colours on grey, not combinations (p. 2, method). ⚠️ The last point is inferred from the design, not stated as a limitation by the authors. → harmony-research
- Earlier research was mostly psychophysical description of preferences, giving no explanation of why (p. 1). → harmony-research

## color-temperature

- Ou et al.'s colour-emotion model rests on three factor-analytic dimensions: active/passive (active preferred), heavy/light (light preferred), warm/cool (cool preferred), explaining 67% of variance in their data (p. 1). → color-temperature
- In this study's data the colour-emotion model explained 55%: active/passive 22% (more active preferred), warm/cool +26% (cooler preferred), heavy/light +7% (lighter preferred) (p. 3). → color-temperature
- Warm/cool was rated directly by participants on a warm-to-cool scale, along with active-passive and strong-weak scales, on a separate day (p. 5). ⚠️ The Methods list "strong/weak" while the Results name the factor "heavy/light"; the paper does not reconcile this. → color-temperature
- Active/passive ratings were transformed with a hyperbolic tangent, following Ou et al. (p. 3, footnote §). → color-temperature
- The paper reports only that cooler colours are preferred on average; it gives no values per hue and no definition of warm/cool beyond the rating scale (p. 3). → color-temperature
