## Equiluminance and the two systems
- Perceived brightness (luminance) is what artists call "value". Yellow and green look brighter than blue and red because of photoreceptor sensitivity, not photon count (Harvard Magazine article) → value-structure
- Luminance drives depth, motion and spatial organization; the colour-sensitive system does not. Livingstone calls the colour-blind luminance pathway the "Where" system and the colour/object/face pathway the "What" system (Harvard Magazine; Bateman review) → equiluminance
- In Monet's Impression, Sunrise, the sun has the same luminance as the sky, so the Where system cannot detect it; the sun appears to vibrate or pulsate. In a black-and-white reproduction it vanishes (Harvard Magazine; Bateman review) → equiluminance
- Bateman: the conflict between the two systems is what gives the sun its vibrancy; a version with a lighter, more naturalistic sun loses it (review) → equiluminance
- Mondrian's Broadway Boogie Woogie uses yellow and gray nearly equiluminant with the off-white ground, so squares seem to jitter or move (Harvard Magazine) → equiluminance
- Shading by luminance gradient gives a sense of three-dimensionality; a shape shaded only by colour change looks flat (Harvard Magazine) → value-structure
- Artists since the 13th century (Leonardo, Michelangelo, Ingres, Matisse named) have built form with luminance contrast; Picasso is quoted as saying reality is found in lightness alone (Harvard Magazine) → value-structure
- Bateman: pigments with only about a 20:1 range can reproduce an outdoor scene whose contrast range is roughly 1000:1 (review; contrast discussion) → value-structure

## Pointillism / resolution
- Livingstone's lecture abstract links the dynamic effect of Pointillist paintings to differences in visual resolution across the visual field (central vs peripheral); same cause makes the Mona Lisa's smile elusive (RIT/Penn State lecture abstract). Mechanism details (colour vs luminance resolution) not seen in accessible material → optical-mixing
