## Notation and system (munsell-system)
- Colors are written as Hue, Value/Chroma, with the example R 5/5 (hue red, value 5, chroma 5). (front matter) → munsell-system
- Purple-Blue at value 3 and chroma 2 is written P-B 3/2. The book contrasts this precise formula with vague names such as plum, olive, mouse. (pp. ~17-18) → munsell-system
- The color sphere has white at the north pole, black at the south, a vertical gray scale as axis and the hues around the equator. (Intro, pp. ~3-5) → munsell-system
- Three scales define a color: value (vertical), chroma (outward from the axis), hue (around the circle). Strong pigments may lie outside the sphere. (Intro) → munsell-system, hue, value, chroma
- Vermilion is given as R 5/10. (Intro) → munsell-system
- The sphere is limited by the shortest chroma path. Maximum chroma differs by hue, so the full set of paths forms an irregular "color tree", not a sphere. (p. ~16) → munsell-system
- Printing inks and dyes can exceed the pigment limits used for the sphere. (pp. ~16-17) → munsell-system
- The system concerns pigments, not light wavelengths (footnote, p. ~13). → munsell-system
- The preface presents the system as an aid to color perception and not a dogma, aimed at printers, publishers and advertisers. (Preface) → munsell-system

## Hue
- Hue is the quality that distinguishes red from yellow, green, blue or purple. (p. ~13) → hue
- The hues are ordered like the spectrum, and the sequence closes back on red, forming a circle. (pp. ~13-14) → hue, color-wheel
- Each main step is subdivided into 10 (numbered 1-10), about the finest difference a trained eye can tell. 5 marks a simple hue, 10 a compound one, and the number goes before the letter (e.g. 7R). (p. ~14) → hue, munsell-system
- The book avoids "orange", "pink" and "violet" and uses mixture names like "yellow-red". (footnote, p. ~13) → hue

## Color wheel
- The hue circle has 5 principal hues (Red, Yellow, Green, Blue, Purple) and 5 intermediate hues between them (Yellow-Red, Green-Yellow, Blue-Green, Purple-Blue, Red-Purple), making 10 main divisions. (Intro; pp. ~13-14) → color-wheel
- Opposite (complementary) hues lie directly across the circle, with the neutral axis between them. (pp. ~18-19) → color-wheel
- Mixing opposites in proper proportions gives neutral gray. The book gives this as the reason they are the strongest contrast. (pp. ~18-20) → color-wheel
- The principal hues are not red-yellow-blue. The book does not discuss RYB or criticize other circles. It only notes that vague color names are misleading, and that this is a pigment-based system. (pp. ~13, 18) → color-wheel-models

## Value
- Value is lightness on a neutral scale from black (0) to white (10), with nine grays between them. Middle value is 5. (pp. ~14-15) → value
- The value steps were set with a photometer. (p. ~15) → value
- Maroon is a low-value red and pink is a high-value red. So value distinguishes what common names call different colors. (p. ~15) → value
- Yellow reaches its maximum chroma at about value step 7, higher than other hues. (footnote, p. ~16) → value, chroma
- Yellow's chroma path is longer than that of purple-blue at high values and shorter at low values. (p. ~19) → value, chroma

## Chroma
- Chroma is strength of color, measured outward from the neutral axis. (pp. ~15-17) → chroma
- Pigments differ in strength. Red has the longest chroma path (about 10 steps from neutral) and blue-green the shortest (about 5). (p. ~16) → chroma, munsell-system
- The strongest dry yellow pigment reaches about chroma 9. (p. ~17) → chroma

## Balance, dominance, accent (⚠️ prescriptive rules, not empirical)
- ⚠️ Color balances around middle gray. A stronger color needs a smaller area, and larger areas should be grayer. (Intro; p. ~20) → dominance-and-accent
- ⚠️ A strong color overpowers a weak opposite of equal area, as in a lopsided tug-of-war. The weak color needs more area. (p. ~20) → dominance-and-accent
- ⚠️ A small patch of strong color can balance a large field of weak or grayed color, which is the accent idea. The text does not use the terms "dominance" or "accent". (p. ~20) → dominance-and-accent
- ⚠️ Rule of thumb: multiply value by chroma for each color. Areas are inversely proportional to these products. Example: Y 7/9 (product 63) against P-B 3/4 (product 12) uses about 12 parts yellow to 63 parts purple-blue. (pp. ~21-22) → dominance-and-accent, value-structure
- ⚠️ Preferably the larger area is the darker, weaker color and the smaller area the lighter, stronger one. This is a guide, not a strict requirement. (p. ~23) → dominance-and-accent, value-structure
- ⚠️ Deliberate imbalance can enliven a design (contrast, discord) if used carefully, as in advertising. (Intro) → dominance-and-accent, color-schemes

## Color combinations (color-schemes)
- ⚠️ The book names "paths" through the color solid: a vertical path (value), an equatorial path (neighboring hues or opposites) and an inclined path that rises in value on one side and falls on the other. (Intro) → color-schemes, value-structure
- ⚠️ Opposite hues give a strong, simple harmony that heightens both. (pp. ~18-19, 23) → color-schemes
- ⚠️ Single hue: vary value and chroma inside one hue, pairing low with high value or weak with strong chroma. Many pleasing results come from this. (pp. ~23-24) → color-schemes, value-structure
- ⚠️ Neighboring hues harmonize through closeness, not contrast. (p. ~24) → color-schemes
- ⚠️ With three or more colors, use a restricted hue field and regular, ordered steps. A third color can be balanced by splitting the opposite of a hue between two colors. (p. ~24) → color-schemes, value-structure
- ⚠️ Elliptical path: colors of opposite hues kept about three steps from the neutral pole, or along the resulting ellipse near the axis, stay in accord. (p. ~25) → color-schemes
- ⚠️ "Color harmony" is described as another name for color order, so harmony is defined by orderly steps in value, chroma and hue. (p. ~25) → color-schemes, value-structure

## Not covered or weak
- color-wheel-models: not covered. The book never mentions RYB, so the caller needs another source.
- dominance-and-accent: only the area-balance idea, with no named concept.
- Level: historical primary source, intro to university level, written for printers and designers. Harmony rules are prescriptive and the book is a 1921 pigment-based version of the Munsell system, so values and chroma limits differ from the modern Munsell Renotation.
