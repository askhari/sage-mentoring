## Lightfastness: general frame, history, environment
- Change in artworks is inevitable and usually irreversible. Cleaning or treatment can reverse a colour change but does not recreate the original pigment: "we are not turning back the clock" (p. 2, p. 15). → lightfastness
- Collections last best in stable environments: gradual, small changes of temperature and humidity, and minimal light exposure. London works stored in dark, air-conditioned quarries in WWII survived in unexpectedly good condition (p. 4). → lightfastness
- Preserving versus exhibiting is a trade-off, since the safest storage is inaccessible. In 1885-86 the National Gallery director argued against evening opening, citing polluted air, visitors' exhaled humidity and damp coats, and gas-lighting fumes (p. 4). → lightfastness
- Some artworks are especially sensitive to UV and bright daylight. In 1886 the perceived fading of watercolours at South Kensington led to a scientific committee. It tested pigments under high humidity, oxygen-free atmospheres and gas-lamp pollutants (p. 5). → lightfastness
- The resulting 1888 Russell and Abney report ranked pigments by relative permanence under varied conditions. It concluded that watercolours would show no visible change for about a century of display, "if painted with any but the most fugitive colours" (p. 5). → lightfastness
- Arthur Church's "Chemistry of Paints and Painting" (1890) listed permanent pigments and was widely read by colour-makers and artists (p. 6). The German Society for the Promotion of Rational Painting Techniques (founded 1872 by Keim) also issued lists of permanent pigments (p. 6). → lightfastness
- Current standards for artists' pigments specify testing them in a binding medium, because the binder affects permanence (p. 6). Lightfastness is therefore a property of the paint, not only of the pure pigment. → lightfastness
- Colorants react with water vapour, oxygen and volatile acids. They also undergo hydrolysis, thermal dissociation and photochemical decomposition, and they are sensitive to gaseous pollutants and biological action (p. 7). → lightfastness
- Sealing artworks in oxygen-free packages protects many pigments, but counterintuitively a few are more likely to change under those conditions (p. 8). → lightfastness
- Vitruvius already warned that vermilion blackens in sun and moonlight, and advised an oil-wax coating against damp air (pp. 8-9). → lightfastness
- Cennini (c. 1400) noted that saffron fades quickly. He also noted that verdigris and lead white interact badly, that red lead and vermilion tend to darken, and that some pigment-binder combinations are incompatible (p. 8). → lightfastness
- The text uses three effects as distinct outcomes: fading, darkening and discoloration. Painters expected all three to alter tonal balance (p. 8). → lightfastness
- Early modern workshop advice for limiting change included:
  - a final yellow lake glaze to unify colours that deepen with age (p. 9);
  - lead white ground in walnut or poppy oil rather than the quickly yellowing linseed oil (p. 9);
  - varnishing verdigris as soon as it is touch dry (p. 9);
  - using the purest pigments available (p. 9).
  → lightfastness
- Van Gogh wrote that heavy impasto and glazes applied only to dry paint last better, and doubted the lasting power of chromates and madder (p. 9). ⚠️ This is his anecdotal opinion, and the article stresses that painters' observations are anecdotal (p. 9). → lightfastness
- Despite being known as changeable, vermilion, orpiment, indigo and the red and yellow lakes were used widely. Nothing replaced the lakes, which artists prized for their range of colour and their translucency in oil even though they bleach quickly in light (p. 9). → lightfastness
- Verdigris darkens to an unpleasant brown but was indispensable in mixtures for greens (p. 9). → lightfastness
- Many nineteenth-century pigments (cadmium yellow, chrome yellow, most new synthetic organics) discolour or fade fairly fast (p. 10). → lightfastness
- Lighter, brighter chromate yellows discolour to brown and green in light (p. 10, fig. 6). → lightfastness
- Monet's "Rouen Cathedral, West Facade, Sunlight" (1894) has highlights that are now brown instead of yellow. The cadmium yellow used has discoloured (p. 10, fig. 7). → lightfastness

## Lightfastness: inorganic pigments, mechanisms
- Azurite and malachite are thermodynamically unstable under normal conditions (metastable). Kinetic barriers explain why they last, and damp conditions can slowly convert them towards black tenorite (CuO), which darkens blues (pp. 11-14). → lightfastness
- Oil or tempera binders appear to protect azurite from observable change. Darkening of azurite in paintings probably has other causes, possibly reaction with oil (pp. 11-12). → lightfastness
- Chloride salts migrating through masonry turn azurite into green hydroxy copper chlorides. An example is the blue-to-green Virgin's mantle at Malancrav. Conservators must distinguish altered pigments from intentionally used green copper minerals (p. 13, fig. 10). → lightfastness
- Lead white and red lead can darken to brown or black. In wall paintings the cause is plattnerite (PbO2). On paper and parchment it is galena (PbS) from sulfur in the air (p. 15). → lightfastness
- The Botticelli drawing's darkened white highlights (galena) were treated by oxidising them to white lead sulfate. The colour was restored, but not the original pigment (p. 15). → lightfastness
- Ions move through oil paint films, forming efflorescences such as cotunnite (PbCl2) and challacolloite on paintings and a Della Robbia terracotta. Paint is a dynamic system (p. 17). → lightfastness
- Many inorganic pigments are light-sensitive: vermilion, orpiment, chrome yellow, cadmium yellow. Impurities from raw materials or manufacture often act as catalysts of the change (p. 17). → lightfastness
- Vermilion (HgS) is light-sensitive. Halide ions (iodide, bromide, chloride) and excess thiosulfate from manufacture promote its darkening. Eibner proposed changing production to make a "permanent" vermilion (p. 22). → lightfastness
- In Antonello da Messina's Madonna and Child the vermilion has blackened only at the surface, and the orange-red remains inside the particles. The loss of light-shadow modelling in the drapery was compensated with a modern red lake glaze (p. 23, fig. 19). → lightfastness

## Lightfastness: lakes and fugitive organic colours
- Natural yellow lakes (buckthorn, weld, fustic, gamboge) fade and shift the greens and oranges made from them (p. 18). → lightfastness
- In Greuze's portrait of La Live de Jully (1759) the upholstery was originally green: ultramarine mixed with a yellow lake, probably stil de grain. The yellow faded, so it now looks blue. Chalk (the base of the lake) and residual yellow fluorescence in colourless particles were the evidence (p. 18, fig. 15). → lightfastness
- Red lakes mixed with blues give violets. When the red fades, the colour shifts towards blue (p. 20). → lightfastness
- Cennini warned against alum-mordanted kermes lake, which loses its colour quickly. Berrie's experiment on cochineal found that the alum-laked form fades fastest in light at neutral pH. The paper's pH changes the rate (p. 20, fig. 16). → lightfastness
- Flesh tones made from red lake, earth and lead white lose their warmth as the lake fades. It is uncertain whether Leonardo's Ginevra de' Benci was always pale or has lost her blush (p. 20). → lightfastness
- Winslow Homer used carmine, which has faded in many of his watercolours. A strip protected by a mat in "Casting, Number Two" still shows the original violet (p. 21, fig. 18). → lightfastness
- For artists and conservators, fading and darkening change the colour relationships, the mood and the time of day an artist intended. Knowing the changes lets scholars reconstruct the original intent (pp. 21, 8). → lightfastness
- Runge's colour-harmony paint-outs (1810) were damaged by pigment alteration, so their intended effect is lost (p. 24-25, fig. 20). → lightfastness
- Almost all pigments eventually discolour or disappear, and a yellow lake fades about as readily as charcoal is erased. The rate of change is constant, but the magnitude slows exponentially because less material remains. New works therefore change visibly, while old ones keep changing imperceptibly (pp. 24-25). → lightfastness
- Practical measures include:
  - limiting light levels for display and examination (p. 25);
  - keeping conditions stable and moderate (p. 25);
  - limiting heat, humidity and water, and watching for halide ions even at low concentrations (pp. 25-26).
  → lightfastness
- Conservation treatments (varnish removal, cleaning) can reveal deteriorated pigment beneath. Historical cleaning with abrasives, acids and lye also damaged paint (pp. 22-24). → lightfastness

## Paint composition: binder, pigment-binder interaction, layers
- Paint is pigment in a vehicle (egg yolk, gum, drying oil and so on). Binders can protect or harm, and components within the paint react with each other (p. 6). → paint-composition
- Metal ions from pigments react with fatty acids in oil to give metal carboxylates ("soaps"). The reaction is common in oil paintings but slow, and it was little recognised about 40 years ago (p. 6). → paint-composition
- Lead, zinc, copper, calcium and other metal soaps change surface texture, cause delamination, and in lead-containing paint form bumps and later craters filled with dirt and varnish (p. 7). → paint-composition
- Lead soaps gave elastic, more water-resistant paint films. A quote from Petit (via Stas) says white lead paint is not just powder mixed with oil, so other white powders cannot replace it (p. 17). → paint-composition
- Over centuries, soap molecules migrate and aggregate into pustules and holes. These were once misread as excess medium, oil-egg emulsion droplets, or added sand (p. 17). Berrie herself misidentified the bumps in a Dutch still life as lead-tin yellow (p. 6-7). → paint-composition
- Pigment chemistry measured in a beaker or from thermodynamic diagrams does not predict behaviour in paint. Examples are the odd lead carbonate phase plumbonacrite in oil paint and ion mobility. Conservators' experience is needed to interpret such cases (p. 15, p. 11). → paint-composition
- Impurities, additives, mixtures, binders and pigment purity affect stability and are factors outside present control (p. 25). → paint-composition
- Layers: Greuze's blue paint is a top layer over a preparation, and the cross-section shows chalk and ultramarine in the top paint layer (p. 18, fig. 15). The Giambono St. Peter has a repainted background identified by zinc white, which was used only after c. 1800 (p. 12). Early artists tried to prevent change with coatings (Vitruvius's oil-wax over vermilion) and varnish on verdigris (p. 9). → paint-composition
- Oil-resin varnishes used until the 19th century darken and become insoluble as they oxidise. Modern mastic and dammar varnishes are easier to remove (p. 23-24). → paint-composition

## Transparency and opacity
- As lead pigment transforms into soaps, paint becomes increasingly translucent and its colour diminishes (p. 7). → transparency-opacity
- Lake pigments were prized for translucency in oil and used in translucent glazes (pp. 9, 18). Van Gogh states that glazes of transparent colour can sink in and disappear if applied over insufficiently dry paint (p. 9). → transparency-opacity
- Lead soap aggregation can produce translucent pustules in the paint surface (p. 17). → transparency-opacity
