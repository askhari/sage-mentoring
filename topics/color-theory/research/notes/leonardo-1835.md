## Local colour vs perceived colour
- No object shows its true colour unless the light striking it is the same colour; light of a different colour from a neighbouring object changes it (Chap. CCXL, "No Object appears in its true Colour...") → local-vs-perceived-color
- Glossy, polished surfaces (leaves, grass) show their own colour least, since they mirror the colour of sun and sky; matte surfaces (cloth, woolly leaves) show it best, unless a neighbour casts a contrary colour on them, such as the red of the setting sun (Chap. CCXXIV, CCXXV) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Light is where the true quality of a colour is seen; in shadow a colour takes on the tinge of the shadow, so the painter should show the true colour only in the lit parts (Chap. CCXXXVIII, CCXXXV) → local-vs-perceived-color
- The truest colour of an object is in the parts free of shadow and of gloss (Chap. CCXLVIII, "General Remarks") → local-vs-perceived-color
- Every opaque surface takes on part of the colour of the objects around it; the nearer and the stronger the neighbour, the more (Chap. CCLXXIV, CCCXLII; CCXLVII, "The Surface of all opake Bodies participates...") → local-vs-perceived-color
- The air (any transparent medium) between eye and object tints the object's surface with its own colour, more so with density and thickness (Chap. CCVIII; CCCXLII) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Colours seen in a dim space lose their variety: the darker the shadow, the less the colours differ from one another (example: pictures seen through a church door from a bright square) (Chap. CCXLII; CCXLIII on total darkness removing all colour) → local-vs-perceived-color
- White is "not a colour" but a receiver of all colours; a white surface takes up the colour of its surroundings, so it is the most variable surface (Chap. CCXLIV; CCLIII) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Coloured glass trick: look at the landscape through coloured glasses to see how each colour is altered or improved by an overlying colour (Chap. CCXXVII) → judging-color
- Fire (candle) light tints everything reddish yellow; the difference is hard to notice unless compared against daylight, for instance in a dark room with a little daylight and a candle; yellow light turns blue toward green (Chap. CCXLV) → colored-light-unity
- Surrounding colours alter how a flesh tone looks: black drapery makes flesh look whiter, white makes it darker, yellow makes it more colourful, red makes it paler (Chap. CCLXXXI, "Of the Nature of Comparison") → simultaneous-contrast
- Colours "appear what they are not" depending on the ground: flesh looks pale on red, a pale colour looks redder on yellow (Chap. CCLV) → simultaneous-contrast
- No colour looks uniform across its extent unless it ends on a ground of its own colour; black against white looks stronger at the edges than in the middle (Chap. CCLXIX) → simultaneous-contrast

## Light and shadow: colour of lights, shadows and reflexes
- The colour of a shadow is never pure unless the body next to it has the same colour; example: a blue figure in a green room has a bright blue lit side but a dingy shadow, spoilt by green reflexions from the wall, and worse with brown walls (Chap. CCXLI, "Of the Colour of Shadows") → light-and-shadow-color
- Shadows of a colour take on that colour more or less according to their nearness to the mass of shadow or the mass of light (Chap. CCLI) → light-and-shadow-color
- The shadow of a white body lit by sun or sky should have a bluish cast, because white takes the colour of the air around it (Chap. CCLII; CCXLIV) → light-and-shadow-color
- At sunset the shadow cast on a white wall is blue: the lit part gets the red of the sun, the shadowed part receives only the sky's blue (Chap. CCLVIII) → light-and-shadow-color
- Lights and shadows of one uniformly coloured object can differ in hue (greenish shadow, reddish light) when a differently coloured body on the other side reflects light into the shadow; white snow mountains at sunset have red lights and bluish shades (Chap. CCLXI) → light-and-shadow-color
- In open country under sun, shadows are dark and sharp and take a hue from the azure of the air, especially on white objects; the sunlit side takes the colour of the sun (strongly red at sunset); the painter should know the cause so the effect looks right ("or else the work will be false and absurd") (Chap. CCLXII) → light-and-shadow-color
- Reflexes take on the colour of the object that casts them and also of the object they fall on, more so when the receiving surface is smoother; a reflected colour is never simple but a mixture of everything reflected there (Chap. CCLXXV, CCLXXVII) → light-and-shadow-color
- Worked example: yellow body, blue reflector gives a green reflexion on the yellow body (Chap. CCLXXVI, CCLXXIX); the same explanation is used for blue + yellow on a white surface (Chap. CCLXXVII). ⚠️ Leonardo reasons as if reflected colours mix like pigments; the modern view would call this a mix of light and surface colour, and the result is not simply "blue + yellow = green". → light-and-shadow-color
- Reflected colours are weaker than directly lit ones; the nearest colour tints a surface most; a reflex from a variety of different coloured bodies is confused (Chap. CCLXXIII, CCLXXIV, CCLXXXII) → light-and-shadow-color
- Reflexes on flesh from other flesh are redder and livelier than any other part of the figure; the painter should mix a little of the nearest drapery colour into reflexes on flesh, without pronouncing it too much unless there is cause (Chap. CCLXXX, CCLXXXII) → light-and-shadow-color
- Reflexes show more against a darker ground and little against a lighter one (Chap. CCXV, CCXVII, CCXIX) → light-and-shadow-color
- A reflected light should be admitted only when its cause is visible to everyone; he describes the practice as divided among painters and advises a middle course (Chap. CCXVI) → light-and-shadow-color
- Reflexes are brightest where the reflecting surface is nearest to the source and the reflection meets at nearly equal angles (Chap. CCXVIII) → light-and-shadow-color
- Original light comes from sun, fire or sky; derivative light is reflected; dark, rough surfaces (grass, shrubs, roofs) give no reflection (Chap. CCXIV) → light-and-shadow-color
- Each colour is at its best in a particular tonal zone: black in the shades, white in the strongest light, blue and green in the half-tint, yellow and red in the principal light, gold in the reflexes, lake in the half-tint (Chap. CCXXXIV). ⚠️ Studio-practice opinion, not an optical rule. → light-and-shadow-color
- Shadow terminates in a half-shadow that blends with the light; the shadow mixes more with the light the farther it is from the casting object (Chap. CCLVII) → light-and-shadow-color
- A small light casts large, sharp shadows; a large light casts small, blurred ones (Chap. CXCII); under thin cloud shadow edges disappear and become soft (Chap. CXC) → light-and-shadow-color
- Shadow shading cannot be copied by tracing as outlines can; there are endless gradations that blend without a precise edge (Chap. CLXXVII); light and shadow ("chiaro-scuro") are the painter's first aim, and Leonardo scorns painters who avoid shadows for the sake of "fine colours" (Chap. CLXXVIII; CCLXX) → light-and-shadow-color
- In a uniform flat shaded body, two kinds of shadow arise: the darkest is the one pointing towards the centre of the sun, as the object also gets general sky light (Chap. CCCXLII, near end) → light-and-shadow-color
- Spoon-measure method: to paint a blue object between a white and a black wall, use the pure light blue for the lightest part and 3 spoons of black to 1 of blue for the darkest shade, with a graded transition between (Chap. CCXLVII) → light-and-shadow-color
- Shadows fall off by distance: nearby objects show their shadows darker; distant ones are deadened by intervening air (Chap. CCLVI) → atmospheric-perspective

## Coloured light and unity of the scene
- Colour of light by situation: sunshine gives dark shades and broad lights; gloomy day gives little difference of light and shade and no cast shadows; indoors with a paper blind and white walls gives the same as a gloomy day; fire light gives red powerful lights and dark precise shadows; mixed sky and fire gives a bright sky-lit side and a reddish fire-lit side (Chap. CXCIII) → colored-light-unity
- Choose the light of the picture deliberately; an open-country scene under overcast has much general light, whereas sun gives dark sharp shadows and objects take the colours of sun and of red clouds (Chap. CCLXII) → colored-light-unity
- Error to avoid: studies drawn at home under one particular light and then used in an open-country picture with general sky light, giving dark shadows and reflexes where nature would give none (Chap. CLXXXV) → colored-light-unity
- At dusk or sunset, everything is of one colour except what the sun lights; buildings and treetops in sunlight take on yellow-red, while the rest merges in a "tender and general mass"; the painter should reuse the colour painted for the sun on all lit parts (Chap. CCCXLII, near end) → colored-light-unity
- When the sun reddens the horizon clouds, distant bluish objects pick up the redness, making a pleasing mixture of blue and red; the air itself takes on the colour (Chap. CCCXLII) → colored-light-unity
- Sun beams through cloud gaps tint with their own colour the dark places behind them (Chap. CCCXXXII) → colored-light-unity
- For drapery colours: they should vary to differentiate, but for harmony be of one nature, not stick together, and vary in light according to distance and the air between them (Chap. CCLXXII) → colored-light-unity
- Open, general light gives the most grace; works painted in it also look pleasing at a distance (Chap. CCVII) → colored-light-unity
- A landscape picture will never match the vivacity of a sunlit landscape unless the picture itself is hung in the same sunlight (Chap. CCCXLIII) → judging-color
- Fog, rain and smoke, mist: objects lose definition, those near are more distinct; seasons and climate change the greens and other colours of trees, meadows, and so on, so do not paint all trees the same green (Chap. CCCXXX, CCCXXXIII, CCCXXXIV, CCCXXXV) → colored-light-unity
- The sea is dark seen from shore (reflecting the earth), blue far out at sea (reflecting the azure of the air); water cannot be lighter or darker than the neighbouring objects it reflects (Chap. CCCXXVIII, CCCXXXIX) → local-vs-perceived-color

## Atmospheric / aerial perspective
- Perspective in painting has three parts: diminution of size, diminution of colour, and diminution of definition with distance (Chap. CCCIII) → atmospheric-perspective
- Linear perspective alone cannot give a complete sense of distance without the perspective of colours (Chap. CCLXXXIII); when light and shadow cannot be distinguished because of the air, one can drop perspective of shadows and use linear perspective with diminished colours (same chapter) → atmospheric-perspective
- Procedure for aerial perspective: assume a somewhat thick air; paint the nearest building in its own colour, each farther one less distinct in outline and bluer, e.g. five times farther means five times more azure (Chap. CCCIV) → atmospheric-perspective
- Distant things take the colour of the air in proportion to the air between them and the eye (two miles of air tints more than one mile) (Chap. CCC; CCLXXXV; CCLXXXIX) → atmospheric-perspective
- The darkest objects (natural dark, or dark from shadow) appear the bluest when distant; dark mountains look bluest, and green fields turn blue sooner than yellow, white, or red; the more a colour differs from black, the farther it keeps its identity (Chap. CCXC, CCXCVI, CCCI) → atmospheric-perspective
- Objects darker than the air get lighter with distance, objects lighter than the air get darker; both are "discoloured" toward the air's value (Chap. CCLXXXVII; CCCXXIII) → atmospheric-perspective
- A pure white keeps visible the farthest; darker colours bear distance less well (Chap. CCXXXVI, CCXLVIII) → atmospheric-perspective
- Order of loss with distance: gloss first, then the lights, then the principal shadows, leaving a middling obscurity; small parts vanish first, then the lines that bound members, so distant objects are painted with unfinished detail and soft outlines (Chap. CCXCI, CCCV, CCCVI, CCCXIV, CCCXV, CCLXIV) → atmospheric-perspective
- Faces and other detailed objects get darker at moderate distance because the lights are small and are lost, leaving only shadows blended into a general half-tint; trees look darker than near by; then the air lightens them by tinging the shades azure (Chap. CCII, CCLIX) → atmospheric-perspective
- Distant outlines are blurred for two reasons: small visual angle, and the air that, like a veil, tints shadows with whiteness toward azure (Chap. CCCXLII) → atmospheric-perspective
- Air is thicker, whiter and lighter near the ground and thinner, darker and bluer higher up; the sky is whitest at the horizon; so lower parts of distant mountains and towers are paler and more confused than the tops (Chap. CCLXXXIV, CCCVII, CCCVIII, CCCXI-CCCXIII, CCCXIX, CCCXXIV) → atmospheric-perspective
- In fog, the sunlit parts are the most distinct, while the unlit remain confused and nearly the colour of the fog; objects in fog seem larger and bases look narrower than tops (Chap. CCCXVII, CCCXVIII, CCCXX) → atmospheric-perspective
- Same colour at levels at different distances: change is proportional to distance when at equal elevation (Chap. CCXCIII); a colour can stay unchanged when greater distance is balanced by thinner air at greater height (Chap. CCXCVII, CCXCVIII) → atmospheric-perspective
- Local colours are completely lost at a distance that depends on eye and object elevation, the hour of the day and the purity of the air (Chap. CCLXXXVIII) → atmospheric-perspective
- Colours in dim places dissolve with distance as the light dwindles to darkness (Chap. CCXCIV) → atmospheric-perspective
- The foremost colours should be pure and simple; those farther away take on the colour of the horizon in proportion to distance (Chap. CCXCV; also CCLXXXVI colours vanish as objects shrink) → atmospheric-perspective
- Many painters wrongly make distant objects simply darker; this is true only of white objects (Chap. CCXCIX); trees can look darker in the distance only if they are clumped so that shadows accumulate (Chap. CCC) → atmospheric-perspective
- Nearby: bold, determined finish; distant: unfinished, confused outlines (Chap. CCCIX); strong outlines on distant objects make them look near (Chap. CCCV, CCCXLII) → atmospheric-perspective
- Pulled-up sources of colour: at distance, only the figures that hold sun light are visible; at sunset, the shadowed parts are "confused and undetermined" (Chap. CCCXLII) → atmospheric-perspective
- Practical exercise: pick trees at intervals of about 65 yards, trace each on a fixed glass with one eye, compare with the real tree, and colour the copy to match; the colour and size changes with distance are then recorded for the picture's background (Chap. CCCXLI, "How a Painter ought to put in Practice the Perspective of Colours") → atmospheric-perspective
- ⚠️ Causes given for the sky's blue ("light and darkness" mixing, air itself colourless, lit moisture particles over dark depth) (Chap. CCXCII, CCCIII, CCLVII, CCXLVIII): modern optics attributes blue to scattering of sunlight by air molecules; blue is not a black-white mix. The painting-practice consequence (distance bluer, whiter near horizon) is useful; the explanation is not reliable. → atmospheric-perspective
- ⚠️ Leonardo says faces look dark at a distance because of the "dark tube" of the eye (Chap. CCCXVI); the other explanation he gave (lights lost, shadows dominate, Chap. CCLIX) is more defensible. → atmospheric-perspective
- Cause of "bluish" distant colours also stated as part of the "propositions" cited throughout (e.g. Chap. CCCXXVII "blue is composed of black and white seen at a great distance"); ⚠️ same objection as above → atmospheric-perspective

## Judging colour while painting
- Use a flat mirror as "master": compare the picture with the model reflected in it, looking with one eye only (Chap. CCCL, "In what Manner the Mirror is the true Master of Painters") → judging-color
- Looking at one's own work in a mirror makes it look like the work of another and shows the faults; also step away from the work often to rest, so that judgment is clearer on return (Chap. CCCLV, "How to judge of one's own Work") → judging-color
- We spot errors in others' work more easily than our own; our own judgment is the most deceptive, and enemies' criticisms help more than friends' (Chap. CCCLV, CCCLVIII) → judging-color
- Judgment should outpace the work (Chap. CCCLXIII, CCCLXIV); the painter should consult nature directly and not trust memory (Chap. CCCLXV) → judging-color
- Painting is best judged by how closely it matches what it imitates (Chap. CCCLI) → judging-color
- Studio light: window open to the sky, walls painted a reddish colour (for flesh), best in summer when clouds hide the sun, so that reflected sun does not destroy shadows (Chap. CCXLIX); window of oiled paper, no cross-bars, slanted reveals painted black (Chap. CCL) → judging-color
- Constant light: a north window keeps lights from varying; with a south window use paper blinds so the sun's movement does not change the shadows (Chap. CLXXXVI); light from on high and not too strong shows the parts best (Chap. CLXXXIII); candle light with a paper blind makes soft shadows (Chap. CLXXXIV) → judging-color
- Painter's position: slightly to the side, between the window and the model, so that the model is seen partly in light and partly in shadow (Chap. CLXXXII) → judging-color
- To find the right shadow for a flesh colour, hold a finger close to the picture so it casts a shadow, and match the shadow, nearer for lighter and farther for darker (Chap. CCLX) → judging-color
- Compare lights and shadows carefully: note which lights are first in brightness and which shadows are darkest, and compare their quality and quantity (Chap. CLXXXI) → judging-color
- For judging a finished painting, the order is: relief of figures according to light, then composition fitting the subject, then life and expression (Chap. CCCLII) → judging-color
- Viewing a picture: stand away from the spot where window light reflects on its surface (especially oil or varnish), nearer the side of the light source (Chap. CCCLVII, "The best Place for looking at a Picture") → judging-color
- The painter has some colours lighter than the lightest part of the model and darker than its deepest shade, so reproduce what the mirror shows (Chap. CCCL) → judging-color
- ⚠️ Mirror-as-judge and one-eye advice rests on his claim that two-eye vision shows more "relief" than a painting can (Chap. CCCXLVIII); the idea is useful for flattening a scene, but the explanation is only partly relevant to colour. → judging-color
- Snow seen against the sky looks darker than against a window onto a dark room, where it looks very white (Chap. CCXII) → judging-color

## Simultaneous contrast (contrast chapters)
- White looks whiter on a dark ground and darker on a light ground; shadow looks darkest next to the brightest white (Chap. CCXII; CCXLVIII; CCCI) → simultaneous-contrast
- Of colours of equal perfection, the most excellent is the one seen near its direct contrary: pale against red, black against white, blue near yellow, green near red (Chap. CCXLVIII, "General Remarks") → simultaneous-contrast
- For great darkness use great light beside it and vice versa; pale yellow makes red more beautiful than purple does; green beside red gives grace, but beside blue does not; blue with pale yellow or white is harmonious (Chap. CCLXXI, "How to assort Colours...") → simultaneous-contrast
- Objects most different in colour detach most from one another; a ground of the same colour and light merges with the object, so avoid it (Chap. CCCXLII; CCLXV, CCLXVII) → simultaneous-contrast
- For detaching figures: light parts against a dark ground and dark parts against a light ground; "all contraries" give force through opposition (Chap. CXCIX, CC, CCIX, CCX) → simultaneous-contrast
- A convex body on a ground of the same colour will show a darker or lighter edge, because convex edges get different light than the ground (Chap. CXCVII, CCV, CCLXV, CCCXLII) → simultaneous-contrast
- Two objects equally dark and equally distant: those against a lighter ground look darker (Chap. CCCIII, CCCXXII) → simultaneous-contrast

## Gamut and palette planning (little material)
- Leonardo lists the simple colours Nature produces as eight: black, white, blue, yellow, green, tawny/umber, purple, red; for painters' purposes he treats white, yellow, green, blue, red, black as the six simple ones, with white for light, black for darkness (Chap. CCXXVI, CCXXVII). ⚠️ This is a historical elemental scheme (white=light, yellow=earth, green=water, blue=air, red=fire, black=darkness) and not a modern colour model. → gamut-planning
- He proposes a systematic mixing approach (each simple colour mixed one with one, two with two, and so on) as a basis, promising a separate treatise on it (Chap. CCXXVI) → gamut-planning
- Clothe figures in the brightest and most lively colours so they detach at a distance; dull colours are obscured in the shades and show little difference between light and dark (Chap. CCCIII) → gamut-planning
- Vary the greens and other natural colours of meadows, trees and stones instead of using one green for every tree (Chap. CCCXXXIV) → gamut-planning
- Coloured glass mixture trial: use one or two or three glasses over a view and pick the new and pleasant colour combinations (Chap. CCXXVII) → gamut-planning
- Transparent colour over another gives a mixed colour (lake on blue gives violet, yellow on blue gives green, saffron on white gives yellow, white scumbled over a dark gives blue) (Chap. CCXXXIII); and the glow of transparent colours is best on a pure white ground, while opaque colours do not care what ground they have (Chap. CCXXXII) → gamut-planning
- Gradation warning: he rebukes painters so in love with beautiful colours that they resist giving them near-imperceptible shadows (Chap. CCLXX) → gamut-planning
