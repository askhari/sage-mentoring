## lightfastness
- ASTM International's subcommittee D01.57 on Artist Paints and Related Materials wrote standards for labelling, composition, physical properties, performance and test methods (p. 1). → lightfastness
- Three lightfastness standards are named: D5383 (visual determination by art technologists), D5398 (visual evaluation by the user) and D4303 (test methods for lightfastness of colorants in artists' materials) (p. 1). → lightfastness
- D4303 offers four accelerating methods: A natural daylight through glass; B daylight fluorescent lamps; C xenon arc simulating daylight through glass; D cool-white fluorescent plus UV sunlamps with soda-lime glass filtering (p. 1). → lightfastness
- Categories come from the colour difference before and after exposure measured by spectrophotometer, in ΔE*ab in L*a*b* space (p. 1). → lightfastness
- Bands: I Excellent, mean change under 4; II Very Good, over 4.0 up to 8.0; III Fair, over 8.0 up to 16.0 (p. 1); IV Poor, over 16.0 up to 24.0; V Very Poor, over 24.0 (p. 2). → lightfastness
- Some manufacturers use "permanence" wording or proprietary scales; do not assume they match ASTM unless stated (p. 2). → lightfastness
- Example three-category permanence scale: I no appreciable change after the equivalent of about 100 years of indoor museum light, suitable outdoors; II suited to indoor use only; III fugitive, not for permanent work (p. 2). → lightfastness
- A pigment's lightfastness can change with the vehicle: Vermilion is I in oil and acrylic but III in watercolour, so check the rating for the specific brand and medium (p. 2). → lightfastness
- Watercolour is generally more light-sensitive than other paints with the same pigments, partly because oil and acrylic binders give slight protection and watercolour is applied thinly (p. 4). → lightfastness
- Pastel lightfastness depends mainly on its pigments since it has little binder; no ASTM pastel standard yet when written (pp. 3-4). Colored pencils have their own standard, D6901 (p. 4). → lightfastness
- Home test 1: expose samples to daylight for months with a control kept dark (p. 3). → lightfastness
- Home test 2 (blue wool): expose dyed blue wool cards of known fastness next to samples; 1 very poor, 2-3 poor, 4-5 fair, 6 very good, 7-8 excellent; when a sample first shows visible change, compare with the wool step that changed to assign a rating. D5398 uses blue wool cards (p. 3). → lightfastness
- Lab testing is more accurate than home testing, which is still worthwhile or can verify a manufacturer's rating (pp. 2-3). → lightfastness
- Compliance with ASTM quality standards is voluntary; D4236 (hazard labelling and general product labelling) is legally required (p. 3). → lightfastness

## colour-index-names
- A buyer should check the tube/jar label for generic pigment names, certification to a standard, and a lightfastness rating (p. 2). → colour-index-names
- A product conforming to D4236 carries a conformance number for artists' quality plus the common colour name, pigment name, lightfastness rating, health certification and warnings (p. 3). → colour-index-names
- The fact sheet does not name the Colour Index (PB 29 etc.) specifically; "generic pigment name" is its wording. → colour-index-names

## paint-composition
- ASTM standards cover testing of pigments, vehicles and additives (p. 1). → paint-composition
- Binders or proprietary additives can affect the purity and stability of charcoal and graphite (p. 3). → paint-composition
- Traditional ink is carbon black with water and a binder such as gelatin or shellac; pigment inks are more lightfast than dye inks (p. 4). → paint-composition
- Of the standards requested: D4236 is described (p. 3); D4302 and D5067 are not mentioned. D5098 is not mentioned. → paint-composition
