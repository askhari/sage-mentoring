## Single-colour preference (harmony-research)
- Early researchers dismissed adult single-colour preferences as unsystematic. Modern studies with standardized colours and better statistics show reliable group patterns by hue, saturation and lightness, despite large individual differences (p. 84, §4.1) → harmony-research
- Hue: among American and British adults, preference follows a smooth curve with a maximum at blue and a minimum around yellow to yellow-green. Most of the variance lies on the blue-yellow axis, little on red-green (p. 84, §4.1 "Hue") → harmony-research
- Saturation: Western adults generally prefer more saturated colours for context-free patches. Some findings show a drop for colours that are "too vivid". The effect varies with gender, culture and object context (p. 85, §4.1) → harmony-research
- Lightness: preference tends to rise with lightness up to a point, but the effect is not always found. Lightness is confounded with saturation, so each hue peaks at a different lightness (yellow light, red and green medium, blue and purple dark) (p. 85) → harmony-research
- Hue x lightness interaction: dark oranges and yellows (browns, olives) are strongly disliked compared with lighter versions of the same hues and with dark red. Classic colour-appearance models struggle to explain this, but ecological accounts predict it (p. 85) → harmony-research
- Figure 1 (p. 85) plots the Berkeley Color Project data: 8 hues at 4 saturation/lightness levels (saturated, light, muted, dark) → harmony-research

## Theories of single-colour preference (color-meaning)
- Four classes of explanation were tested against the same data: physiological (cone contrast), psychophysical (colour appearance), emotional, and ecological (pp. 85-86, §4.1.1) → color-meaning
- Cone-contrast model: even the augmented version explained only 37% of the variance in the Berkeley data (pp. 85-86) → color-meaning
- Colour-appearance model: weighted redness-greenness, blueness-yellowness, saturation and lightness explained 60% of the variance (p. 86) → color-meaning
- Colour-emotion model (Ou et al. 2004): three dimensions predict preference. Active, light and cool colours are preferred over passive, heavy and warm ones. Fitted to the Berkeley data it explained 55% (p. 86) → color-meaning
- Weakness of the emotion account: it does not explain where colour emotions come from. Happy is tied to less-liked hues (yellow) and sad to more-liked hues (blue) (p. 86) → color-meaning
- Ecological valence theory (EVT), Palmer & Schloss 2010: people like a colour to the degree that they like all the objects associated with it. Example: blues and cyans are liked partly because of clear sky and clean water, and browns and olives are disliked partly because of faeces and rotting food (p. 86) → color-meaning
- EVT test: the "weighted affective valence estimate" (WAVE) is the average liking of a colour's associated objects, weighted by colour similarity. It explained 80% of the variance in Figure 1 with no fitted parameters (p. 86) → color-meaning
- EVT prediction confirmed (Strauss et al. 2012): viewing positive red and negative green images (or the reverse) shifted preference for those colours in the predicted direction (pp. 86-87) → color-meaning
- EVT prediction confirmed (Schloss et al. 2011): Berkeley and Stanford students preferred their own university's colours, in proportion to their school spirit. Institutional affect influences colour liking (p. 87) → color-meaning
- Knowledge matters even for simple colour preference, because it depends on stored affective responses to the objects typically associated with each colour (p. 100, §7.5) → color-meaning

## Infants, gender, culture, objects
- Infants (about 3-6 months) have a hue-preference curve similar to adults' for saturated colours (peak around blue, minimum around yellow or yellow-green). This led some to think preference is innate (p. 87, §4.1.2) → color-meaning
- Later infant studies undercut innateness. Infant preferences vary mainly on the red to blue-green dimension, and the hue x lightness pattern is nearly the opposite of adults' (r = -0.46). Colour preference changes across the lifespan (p. 87) → color-meaning
- Gender: Western men prefer saturated colours more than women do. This correlates r = +0.73 with how "active" the colours are judged. The difference is absent in children aged 6-9 and clear by 17-18 (p. 87, §4.1.3) → color-meaning
- Girls aged 3-12 tend to prefer pink and purple, and boys red and blue. The authors link this to the stereotyped colours of toys, which fits EVT (pp. 87-88) → color-meaning
- Male WAVEs predicted male preferences better than female preferences, and the reverse for female WAVEs (Taylor & Franklin 2012) (p. 88) → color-meaning
- Cross-culturally, the strongest candidate for a universal is a liking for bluish colours, with exceptions such as Kuwait. A second candidate is a dislike of dark yellow (olive), found in Chinese, British, Japanese and American samples. The authors suggest this may reflect shared ecology (sky and water vs waste and rot) rather than innate preference (p. 88, §4.1.4) → color-meaning
- Claims of universal colour preference (e.g. Birren 1961, Eysenck 1941) are contradicted by modern research, which finds both similarities and differences (p. 88) → color-meaning
- Culture-specific WAVEs predicted own-culture preferences better than the other culture's, for Japanese vs American samples. This evidence is described as preliminary (p. 88) → color-meaning
- The Himba of rural Namibia strongly preferred highly saturated colours regardless of hue. The authors attribute this to a psychophysical effect and/or novelty in a less saturated natural environment (p. 88) → color-meaning
- Colour symbolism is proposed to explain cultural differences: white is preferred more in Japan, Korea and Taiwan (purity, cleanliness), and red more in China than Britain (good luck). The authors note that solid empirical evidence for symbolism driving preference is largely lacking (pp. 88-89) → color-meaning
- Object-based preferences: for artifacts that could be any colour (walls, couches, shirts and so on), hue preferences resemble those for squares, with cool and blue liked more than warm. The main exception is that people dislike large objects such as walls and couches in red (p. 89, §4.1.5) → color-temperature
- Lightness preferences vary by object for practical reasons (for example, lighter walls to make rooms look brighter or bigger). Context-free squares were the only case where highly saturated colours were preferred over less saturated ones (p. 89) → color-meaning
- Conventions override generic preferences: luxury sedans are preferred in achromatic colours (formal, serious) and VW Beetles in bright saturated colours including yellow (fun). A blue banana is the extreme case of prototypical colour (p. 89) → color-meaning

## Warm and cool
- Cool colours (green, cyan, blue) are generally preferred to warm ones (red, orange, yellow) among American and British adults (p. 84) → color-temperature
- In Ou et al.'s colour-emotion factors, cool-warm is one of three dimensions predicting preference, with cool preferred (p. 86) → color-temperature
- The cool-over-warm preference holds for coloured artifacts such as walls and clothing (p. 89) → color-temperature
- The paper does not discuss warm/cool as spatial advance/recede or as a means of mixing temperature in painting. Its only treatment is as a hue-preference and colour-emotion dimension → color-temperature

## Colour combinations: preference vs harmony (harmony-research)
- Historical colour-combination theories rest on untested assumptions. They use "preference" and "harmony" interchangeably and confuse preference for a pair with preference for a figural colour against a background (p. 89, §4.2) → harmony-research
- Schloss & Palmer 2011 separate three judgments for figure-ground pairs: pair preference (how much the two colours are liked together), pair harmony (how well they go together, regardless of liking), and figural preference (how much the foreground colour is liked against its background) (p. 89) → harmony-research
- Analogy: Mozart is judged more harmonious than Stravinsky, yet people differ in which they prefer (p. 89) → harmony-research
- Figural preference matters for combinations because of simultaneous colour contrast, in which the same colour looks different on different backgrounds (Chevreul, da Vinci, Helmholtz) (p. 89) → dominance-and-accent
- Classic theories of harmony (p. 90, §4.2.1):
  - Chevreul (1839) proposed two types, harmony of analogous colours and harmony of contrast.
  - Itten (1973) held that colours are harmonious if they mix to neutral gray as paints.
  - Munsell (1921) and Ostwald (1932) held that harmony comes from regular relations in colour space (for example, constant hue and saturation with varying lightness).
  - Goethe and Moon & Spencer also proposed theories. → harmony-research
- None of these classic theories was formulated from aesthetic measurements, although some have since been tested (p. 90) → harmony-research
- Empirical findings conflict, mostly because the three judgments were not distinguished. Granger found harmony and preference rising with hue contrast, but several later studies find both decreasing with hue contrast (p. 90, §4.2.2) → harmony-research
- Schloss & Palmer 2011: both pair preference and pair harmony increased with hue similarity. This supports Chevreul's analogous harmony (p. 90) → color-schemes
- Pair preference increased with lightness contrast, but pair harmony did not. Harmony was also higher for lighter pairs and for pairs more similar in saturation (p. 90) → harmony-research
- Pair preference and pair harmony correlate strongly on average (r = +0.79), which explains why people equate them. Agreement between observers was higher for harmony (r = 0.51) than for preference (r = 0.36), because harmony was almost independent of how much the two single colours were liked (p. 90) → harmony-research
- Over 80% of the variance in pair preference was explained by a linear combination of pair harmony, liking of the two colours alone, and figure-ground lightness difference (multiple r = 0.89) (p. 90) → harmony-research
- Figural preference correlated r = 0.87 with preference for the figure colour on a neutral background and r = 0.74 with average pair preference. After removing these, hue contrast and lightness contrast raised figural preference (p. 90) → color-schemes
- The authors suggest this effect explains Chevreul's "harmony of contrast". Contrast effects raise the figure colour's perceived saturation, and saturation is generally liked (pp. 90-91) → color-schemes
- ⚠️ Summary for schemes: this review gives no data on triadic, split-complementary or tetradic schemes. It speaks only of analogous (hue-similar) vs contrasting-hue pairs, and the data cover two-colour pairs only → color-schemes

## Figure/ground, area, weight (dominance-and-accent)
- The preference-vs-harmony work used a small square on a larger square, so figure and ground are asymmetric. Figural preference is a separate judgment from pair preference (pp. 89-90) → dominance-and-accent
- Colour affects the perceived "weight" or balance of a composition. Darker colours behave as heavier (Bullough 1907). For squares, red influenced balance most and blue least (p. 93, §5.3.2) → dominance-and-accent
- In Mondrian studies (Locher et al. 2005), perceived weight depended on region size and colour: red heaviest, blue intermediate, yellow lightest. The original paintings were judged more balanced than colour-swapped variants (p. 93) → dominance-and-accent
- In photo cropping (McManus et al. 2011), low-level features such as colour had little effect on choices, and meaning-based features dominated (p. 95, §5.3.3) → dominance-and-accent
- ⚠️ The paper has no study of dominant, subordinate or accent colours by area proportion. Only figure/ground pairs and weight/balance are covered → dominance-and-accent

## Individual differences and training (harmony-research)
- Individual models fit well: Ling & Hurlbert's four-parameter cone model accounted for individuals' colour preferences (mean r = 0.71). Each observer's own WAVEs correlated better with their own preferences (r = 0.55) than with others' (r = 0.40) (p. 96, §6) → harmony-research
- The correlation between pair preference and pair harmony ranged across individuals from about 0 to +0.75. It followed an inverted-U with formal colour training: highest at intermediate training and lower for both untrained and highly trained people (p. 96) → harmony-research
- Palmer & Griscom 2012: "preference for harmony" is an individual trait that holds across colour pairs, shapes, composition and music. Some people dislike harmonious stimuli, and specific art or music training lowers preference for harmony in that domain (pp. 96-97) → harmony-research
- Harmony is characterized there as simplicity, regularity and parts that fit well together (p. 96) → harmony-research
- Preference for the most prototypical colour examples is documented (Martindale & Moore 1988) (pp. 92, 98) → color-meaning
- Berlyne's arousal theory predicts an inverted-U between pleasure and arousal. The authors report that some of its key predictions have not been supported (pp. 97-98) → harmony-research
- Fluency theory (preference tracks ease of processing) has trouble explaining single-colour preferences, given the strong evidence of links to ecological objects (p. 99) → color-meaning
- Concluding remarks: Mondrian grid paintings and Albers's Homage to the Square are suggested as a bridge between colour-preference findings and fine art. The authors note that this research covers mainly formal characteristics, not emotion and meaning (p. 101, §8) → harmony-research

## Not covered / gaps
- Concepts requested but covered only thinly: color-temperature (no warm/cool in the painter's sense), color-schemes (only analogous vs contrast pairs), dominance-and-accent (no proportion-of-area or accent studies).
- Colour-emotion is covered only as three rating dimensions (active-passive, light-heavy, cool-warm) and the happy/sad problem. There is no list of specific colour-emotion pairs.
