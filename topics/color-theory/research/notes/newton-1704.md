## Circle construction (Book I, Part II, Prop. VI, Prob. II)

- Prop. VI Prob. II gives a method for finding the colour of a mixture of known amounts of the primary colours. Newton draws a circle and divides its circumference into seven arcs, one per prismatic colour, with lengths proportional to the seven musical tones (the octave intervals). The colours are red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet. (Book I, Pt II, Prop. VI) → color-wheel
- The centre of the circle (O) is the white point. The mixture's colour is read from the weighted position of the colours in it. (Prop. VI) → color-wheel, saturation-and-colorfulness
- Procedure: mark the centre of gravity of each arc, draw around it a small circle proportional to the number of rays of that colour in the mixture, then find the common centre of gravity Z of all those circles. (Prop. VI) → color-wheel, additive-mixing
- The line from O through Z, extended to the circumference at Y, tells the colour: the hue is the arc where Y falls, and nearer an end it leans toward the neighbouring colour. Hue is thus named by angular position on the circle. (Prop. VI) → hue, color-wheel
- The length OZ is proportional to the "Fulness or Intenseness" of the colour. If Z lies on the circumference, the colour is intense and florid to the highest degree. As Z approaches O the colour weakens toward white. (Prop. VI) → saturation-and-colorfulness
- Newton's example for intensity: a Z about halfway between rim and centre corresponds to a colour like the most intense yellow diluted with an equal quantity of whiteness. (Prop. VI) → saturation-and-colorfulness
- Worked example: violet 1, indigo 1, blue 2, green 3, yellow 5, orange 6, red 10 gives an orange leaning slightly to red, with a little under half the saturation of a pure orange. (Prop. VI) → color-wheel, hue
- If Z falls near the chord joining the red and violet ends, the result is not a prismatic colour but a purple. So purples have no place among the spectral arcs; they come from mixing the two ends. (Prop. VI) → color-wheel, hue

## What the circle predicts and its limits

- Two opposite colours mixed in suitable proportion put Z at or near O, so the model predicts a white or whitish result. Newton says in practice he never managed a perfect white from only two primary colours. (Prop. VI) → color-wheel-models, complementary-colors
- With three, four or five colours well spaced around the circle, the model gives white. Newton is unsure about three and treats such artificial whites as curiosities of little importance, because natural white light contains all colours. (Prop. VI) → color-wheel-models, additive-mixing
- Newton calls the centre-of-gravity rule "accurate enough for practice" but not mathematically exact. (Prop. VI) → color-wheel-models
- The circle is a rule for mixing lights (rays), not painters' powders. It works on the rays that reach the eye. (Prop. VI; Prop. V) → color-wheel-models
- ⚠️ The musical proportion of the arcs is presented as the basis of the circle. In Book II, Pt I, Obs. 14 Newton says a different relation fits the ring measurements somewhat better than the simple musical one, so the analogy is loose. Whether the same caveat applies to the circle's arcs was not verified. (Book II, Pt I, Obs. 14) → color-wheel-models

## Lights vs. pigments (Prop. V, Exper. 15; Prop. VII)

- Prop. V holds that white is compounded of all the spectral colours in the right proportions. It is shown by recombining the spectrum with a lens, by a rapidly moved comb, and by a prism setup. In the comb experiment, colours seen when it moves slowly blend into white when it moves fast. (Prop. V, Exper. 9–13) → additive-mixing
- Soapy froth shows colours on its bubbles up close and looks white from far enough away, where the colours blur together. (Prop. V, Exper. 14) → additive-mixing
- Mixing coloured powders (painters' pigments) does not give bright white. Coloured powders stop and absorb a considerable part of the light, so the mixture is dusky: grey, dun or russet brown. (Prop. V, Exper. 15) → pigment-mixing-behavior, color-wheel-models
- Newton's powder trials: red lead with verdigris (Viride Æris) gave a mouse-coloured dun, and red lead with blue bice gave a dun with a purple tinge. Orpiment, the brightest powder, contributed most to the whiteness of the mixture. (Exper. 15) → pigment-mixing-behavior
- A greyish powder rubbed and set in direct sunlight looked intensely white, even whiter than a sheet of white paper kept in shadow. Newton used this to show that the dull grey of mixed powders is a matter of the amount of light reflected, not a different kind of colour. (Exper. 15) → pigment-mixing-behavior
- Conclusion from Exper. 15: perfect whiteness can be produced from coloured components, and the white of the sun is a mixture of the colours of all its rays. (Exper. 15) → additive-mixing
- A white powder mixed with any colour is quickly tinged with that colour, and the rays in a mixture keep their own colour-producing properties. (Prop. VII) → saturation-and-colorfulness, additive-mixing
- Prop. IV: mixed colours can look like homogeneal spectral colours but differ in nature, since compound colours can be split again by a prism and simple ones cannot. Dilution with white or other colours weakens a colour toward white or grey. (Prop. IV; paraphrase of the reader, wording not re-checked) → hue, saturation-and-colorfulness
