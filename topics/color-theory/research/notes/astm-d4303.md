- The standard defines four accelerated test methods that approximate the colour change of artists' colorants under long-term indoor light (daylight through window glass) (store page, Scope) → lightfastness
- Method A is natural daylight through glass in southern Florida, B the same in Arizona, C xenon arc through window-glass filtering without humidity control, D xenon arc with humidity control (store page, Scope) → lightfastness
- Purpose: establish the relative resistance of colorants to change so they can be grouped into lightfastness categories (store page, Summary) → lightfastness
- Categories are assigned from the colour difference between specimens before and after exposure; the colour difference is computed with the CIE 1976 L*a*b* equation (store page, Summary/Significance). Numerical limits per category not visible → lightfastness
- Significance: keeping colour over many years matters for artwork; accelerated exposure helps producers and users select suitable colorants (store page, Significance and Use) → lightfastness
- Results may vary between methods and between repeat tests; outdoor condition differences between A and B are not accounted for when exposures are matched by radiant exposure (store page, Significance and Use) → lightfastness
- Per a search-result description, the scope covers oil, resin-oil, acrylic emulsion, alkyd, watercolour and gouache paints, and one daylight and one xenon method are used to categorize colorants; D4302 (oil/alkyd), D5067 (watercolour) and D5098 (acrylic) are the product specifications that reference it. Unverified at the primary page → lightfastness
- Current edition D4303-10 (reapproved 2022); one listing suggests it may be superseded or withdrawn, to check with ASTM → lightfastness
