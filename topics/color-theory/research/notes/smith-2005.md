## Rayleigh scattering and the blue sky
- Sunlight entering the atmosphere is scattered mainly by oxygen and nitrogen molecules (Rayleigh scattering); the scattered energy is proportional to 1/λ^4, so short (blue) wavelengths are scattered far more than long ones (p. 590, Sec. I) → atmospheric-perspective
- Zenith skylight on a very clear day with the sun well above the horizon is this scattered light. Its spectrum is roughly the sun's spectrum multiplied by 1/λ^4 (the "Rayleigh sky"), so it increases toward short wavelengths (p. 590, Sec. I, Fig. 1b) → atmospheric-perspective
- Measured zenith skylight (sea level, clear day, sun at 33.2° elevation, 1° field of view) follows the same trend but falls below the Rayleigh prediction at the shortest wavelengths (p. 590, Sec. I; p. 596 note 7) → atmospheric-perspective
- Scattering alone would favour violet as much as blue, since the scattered light is strongest at the violet end; the usual textbook answer "more short-wavelength scattering" therefore does not settle the colour. What colour we see depends on how the eye responds (p. 590, Sec. I) → atmospheric-perspective
- The sky is not violet because the colour we see is the eye's response to the whole spectrum, not to the peak. The cone responses to skylight equal those to a mixture of spectral blue light and white light (p. 594, Sec. III; p. 596, Sec. V) → atmospheric-perspective
- The sky is blue but unsaturated (whitish): the matching mixture is monochromatic blue at about 474 nm (Rayleigh sky) or 476 nm (measured sky), plus white light. The white-to-blue spectral irradiance ratio is about 0.049 (Rayleigh) or 0.063 (measured) (p. 594, Sec. III, Figs. 6-7) → atmospheric-perspective
- The measured-vs-Rayleigh gap at short wavelengths has little effect on perceived colour, because S-cone sensitivity is small where the gap is largest (p. 594, Sec. III) → atmospheric-perspective
- The paper treats only the zenith sky, clear day, sun well above the horizon. Under other conditions other mechanisms add to sky colour; at sunset and twilight, ozone absorption is a major cause of the blue zenith sky (p. 590, Sec. I) → atmospheric-perspective
- In the demonstration, the match to skylight is a blue filter (470-480 nm) plus a near-white lamp. The 470 nm light looks slightly more violet than the 480 nm light, but both resemble skylight (pp. 595-596, Sec. IV) → atmospheric-perspective

## Sun and sky spectra (illuminant data)
- The sun radiates approximately as a blackbody; the paper fits T = 5762 K (to measured data with the Stefan-Boltzmann formula). Fig. 1a compares the measured extraterrestrial solar spectrum with the Planck curve (p. 590, Sec. I) → illuminant-color
- Skylight's spectrum is shifted toward the blue end relative to sunlight. Modelled as sun × 1/λ^4, it rises with decreasing wavelength; measured skylight shows the same trend (p. 590, Fig. 1b) → light-and-shadow-color
- Equal irradiance at all visible wavelengths is seen as white light by the average observer (p. 594, Sec. III) → illuminant-color
- Mixing white light with a monochromatic light gives an "unsaturated" colour; saturation is set by the relative amount of monochromatic light, and full saturation means no white (p. 594, Sec. III; example: pink = white + red) → illuminant-color
- Problem 2 (a pen-and-paper exercise, answer not given): for a planet with a 2500 K red giant star, the same method would be used to find the sky colour. This implies the sky colour depends on the source spectrum as well as the scattering (p. 597, App. A) → illuminant-color

## Colour vision used in the argument
- Colour is not a property of light itself but a sensation produced by the visual system; the paper quotes Newton that rays are not coloured (p. 591, Sec. II) → illuminant-color
- Rods work at low light and play no part in colour vision. Cones work in ordinary daylight and give colour vision. A typical retina has about 100 million rods and 5 million cones (p. 591, Sec. II) → trichromacy
- There are three cone types (L, M, S). More than 90% are L or M, with L:M between 1 and 4 (p. 591, Sec. II) → trichromacy
- Cone peak sensitivities (Stockman and Sharpe, 2° field): L about 570 nm, M about 543 nm, S about 442 nm (p. 592, Sec. II, Fig. 3) → trichromacy
- One cone type cannot tell wavelength from intensity (univariance). The same L-cone response arises from 570 nm at 1 unit or from 513 or 623 nm at 2 units, so colour needs comparison of all three cone signals (p. 592, Sec. II) → trichromacy
- Trichromatic theory dates back at least to Thomas Young (1802). The cone sensitivities are now seen as its physical basis (p. 592, Sec. II) → trichromacy
- Each cone response is the integral over wavelength of its sensitivity times the light's spectral irradiance, and the response is linear in the light's amplitude (until cones saturate at very high irradiance). Two different spectra look the same colour (metamers) if all three cone responses are equal. This is why very different spectra can look identical (p. 592, Eqs. 2-3, Sec. II) → trichromacy
- Metamerism example: monochromatic red (640 nm) plus green (540 nm) at irradiance ratio about 2.71:1 matches monochromatic yellow at 580 nm. Overlapped red and green lights look yellow, with no yellow wavelength present (p. 593, Sec. II, Figs. 4-5) → trichromacy
- Metamerism also underlies the sky result: skylight, a broad spectrum, matches a blue-plus-white mixture. The paper says the visual response to skylight is not equivalent to any single spectral light (p. 590, Sec. I; p. 594, Sec. III) → illuminant-color
- Lord Rayleigh's 1881 red+green=yellow match gave nearly the same ratio for most observers, but colour-deficient observers differed. About 8% of the population has anomalous colour vision (pp. 593-594, Sec. II; p. 596, App. A, Problem 1) → trichromacy
- The author deliberately uses cone responses rather than the CIE colorimetry system or chromaticity diagrams, as a more direct path (p. 596, Sec. V) → trichromacy

## What the paper does NOT cover
- No treatment of distant objects, haze, aerial perspective or how colours of far-away things shift. Only zenith sky colour is analysed.
- No data on horizon vs zenith sky, apart from the single remark that other mechanisms matter (ozone at sunset/twilight). It mentions sunrise/sunset colours and polarization only as outside its scope and points to atmospheric optics literature (p. 590, Sec. I; p. 596, Sec. V).
- Nothing on the colour of direct sunlight at the ground, low-sun reddening, or the colour of outdoor shadows lit by skylight. The only link is that skylight's spectrum is blue-weighted relative to sunlight. The blue-shadow inference is not stated by the paper.
- Nothing on painting, pigments or mixing in paint. No CIE chromaticity coordinates of the sky.

Not covered: colored-light-unity (nothing about it explicitly; only illuminant-color-type data above).
Level: university physics / intro-to-intermediate (American Journal of Physics, pedagogical article with integrals; the conclusions are accessible in plain terms).
