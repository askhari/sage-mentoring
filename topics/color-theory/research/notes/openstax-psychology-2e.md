## light-spectrum
- Wavelength is the distance from one wave peak to the next. It is tied to frequency: longer wavelengths mean lower frequency, shorter ones mean higher (§5.2, "Wavelength", Figs. 5.5, 5.6) → light-spectrum
- Amplitude is the height from the centre line to a crest. For light, larger amplitude goes with brighter colours (and for sound, louder) (§5.2, "Amplitude", Fig. 5.5) → light-spectrum
- Brightness (intensity) is associated with amplitude (§5.2, Figs. 5.5, 5.8) → light-spectrum
- Colour perception is linked to wavelength. Long wavelengths look red, intermediate green, short blue/violet. The text does not use the word "hue" here (§5.2, Fig. 5.8) → light-spectrum
- Humans see roughly 380–740 nm. Other species differ: bees see UV, some snakes detect infrared (§5.2, Figs. 5.7, 5.8) → light-spectrum
- Visible light is a small slice of the electromagnetic spectrum, which also includes gamma rays, X-rays, UV, infrared, microwaves and radio waves (§5.2, Fig. 5.7) → light-spectrum

## photoreceptors
- Light passes through the cornea and pupil. The pupil widens in dim light and narrows in bright light. The lens adjusts to focus (§5.3, "Anatomy of the Visual System", Figs. 5.10–5.11) → photoreceptors
- The image is focused on the fovea, a small indentation of the retina packed with cones (§5.3) → photoreceptors
- Rods and cones pass signals via interneurons to ganglion cells, whose axons form the optic nerve (§5.3) → photoreceptors
- Cones work in bright light, are concentrated in the fovea, give high acuity and fine detail, and are involved in colour. Rods work in low light, lie across the rest of the retina, give low acuity, are not involved in colour, and help detect movement in the periphery (§5.3, Fig. 5.12) → photoreceptors
- Moving from a bright place into a dark one (lobby to theatre) shifts vision from cone to rod dominance, with a delay. Night blindness occurs when rods do not respond efficiently to light (§5.3) → photoreceptors
- Each eye's blind spot is covered by the other eye and filled in by the visual system (§5.3) → photoreceptors

## trichromacy
- Trichromatic theory: there are three cone types, each most sensitive to a different wavelength range. Combining red, green and blue can produce every colour in the spectrum (§5.3, "Color Vision", Fig. 5.14) → trichromacy
- Approximate sensitivity peaks in Fig. 5.14: blue ≈ 455 nm, green ≈ 535 nm, red ≈ 580 nm (§5.3, Fig. 5.14) → trichromacy
- ⚠️ The section does not name Young or Helmholtz in the portion read, so the attribution must come from another source. The 580 nm "red" peak is in the yellow-green part of the spectrum, so "red cone" is a loose label.

## opponent-process
- Opponent-process theory: colour is coded in opposing pairs, black–white, yellow–blue and green–red. Some cells are excited by one colour of a pair and inhibited by its opponent (§5.3, "Opponent-Process Theory", Fig. 5.16) → opponent-process
- Greenish-reds and yellowish-blues are not experienced as colours (§5.3) → opponent-process
- Both theories fit by working at different levels. In the retina the cones follow the trichromatic theory (three wavelength ranges). Beyond the retina, cells respond in the opponent-process way (§5.3, same section) → opponent-process

## color-vision-deficiency
- The Ishihara test detects colour deficiency: the person must find numbers hidden in dot patterns (§5.3, "Connect the Concepts: Colorblindness", Fig. 5.15) → color-vision-deficiency
- Red-green colour blindness is the most common X-linked inherited colour abnormality. About 8% of males of White European descent are affected, against about 0.4% of females of that descent (§5.3, same box) → color-vision-deficiency
- Grayscale vision is extremely rare. Such people have only rods and therefore very low acuity (§5.3, same box) → color-vision-deficiency
- The box does not explain the cone-level mechanism of red-green deficiency (as read) → color-vision-deficiency

## afterimages
- An afterimage is a visual sensation that continues after the stimulus is removed. Staring at the white dot on the flag image and then looking at white paper gives a negative afterimage, presented as support for opponent-process theory (§5.3, Fig. 5.16) → afterimages
- ⚠️ The text only says the afterimage supports the theory, as read. A step-by-step explanation (fatigue of one member of a pair) is not confirmed from this source.
