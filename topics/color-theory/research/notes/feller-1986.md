Accessed: extracted full text of the 2012 reissue of Vol. 1 (all 10 monographs plus Appendices). Read in full or in large part: Introduction, Indian Yellow, Cobalt Yellow (sections 3-6), Barium Sulfate, Cadmium Yellows/Oranges/Reds (sections 1-3), Zinc White (sections 2-4), Carmine (3.x and 4.2-4.3), Appendix "Standard Specifications of Pigment Composition and Color". NOT read: Red Lead, Green Earth, Chrome Yellow, Lead Antimonate, and the analytical sections (5.x) of most chapters. Page numbers are the PRINTED page numbers (PDF page = printed + 1). Every chapter is organised as 1.0 nomenclature, 2.0 history, 3.0 artist-relevant properties (3.1 colour, 3.2 hiding power and tinting strength, 3.3 permanence, 3.4 compatibility, 3.5 chemistry, 3.6 oil absorption, 3.7 toxicity), 4.0 composition, 5.0 identification, 6.0 occurrences. The book is a conservation-science handbook (historic pigments), not a painter's manual.

## Book structure and what it assumes

- The Introduction lists four reasons for studying pigments, including to describe the artist's way of working: which basic pigments, pure or mixed, and whether the paints were essentially opaque or transparent (Intro, p. 12). → transparency-opacity
- Section 3.0 of every monograph is where "matters of particular concern to the artist" are collected: colour, permanence, compatibility and handling qualities (Intro, p. 13). → lightfastness
- Pigment identification is described at three levels of precision: general chemical class, variety within the class, and identity/correspondence between two samples (Intro, p. 13). → colour-index-names

## Colour Index names and numbers

- Each monograph uses the Colour Index (CI) generic name plus number and the five-digit CI constitution number as standard identification (Appendix, R. Johnston-Feller, p. 299). → colour-index-names
- The generic name is built from the usual way the colorant is applied (pigment = P, vat dye, acid dye...) combined with a hue abbreviation (R red, etc.), followed by a number for the specific type; example CI Pigment White 22 (PW-22) for natural barytes and CI Pigment White 21 for synthetic blanc fixe (Appendix, p. 299). → colour-index-names
- The five-digit constitution number marks the chemical constitution when it is known; number blocks go by chemical family: natural colorants 75000-75999, inorganic pigments 77000-77999, phthalocyanines 74000-74999. Some modern pigments have no constitution number because their structure is proprietary (Appendix, p. 299). → colour-index-names
- Natural and synthetic barium sulfate have different generic names (PW-22, PW-21) but the same constitution number 77120, since both are BaSO4. Customary format: "CI Pigment White 22, No. 77120" (Appendix, p. 299; Barium Sulfate 1.0, p. 47). → colour-index-names
- Cadmium chapter (1.1, p. 65): pure CdS yellow is CI Pigment Yellow 37, No. 77199; cadmium-barium lithopone yellow is Pigment Yellow 35, No. 77117; sulfoselenide orange is Pigment Orange 20 and the CdSe end member is Pigment Red 108, both No. 77196 (Red 108 also given 77202); cadmium/mercury orange Pigment Orange 23 and red Pigment Red 113, No. 77201. The CI designations of oranges and reds include the lithopone versions. → colour-index-names
- One CI generic name therefore covers a family of shades and compositions (cadmium sulfide, zinc-in-solid-solution and selenium variants differ in hue yet share a code block), so the CI name identifies the chemical class rather than a specific shade or manufacturer's product (inference from Cadmium 1.1 and 3.1, pp. 65, 70; marked as the note-taker's reading, not stated in those words). → colour-index-names
- Indian yellow is listed as Natural Dye CI No. 75320 in the 1971 Colour Index (Indian Yellow 1.0, Baer et al., p. 17). The Colour Index gives a number in the natural-colorant block even to an obsolete pigment. → colour-index-names
- Zinc white and similar entries: the chapters also cite CI entries, but only the examples above were verified by me. Not further covered. → colour-index-names

## Historical, trade and misleading names (versus CI)

- Cadmium: modern names are generic (cadmium yellow primrose/lemon/light/medium/dark, orange and red in light/medium/dark) because the pigment is recent; trade names such as Aurora yellow, jaune brilliant, Cadmolith, Mercadium, Cadmere persist (Cadmium 1.2 and 2.1, pp. 66-67). → colour-index-names
- "Jaune brillant" was historically a mixture of cadmium yellow with lead white, not a pure cadmium pigment; "cadmium green" = cadmium yellow plus viridian; an imitation Naples yellow was a mixture of cadmium sulfide, ferric oxide and zinc oxide (watercolour) or cadmium yellow, lead white, light red and yellow ochre (oil) (Cadmium 2.1, p. 67). → colour-index-names, paint-composition
- Names are unreliable across time: "permanent white" was used for barium sulfate and, at times, for zinc white; barytes, blanc fixe, Venice/Hamburg/Dutch white (increasing barytes in lead white) and "mineral white" show how names mixed pigments, extenders and adulterated pigments (Barium Sulfate 2.1-2.2, pp. 48-49). → colour-index-names
- The name "Indian yellow" was at one time also applied to cobalt yellow (Indian Yellow 2.1, p. 17); Indian yellow has many archaic names (piuri, peori, purree, gogili ...) (p. 17). → colour-index-names
- Zinc white: many archaic and alchemical names (flores zinci, nihilum album, tutia, Chinese white, blanc leger ...) and apothecary jars labelled "tutia" proved to contain pumice or gypsum, so a historical name does not guarantee the material (Zinc White 2.1-2.2, pp. 169-170). → colour-index-names
- German standards define "zinc white" as at least 99% ZnO, while "zinc oxide" or "leaded zinc oxide" means lower purity with lead sulfate: the same word covers different compositions depending on country and period (Zinc White 4.1, pp. 175-176). → colour-index-names, paint-composition

## Paint composition: pigment, dye, lake, binder, extender

- Carmine lakes: carmine pigment is made by extracting cochineal with water and precipitating an aluminium salt of carminic acid with alum; lakes of different hues arise with different metals (tin or mercury scarlet, zinc or aluminium crimson, iron grey-purple) (Carmine 3.5 and 4.2, Schweppe and Roosen-Runge, pp. 265-267). → paint-composition
- Deep carmine lakes made with alum can be lightened by adding inert white substrates (starch, blanc fixe, kaolin) before precipitation, giving a lighter lake; tin salts give scarlet (Carmine 3.1, p. 263). → paint-composition
- Barium sulfate is used as the base (substrate) for preparing lakes and as an extender for lead white (Barium Sulfate 1.0, p. 47; 2.3, p. 50). → paint-composition
- Extender/pigment mixtures: cadmium lithopone is cadmium sulfide coprecipitated with barium sulfate (up to about 62% BaSO4); lithopone itself is ZnS plus BaSO4 (Cadmium 1.0 and 2.2, pp. 65, 69; Barium Sulfate 1.0, p. 47). → paint-composition
- In the nineteenth century lead white was commonly adulterated with barytes, and zinc white in oil in artists' paints is generally found mixed with lead white, barium sulfate and titanium white; manufacturers also added zinc white to coloured paints as a lightening agent, so painters used it without knowing (Barium Sulfate 2.21, p. 49; Zinc White 4.4 and 2.2, pp. 178, 172). → paint-composition
- Cobalt yellow, being translucent, was often adulterated with chrome or cadmium yellow to improve hiding power and hue (Cobalt Yellow 4.4, Cornman, p. 41). → paint-composition, transparency-opacity
- Indian yellow's molecule has a water-soluble part (glucuronic acid) and a part compatible with oily media (euxanthone), which the authors relate to its working properties (Indian Yellow 3.5, p. 24). → paint-composition
- Comparative test paints were made in different vehicles: gum arabic, gum arabic plus zinc white, Acryloid B-72 acrylic, an alkaline acrylic emulsion (Rhoplex AC-33) and an acidic vinyl emulsion (Indian Yellow 3.3, Table 2, p. 23). → paint-composition, lightfastness
- Binder pH matters: Indian yellow paint's initial acidity or alkalinity changes its colour (acid gives a paler yellow) (Indian Yellow 3.1, p. 22). → paint-composition
- Oil absorption is quoted as a handling property that depends on pigment: cadmium 18-26% (yellows/oranges), lithopones 14-20%, blanc fixe 14-21 lb per 100 lb, barytes 7-13, Indian yellow ~40% to 100% depending on authority, carmine lakes 46-100%, zinc white 20-25% and more with fine grades (Cadmium 3.6, p. 73; Barium Sulfate 3.6, p. 51; Indian Yellow 3.6, p. 25; Carmine 3.6, p. 266; Zinc White 3.6, p. 175). → paint-composition
- Zinc oxide reacts with fatty acids of drying oils to form zinc soaps, hardening the film and making it brittle, and the reaction is faster for finer particles; this is an example of pigment-binder interaction (Zinc White 3.3 and 3.5, pp. 174-175). → paint-composition
- Zinc white is unsuitable for vehicles rich in free fatty or resin acids and for lime-based vehicles (Zinc White 3.5, p. 175). → paint-composition
- Raw pigments from a mineral source can carry impurities that change colour (barytes often pinkish from iron oxides, corrected historically by adding ultramarine) (Barium Sulfate 3.1, p. 50). → paint-composition

## Transparency and opacity (hiding power)

- Definition by test: in the paint industry, paint applied over a half-white, half-black ground shows complete hiding if the boundary is invisible and incomplete hiding if it is seen (Carmine 3.1, p. 263). → transparency-opacity
- Indian yellow particles are clear and transparent; their refractive index is close to that of the mounting resin (Canada balsam, n = 1.53) so they look like a stain; the low refractive index means oil and varnish preparations are translucent while aqueous binders can give bright colour with good hiding. Wehlte rated its tinting strength very good and hiding power extremely low, useful for retouching over yellowed varnish (Indian Yellow 3.2, p. 22). → transparency-opacity, tinting-strength
- Barium sulfate has a moderate refractive index and so hides poorly in oil, which is why it first found use in watercolour and other water-based paints (casein, glue). Poor hiding in oil also leads to paints that become more translucent with age (pentimenti) and tend to chalk (Barium Sulfate 3.2-3.3, p. 50). → transparency-opacity
- Barium sulfate's low hiding power also makes its colour easy to shift with dyes or toners (Barium Sulfate 3.1, p. 50). → transparency-opacity
- Cadmium yellows/oranges: high refractive index plus small particle size gives good hiding and light scattering; lighter shades are somewhat more opaque because of smaller particles. Lithopone versions have markedly poorer hiding power and lower tinting strength, because barium sulfate has a low refractive index (Cadmium 3.2, p. 71). → transparency-opacity, tinting-strength
- Zinc white and lead white have similar refractive indices (zinc about 2.0, lead 1.94-2.09) but zinc white hides less in oil, mainly because it needs more oil and so has a lower pigment volume concentration; slightly coarser grades hide better than the finest, and hiding falls when particles are below about 0.25 micrometre (Zinc White 3.2 and 3.6, pp. 172-173, 175). → transparency-opacity
- Titanium white's greater hiding power is given as the main reason it replaced zinc white on the artists' palette (Zinc White 2.2, p. 172). → transparency-opacity
- Carmine lakes are translucent in varnish or oil and used as glazes there, but are reasonably opaque in tempera and glue-size (Carmine 3.2, p. 264). → transparency-opacity
- Early zinc white in oil dried poorly and hid poorly; Leclaire later obtained excellent hiding power in oil by his own manufacturing method (Zinc White 2.2, pp. 171-172). → transparency-opacity
- Medium changes opacity: the same pigment can be translucent in oil and opaque in an aqueous binder (Indian yellow, carmine), the main reason being the refractive index difference between pigment and vehicle (Indian Yellow 3.2, p. 22; Carmine 3.2, p. 264). The book does not give a general formula for this. → transparency-opacity, paint-composition

## Tinting strength

- Section 3.2 of each monograph treats hiding power and tinting strength together; strength is mentioned qualitatively ("very good" for Indian yellow, "considerably lower" for cadmium lithopones) rather than measured in tint ratios (Indian Yellow 3.2, p. 22; Cadmium 3.2, p. 71). → tinting-strength
- Zinc white is mentioned in "mixtures of toners of high tinting strength" and as a lightening agent added to coloured paints by manufacturers (Zinc White 2.2, p. 172). → tinting-strength
- Cadmium lithopone tinting strength and heat/chemical resistance were only slightly below the pure pigment, while price was one-half to one-third (Cadmium 2.2, p. 69). → tinting-strength
- The book gives no quantitative definition of tinting strength; it describes only the relative standing of particular pigments. → tinting-strength

## Masstone and tint (undertone)

- The book uses "masstone" for the colour of a pigment at full strength in its vehicle, and describes tints as mixtures with white (Appendix, p. 299; Indian Yellow 3.1, p. 21). → masstone-undertone
- Colours are expressed in masstone and in mixtures with white through the Universal Color Language (ISCC-NBS), which divides Munsell space into 267 blocks named by hue plus lightness and saturation modifiers; adding white moves "strong red" toward "deep pink", adding grey toward "moderate red" and "grayish red" (Appendix, pp. 299-300). → masstone-undertone
- Examples of masstone colour names: green earth "dark grayish green", carmine lakes in transparent watercolour from "dark purplish red" to "deep yellowish pink", aureolin in watercolour "brilliant yellow" (Appendix, p. 300). → masstone-undertone
- Indian yellow in masstone has more body and saturation than gamboge; Munsell values for six masstone specimens are tabulated (Indian Yellow 3.1, p. 21). → masstone-undertone
- Cadmium masstones: tristimulus values and Munsell notation for light yellow (8.0Y 9.2/9.0) and dark yellow (9.0YR 7.8/9.0) are given; colour range depends mainly on particle size, finer particles giving lighter shades (Cadmium 3.1, p. 70). → masstone-undertone
- Carmine: figure 8 plots the chromaticity of the masstone of a carmine lake in animal glue and in oil against lead white in oil, showing that the same pigment in different binders gives different masstone chromaticity (Carmine 3.1, fig. 8 caption, p. 265). → masstone-undertone, paint-composition
- Carmine paintouts made at incomplete hiding over white paper vary with hue from purple-red to strong red (Carmine 3.1, pp. 263-264). → masstone-undertone
- The book does not use the word "undertone" in the sense of the thin-film/tint colour of a pigment; the nearest idea is colour at incomplete hiding and in mixtures with white. (Sections read; might appear in unread chapters.) → masstone-undertone

## Lightfastness and permanence

- Indian yellow tests: paints exposed 205 and 384 h in an Atlas xenon-arc Fadeometer (Pyrex filter). Colour difference dE (CIE L*a*b*) after 384 h: gum arabic 10.3, gum arabic with added zinc white 12.5, acrylic B-72 28.2, alkaline acrylic emulsion 34.6, acidic vinyl emulsion 39.1, versus 20.8 for the alizarin control. The binder therefore changed fading drastically (Indian Yellow 3.3, Table 2, p. 23). → lightfastness
- Blue wool calibration: after 384 h British Standard BS1006:1971 cloths were 7 -> GGS 4.2, 6 -> 3.0, 5 -> 2.0, 4 -> 1.2 (grey scale contrast), and Indian yellow in gum arabic was judged comparable to BS1006 class 5-6 (Indian Yellow 3.3, p. 23). → lightfastness
- Fluorescence complicates fade measurements for Indian yellow, since fluorescence also fades; an ultraviolet filter cutting off near 400 nm reduced the net colour change only slightly (about 80% as much fading in four samples) and had almost no effect when zinc or titanium white were present (they themselves filter UV) (Indian Yellow 3.3, pp. 23-24). → lightfastness
- Historical opinions diverged: Field found it fugitive in oil and in a closed book; Church said it keeps its hue in watercolour in diffuse light but sunlight bleaches it to brownish; Eibner reported no change in two years of exposure in gum arabic. The authors say they cannot explain the inconsistency (Indian Yellow 3.3, p. 24). → lightfastness
- Indian yellow was on the German "Normalfarben" list of lightfast pigments (Munkert, 1905) (Indian Yellow 3.3, p. 22). → lightfastness, limited-palette
- Carmine lakes: poor resistance to light, fade even under incandescent light; modern carmine lake oil and watercolour paints are equivalent to blue wool 1-2 (BS1006:1971). Cochineal/kermes dyed on wool: class 3 with aluminium mordant, class 5-7 with tin mordant (Padfield and Landi); another test gave class 4 for tin mordant; alizarin with aluminium or tin class 5-6. So dyed fabric, lake and mordant change the rating (Carmine 3.3, pp. 264-265). → lightfastness
- Cadmium: permanence depended on preparation. Early light shades contained free sulfur, cadmium oxalate or carbonate and bleached or darkened (Hunt/Holman Hunt complained of samples turning dirty beeswax colour in 20 years); darkening was caused by free sulfur and by iron, copper, lead or arsenic impurities even at 0.1%. The authors attribute the poor reputation to impurities more than to CdS itself; by the 1920s commercial pigment was reproducibly permanent (Cadmium 3.3, pp. 70-71). → lightfastness
- Outdoor cadmium bleaching results from oxidation of sulfide to colourless sulfate under combined air, moisture and ultraviolet; an impervious vehicle reduces it, and a suitable binder reduces chalking. Sulfoselenide reds weather better than yellows (Cadmium 3.3, p. 72). → lightfastness
- Pigment incompatibilities (see also paint-composition): cadmium yellow darkens in contact with lead or copper pigments (black sulfides), fastest with emerald green, slowest with lead; the reaction is faster in watercolour and slowest in oil; Monet's cadmium yellow darkened where mixed with chrome yellow; iron palette knives can cause iron sulfide darkening (Cadmium 3.4, pp. 72-73). → lightfastness
- Zinc white is itself colour-stable and yellows least among whites in oil, but photochemically catalyses hydrogen peroxide under near UV and accelerates fading of some organic pigments (Eibner), such as Prussian blue/zinc white mixtures in gum arabic; Doerner Institute tests had Prussian blue with lead white fade most, titanium white next, zinc white least in oil, and binder drying course also mattered (Zinc White 3.3-3.4, pp. 173-174). → lightfastness
- Barium sulfate is highly stable to light and compatible with all other pigments (Barium Sulfate 3.3-3.4, p. 50). → lightfastness
- Cobalt yellow: grinding severely makes it darken (Cobalt Yellow 3.6, p. 40). → lightfastness
- The book distinguishes lightfastness (light only) from permanence/compatibility (reaction to pollutants, binder and other pigments); in this book 3.3 "Permanence" is the heading used for both. → lightfastness

## Limited palette and historical palettes

- Quote of survey data: of 46 watercolour artists who answered Russell and Abney in 1888, 18 used aureolin and 12 used Chinese white (zinc white); Huish found half of 32 British watercolourists used aureolin over 1804-1904 (Cobalt Yellow 6.0, p. 44; Zinc White 6.0, p. 183). → limited-palette
- Analysis of the Indian paintings shows Indian yellow mixed with lead white for bright yellow, with indigo for green, with vermilion or hematite and lead white for browns and flesh tones, and with red lead for orange (Indian Yellow 3.1, p. 22). → limited-palette
- Fischer's "The Permanent Palette" (1930) considered cadmiums suitable; the German "Normalfarben" list of the late nineteenth century (Munkert, 1905) is described as a palette of lightfast artists' pigments, to which cadmiums and Indian yellow belonged (Cadmium 3.3, p. 71; Indian Yellow 3.3, p. 22). → limited-palette, lightfastness
- Pigments can be a way of reading an artist's set of basic pigments and whether a green came from one green pigment or a yellow/blue mixture (Intro, p. 12). → limited-palette
