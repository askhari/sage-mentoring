## Color constancy (core, §3, pp. 481-485)
- Color constancy means that the colour assigned to an object stays stable over space and time despite changes in illumination. It matters because colour can serve as a stable object attribute in memory only if it is stable (§3, p. 481) → color-constancy
- Cone signals depend on both surface reflectance and illuminant spectrum, but perception seems influenced mainly by reflectance. The research question is how the visual system separates the two (§3, p. 481) → color-constancy
- Illustration: two photos of one scene under reddish and greenish light. The grapes under red light have the same colorimetric colour as the orange under green light, yet we still see grapes as green and orange as orange (Fig. 2, p. 479; §3, p. 481) → color-constancy
- Same Fig. 2: the grapes do not look identical in the two photos. We attribute the difference to the illumination, which is "inferential" identification, not equal appearance (Fig. 2 caption, p. 479; §3.1, p. 483) → color-constancy
- Quantification: constancy index. 0 = match based on cone excitations only (no correction for illuminant). 100% = match based on the same physical reflectance under both illuminants (§3.1, p. 481) → color-constancy
- Laboratory results vary hugely, from barely any constancy to almost perfect (Foster 2011). Fig. 3 (p. 482) shows indices from about 0 to 1.0 over 30 years of studies (§3.1, p. 481) → color-constancy
- Indices correlate positively with year of publication, r(64) = 0.43, p < 0.001. The explanation offered is more naturalistic settings, full-field adaptation and naturalistic tasks (Fig. 3, p. 482; §3.1, p. 483) → color-constancy
- Studies reaching 90% or more constancy always ensured adaptation, or used naturalistic scenes with many cues for inferential constancy (§3.1, p. 483) → color-constancy
- Limit, metamerism: surfaces that look identical under one light (metamers) can look different under another (metamer mismatching), and constancy is then impossible. Example: clothing that looks different in a badly lit shop and in daylight. This seems rare in the real world (§3.1, p. 481) → color-constancy
- Constancy can be modulated between close to zero and nearly perfect by the presence or absence of cues (Kraft & Brainard 1999; Hansen et al. 2007) (§3.1, p. 482) → color-constancy
- Main mechanisms: chromatic adaptation and simultaneous local contrast (Smithson & Zaidi 2004) (§3.1, p. 482) → color-constancy
- Main mechanisms, continued: "inferential" constancy uses references or anchors. Cited cues are 3D interreflections and shading, depth segmentation, specularities, memory colours, and #theDress (§3.1, p. 482) → color-constancy
- Adaptation: much occurs within a fraction of a second, but complete adaptation takes several minutes (Rinner & Gegenfurtner 2000) (§3.1, pp. 482-483) → color-constancy
- Methods affect measured constancy. Simultaneous displays (Fig. 2 type) prevent full adaptation to both scenes. Appearance-match instructions give particularly low indices. Surface-match (paper-match) instructions allow inferential constancy and give higher indices (§3.1, p. 483; Fig. 3 caption, p. 482) → color-constancy
- Summary: variability is explained by the spectra of surfaces and illuminants, plus the cues for adaptation, contrast and anchoring. In everyday life (full-field adaptation, local contrast, many cues) constancy should be at least as high as in the best lab conditions (§3.1, p. 483) → color-constancy
- Across colours: constancy varies with surface colour (Olkkonen et al. 2009, 2010; Witzel et al. 2016b) (§3.2, p. 483) → color-constancy
- Across illuminant colours: no major difference was found between daylight-axis changes and other directions in older studies. Newer work finds a "blue bias": observers are less sensitive to illuminant changes toward daylight blue, and constancy is best near daylight blue (Weiss et al. 2017). The authors read this as constancy being adapted to daylight and shadows (§3.2, p. 484) → color-constancy
- Colour variation under natural daylight runs along the blue-yellow direction, which is why blue-yellow colour judgements are the most uncertain. Illuminant changes along the daylight axis therefore produce uncertainty in that direction (§2.2, pp. 480-481) → color-temperature
- #theDress (§3.3, p. 484): observers see body and lace as blue/black or white/gold, with perceived colours spread continuously along the daylight locus. The image pixels also lie along the daylight locus, so variation can be attributed either to the fabric or to the lighting. Observers who assume shade (bluish, dark light) see white/gold. Those who assume bright white light see blue/black → light-and-shadow-color
- #theDress, continued: rotating the pixel colours away from the daylight axis strongly reduces the individual differences. The percept is stable over time but can be primed by the context, which points to unconscious inferences about illumination. The authors treat it as a rare failure of constancy (§3.3, pp. 484-485) → color-constancy
- Conclusion: the combined mechanisms allow almost perfect identification of objects and materials across illumination changes under natural conditions (§5, p. 491) → color-constancy

## Object colour vs light (local vs perceived colour)
- Colorimetry describes light reaching the eye at isolated points. This cannot yet predict the appearance of a banana, whose reflected light forms a complex distribution in colour space (Fig. 1, p. 476; §1, p. 477) → local-vs-perceived-color
- A single colour coordinate can be assigned to an object only under severe assumptions about shape, material and illumination. This holds for flat, matte surfaces under idealized light (§1, p. 477) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Observers represent an object with one intrinsic colour (e.g. saturated yellow for a banana). They attribute stains, shading and lighting variation to other causes (§2, p. 478) → local-vs-perceived-color
- For lightness, observers fixate the brightest parts of the object (excluding highlights) as their estimate, because those parts are least affected by shading (§2, p. 478) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Memory colour effect: a colorimetrically gray banana looks slightly yellowish, and a slightly bluish banana looks gray. Shown by achromatic adjustment (Hansen 2006; Olkkonen 2008). Strongest for blue and yellow memory colours. A Bayesian prior is proposed as an explanation (§2.2, pp. 480-481) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Colour is a property of objects and materials, not only of light. The authors list "how to define colours as object properties rather than light properties" as an open issue (Future issues 7, p. 492; §5, p. 491) → local-vs-perceived-color

## Light, shadow and chromatic contrast
- In natural scenes, chromatic contrast and luminance contrast are statistically independent. Object boundaries bring colour changes independent of lightness (§2.1, p. 478) → equiluminance
- Chromaticity tends to indicate objects, while luminance also varies with shadow and shading (Kingdom et al. 2004). Coincident luminance and chromatic edges help to tell object contours from shadows (§2.1, pp. 478-479) → light-and-shadow-color
- Contour detection in natural scenes combines luminance and chromatic contrasts (Hansen & Gegenfurtner 2017). Camouflage uses chromatic contrasts not aligned with object contours (§2.1, p. 479) → equiluminance
- Colour discrimination tests used an isoluminant hue circle (Witzel & Gegenfurtner 2015). This is a single brief mention of isoluminant stimuli; the paper gives no definition or discussion of equiluminance (§4.1, p. 488) → equiluminance

## Simultaneous contrast and afterimages (marginal)
- "Adaptation and simultaneous local contrast" are named as the major mechanisms behind constant colour appearance under changing illumination. There is no description of the effect itself (§3.1, p. 482) → simultaneous-contrast
- Observers overcompensate afterimages of color-diagnostic images (Lupyan 2015). This is cited only as a memory-colour finding, with no explanation of afterimages (§2.2, p. 480) → afterimages

## Color categories (core, §4, pp. 485-491)
- People use colour terms that group many colours into few categories, not metric hue/lightness/saturation (§4, p. 485) → color-categories
- English basic terms are 11: black, white, gray, pink, red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple, brown. Basic terms are the most common ones, understood by most speakers (Berlin & Kay 1969). Chartreuse and mauve are non-basic. Basicness is a continuum (§4, pp. 485-486) → color-categories
- Category sizes differ: red and yellow cover a narrow range, green a wide one. Naming is highly consistent at category centres and variable at boundaries (Fig. 4, p. 485; §4, p. 486). Categories therefore inform some colours more precisely than others → color-categories
- Number of basic categories by language: English and German 11, Himba and Berinmo 5, Korean 15, Russian 12 (§4.2, p. 489). Russian splits blue into siniy (dark) and goluboy (light) (§4.1, p. 486) → color-categories
- Universals: statistical regularities exist across languages, and category prototypes are relatively stable across languages (Berlin & Kay; Regier 2005; Rosch Heider 1972). Infants show categorical responses to colour before language (§4.2, p. 489) → color-categories
- Focal colours: the prototypes of red, yellow, green, blue, black and white may be more perceptually salient. Their hues roughly match unique hues. Neither prototypes, unique hues nor category boundaries map systematically onto the cone-opponent channels (§4.2, p. 489) → color-categories
- Critique of focal colours: prototypes lie near saturation "bumps" of the maximally saturated Munsell chip set. Much support for focal colours depends on this stimulus set, and high saturation at prototypes is "a peculiarity" of the set, not a feature of human perception (§4.2, p. 490) → color-categories
- Alternative accounts: ecological origin (statistics of colours in the environment). Communicative informativeness (categories convey info about surfaces and objects). Prototypes as a consequence of linguistic categories (Abbott et al. 2016) (§4.2, p. 490) → color-categories
- Categorical perception: pairs of colours equally far apart look more different, or are discriminated faster, across a category boundary (e.g. red/brown) than within a category. Equidistance is hard to establish (§4.1, p. 486; Fig. 5b, p. 487) → color-categories
- Evidence for language effects (§4.1, pp. 486-487): language-specific effects (Russian vs English, Korean, Greek); effects from training on new categories; disappearance under verbal interference but not nonverbal interference; lateralization to the right visual field (left hemisphere). Many studies failed to replicate the lateralized effect (pp. 487-488) → color-categories
- No category effect at the low sensory level: discrimination thresholds (sensitivity) show no benefit at category boundaries (Fig. 5a, p. 487; §4.1, p. 488). Sensitivity follows the second-stage cone-opponent mechanisms. Speeded suprathreshold discrimination shows "categorical facilitation" (e.g. at the red-brown boundary), only in naive observers. It disappears with training (§4.1, p. 488) → color-categories
- Interpretation: category effects work at a higher processing level, probably by combining category and sensory information (Bayesian). Perceptual learning accounts are contradicted by verbal-interference effects and by the absent threshold effects (§4.1, pp. 488-489) → color-categories
- Category constancy: categorisation across illuminants is most stable at category centres (Olkkonen 2009, 2010). No relationship between categorisation and perceptual constancy once saturation is controlled (Witzel 2016b; Weiss 2017) (§4.2, p. 490) → color-constancy

## Context and background (from §1, pp. 476-477)
- Colour processing has three stages: cone absorption, cone-opponent channels in retinal ganglion cells, and higher-level mechanisms. Appearance models include CIECAM02, CIELAB and CIELUV (§1, p. 476) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Colour appearance is recalibrated at a higher level. Examples are wearing coloured filters for a long time and cataract surgery (§1, p. 477) → color-constancy
