## Terminology and framing

- "A colour modifies its neighbour" is only a manner of speaking: the two objects have no physical or chemical action on each other; the change occurs in our perception of the two colours seen together (Author's Preface, p. xii) → simultaneous-contrast
- Two kinds of modification when two stripes are seen side by side: contrast of tone (change in intensity / depth of colour) and contrast of colour (change in the "optical composition", i.e. hue). The stripes appear different from what they really are (§8, p. 7) → simultaneous-contrast
- Vocabulary: "tone" = a colour lightened with white or deepened with black, within one "scale" (gamme); "hue" (nuance) = modification by a small amount of another colour. He says he never uses the two words interchangeably (§146-150, pp. 54-55) → simultaneous-contrast
- Origin of the research: as director of dyeing at the Gobelins he found that the complaint of weak blacks in blue/violet drapery shadows was due not to the dye but to the neighbouring colour, i.e. contrast (Author's Preface, p. x; §386, p. 153) → simultaneous-contrast

## The law of simultaneous contrast (Part I, Sect. I)

- Law (§16, p. 11): two contiguous colours seen together appear as dissimilar as possible, both in optical composition (hue) and in height of tone (value) → simultaneous-contrast
- Contrast of tone, experiment (§9-10, pp. 7-8): two light-grey pieces o, o' and two darker-grey pieces p, p' (same black+white mixture), with o touching p. After some seconds o looks lighter than o' and p deeper than p'. The lighter one gets lighter, the darker one gets darker → simultaneous-contrast
- The effect is strongest at the line of contact and weakens gradually with distance from it; it also acts across a gap (covering the nearest strips still shows the farther ones modified) (§10, p. 8; §14, p. 9; §132, p. 42) → simultaneous-contrast
- Ten-stripe grey scale (§11, pp. 8-9): ten flat stripes, each darker than the previous, do not look flat; each intermediate stripe appears lightened on the side next to its darker neighbour and darkened on the side next to its lighter neighbour, so the set looks like channelled grooves → simultaneous-contrast
- The same tone contrast occurs with two tones of one colour (§12, p. 9) → simultaneous-contrast
- Contrast of colour, experiment (§13, p. 9): two colours of as nearly as possible equal tone, set up like the greys; each looks changed in hue compared with its separate piece (the comparison with isolated pieces o', p' is the method) → simultaneous-contrast
- Table of 17 observations with colours "of equal intensity" (§15, p. 10), e.g. red beside orange: red inclines to violet, orange to yellow; red beside yellow: red goes violet, yellow goes green; red beside blue: red goes yellow/orange, blue goes green; yellow beside blue: yellow goes orange, blue goes indigo. The names (indigo, etc.) are his spectral names → simultaneous-contrast
- Explanation by complementaries (§17-18, pp. 11-12): each colour looks as if the complementary of its neighbour were added to it; equivalently, each loses the rays it shares with the neighbour. He works this out for the 17 pairs (§20-37, pp. 13-16), e.g. blue added to green makes it bluer; red added to violet makes it redder → simultaneous-contrast
- ⚠️ This "complementary added to the neighbour" account is 19th-century and expressed in terms of mixing coloured rays; modern colour science explains the effect through opponent / lateral-inhibition processes in the visual system (not in this source). Present his rule (the direction of the observed shift) as a sound description and his mechanism as historical → simultaneous-contrast
- The greater the difference between a colour and the complementary added to it, the more striking the change (§38, p. 16) → simultaneous-contrast
- With near-complementary pairs (red/green, orange/blue, orange-yellow/indigo, greenish-yellow/violet) the main result is greater intensity, purity and brilliance; any hue shift is smaller and not constant (it varies on repeated viewing, with light intensity and eye fatigue) (§38-42, pp. 16-18) → simultaneous-contrast
- No pigment is a pure colour: any coloured object reflects rays of all kinds besides white light, so no two pigments are exactly complementary (§7, p. 6; §38, p. 16) → simultaneous-contrast
- Illustration (§43, p. 19): a coloured disc on white paper tends to tint the surrounding paper with its complementary, fading outward (red→green, green→red, orange→blue, blue→orange, greenish-yellow→violet, violet→greenish-yellow, indigo→orange-yellow) → simultaneous-contrast
- Colours with white (§44-52, pp. 19-21): the white takes a faint complementary tint (hard to see) and the colour looks brighter and deeper. Black and white together differ more than apart (tone contrast) → simultaneous-contrast
- Colours with black (§53-62, pp. 21-22): tone contrast lowers the colour's tone; colour contrast tints the black with the complementary (faintly, since black reflects little light). If the neighbour is dark and has a luminous complementary (orange, orange-yellow, greenish-yellow), the black itself looks weaker; e.g. black beside red looks less reddish, beside orange bluer, beside blue or indigo brighter. Some yellows look weaker beside black → simultaneous-contrast
- Colours with grey (§63-70, pp. 23-24): grey (pure black + white) shows the effect best because it neither overpowers the colour (white) nor is too dim (black). Grey looks greenish beside red, bluish beside orange, violet beside yellow, reddish beside green, orange-tinted beside blue/indigo, yellowish beside violet; the colour looks purer/brighter. A tinted grey is strongly exalted (bluish-grey beside orange gets bluer; yellowish-grey turns green) (§70 ter, p. 24) → simultaneous-contrast
- The chemical composition of the pigment is irrelevant: ultramarine, cobalt and Prussian blue of the same colour give the same modification; likewise oranges from red lead, annatto or woad+madder (§71, pp. 24-25) → simultaneous-contrast
- The same colour looks different beside different neighbours in the same colour group: one red looks purple beside orange-red and orange beside purple-red; one yellow looks greenish beside orange-yellow and orange beside greenish-yellow; one blue looks violet beside greenish-blue and greenish beside violet-blue (§72-74, pp. 25-26) → simultaneous-contrast
- Consequence (§75, p. 26): painters' "primary" red, yellow and blue pass by juxtaposition to the state of secondaries; so pigments cannot fix pure type colours unless contrast is taken into account (§74) → simultaneous-contrast
- He restates the law in painters' language (red, yellow, blue primaries): each colour loses the component shared with its neighbour, e.g. orange beside green loses yellow so orange looks redder and green bluer (§76, pp. 26-30). For complementary pairs (green/red etc.) this language gives no reason why the shift should go one way rather than another (p. 30) → simultaneous-contrast
- Equal bands modify each other: earlier authors (Buffon, Prieur) mostly studied a small patch on a large ground, so they saw only half the phenomenon (§132, pp. 41-42) → simultaneous-contrast
- Cause (§141-142, p. 48): he believes eye fatigue explains successive contrast but not simultaneous contrast, since the modification appears before fatigue (though it can take several seconds to see). White light reflected by the surface weakens it, which is why grey and black show it more than white. He concludes only that the similar character of the two sensations diminishes when two colours are seen together, so their difference increases → simultaneous-contrast
- ⚠️ Chevreul gives no complete physiological theory (§142); the mechanism was an open question. Do not present his reasoning as established physiology → simultaneous-contrast

## Equal tone / equiluminance

- Method (§13, p. 9; §15, p. 10): the colour-contrast experiments need colours of as nearly as possible equal tone/intensity, so that only the hue modification shows. He does not treat equal-lightness colours as a special perceptual case (no mention of vibration, unstable edges or loss of form) → equiluminance
- Contrast of the most opposite colours is most agreeable when they are of the same tone; if crudity from too much intensity is feared, use light tones of their scales (§349, p. 132) → equiluminance
- The translator's preface warns that the chief error in applying the law is overlooking contrast of tone or intensity (Translator's Preface, p. vi) → equiluminance

## Successive and mixed contrast (Part I, Sect. II)

- Three kinds of contrast (§77-79, p. 32): simultaneous (changes in hue and tone when colours are seen together); successive (after looking at coloured objects for a while and turning the eyes away, one sees images in the complementary colour of each); mixed (below) → afterimages
- Mixed contrast (§81, p. 33): the eye that has seen a colour for a time is predisposed to see its complementary; if it then looks at a new colour, the sensation is that of the new colour plus the complementary of the first → afterimages
- One-eye method (§82-84, pp. 33-34): fix one eye on colour a until it dims, then look at colour b with that eye: b appears modified by the complementary of a. Closing that eye and using the other (fresh) eye shows b unmodified. Repetition weakens the effect until the eye returns to normal; with both eyes open the effect is much weakened. He thinks the experiment is particularly useful to painters (§85) → afterimages
- Examples (§86-115, pp. 34-35), always a shift of the second colour toward the complementary of the first: red then yellow, yellow may look more orange; yellow then red: red looks violet; blue then red: orange-red; yellow then blue: blue-violet; red then orange: yellow; orange then red: violet-red; yellow then violet: bluer; violet then yellow: greenish; blue then orange: yellower; orange then blue: slightly more violet → afterimages
- The strength varies: yellow/violet gives a larger and longer-lasting modification than blue/orange; red/green the least intense and persistent. Tone also matters: dark blue after orange looks greener rather than violet (§116, pp. 35-36) → afterimages
- Fabric dealers (§117-119, p. 36): a buyer who has stared at yellow cloth takes orange or scarlet for crimson; after seven or eight identical red pieces, the later ones look less beautiful; the remedy is to show green so the eye returns to normal (prolonged green then makes red look richer) → afterimages
- Earlier work (§120-124, pp. 37-38): Buffon's "accidental colours" (a small coloured square on white paper looks bordered by the complementary; after looking away at the white ground an equal square in the complementary colour is seen); Scherffer (1754) showed that each colour yields its complementary as accidental colour, which Chevreul counts as the law of successive contrast → afterimages
- Scherffer's experiments (§133, pp. 43-44): a white square on black leaves a darker square; a blue square on white leaves an orange square; a red square on yellow leaves a green square on a blue-violet ground. Explanation: the retinal region fatigued by a colour is predisposed to see the complementary → afterimages
- ⚠️ The fatigue-of-the-retina account is an 18th/19th-century explanation; modern accounts speak of photoreceptor adaptation and opponent processes. Treat as historical → afterimages

## Mixing of fine elements / tapestry (Part II, Div. 2) → assimilation

- Principle (§373-375, p. 144): Gobelins weaving rests on mixing and on contrast. Mixing occurs when coloured materials are so finely divided that the eye cannot distinguish them and receives one impression (blue + yellow of equal strength gives green). Contrast occurs when differently coloured surfaces are large enough to be seen distinct and simultaneously; a blue beside a yellow then differ and gain red, instead of tending toward green → assimilation
- Two kinds of mixture (§377-379, p. 145): by threads (threads of the same tone of two analogous scales) and by hatchings (interweaving); with them all colours of a painted pattern can be imitated → assimilation
- Rules from Deyrolle's thread samples at the Gobelins (§380, pp. 145-150): I. binary mixes of primaries give bright orange, violet, green only if the threads carry little of the third primary; II. mixing complementaries (red+green, orange+blue, yellow+violet) neutralises them to grey, generally of higher tone than the colours mixed; III. three primaries in unequal proportions give a greyer, broken colour → assimilation
- Distance (§380 F, p. 149): viewing a yellow+violet thread mixture from farther than the distance at which the colours neutralise, the yellow is so weakened that the mixture looks dull violet; yellow/blue behaves likewise → assimilation
- Orange and blue thread bands (§380 E, p. 149): depending on lighting and viewing position, violet, green, red and yellow fringes appear, and with strong light the bands would not be seen separately; mixing and contrast interfere → assimilation
- Chevreul's criterion for mixing vs contrast is whether the eye can separate the elements (§374-375). He gives no threshold and does not use the term "assimilation" → assimilation
- ⚠️ Chevreul sets thread-mixing against contrast as a blending of colours; he does not describe the modern effect in which small areas make neighbouring colours look more alike (spreading). Do not attribute that to him → assimilation

## Harmonies (Part II, Prolegomena §3-4) → color-schemes

- Six harmonies in two kinds (§180, pp. 69-70). Harmonies of analogy: harmony of scale (tones of one scale), of hues (tones of equal height in nearby scales), of a dominant coloured light (contrasting colours seen as through slightly tinted glass). Harmonies of contrast: contrast of scale (two tones of one scale far apart), of hues (tones of different height in adjacent scales), of colours (colours of distant scales assorted by the law of contrast; tone difference can add to the contrast) → color-schemes
- The eye takes pleasure in colours by themselves, which he compares to taste (§174, p. 67); cases listed: one colour, tones of one scale, near scales, very distant scales, colours seen through tinted glass (§175-179, pp. 68-69) → color-schemes
- Colours from near scales are hard to assort because they often injure each other; a painter can sacrifice one to make the other brilliant (§177, p. 68). Complementary pairs, or very different pairs, are pleasant (§178, p. 68) → color-schemes
- Complementary association is the only one in which colours mutually improve, strengthen and purify each other without leaving their scales; it still works when not exactly complementary or when greyed (Translator's Introduction, p. xl) → color-schemes
- His assortments with white, black and grey (§181-254, pp. 70-92) are stated to be personal judgement, not rigorous deduction (§181, p. 70) → color-schemes
- Harmony of contrast is not always better; for two close colours it may be preferable to move toward harmony of scale or hue (§252-253, pp. 91-92). A grey tinted with the complementary of the adjacent colour always gives an effective contrast (orange-grey/marron with light blue) (§254, p. 92) → color-schemes
- For painters (§348-350, p. 132; §355, p. 133): to make colours attract the eye use harmony of contrast, with pure colours or colours broken with grey; where less diversity is wanted, use harmony of scale or hue → color-schemes
- Rumford's principle (a harmony of two colours must together make white light) is called an ingenious fancy of little use to painting (§128, p. 40) → color-schemes
- Colours fixed by the subject (flesh, hair) versus colours free to choose (draperies, background) (§343-346, p. 131); in a portrait, find the predominating colour of the complexion and choose accessories that set it off (§351, p. 132) → color-schemes

## Consequences for painters

### Local colour versus perceived colour

- Neither of two coloured objects seen together appears in its own colour: each shows its own colour plus the complementary of the other's, and the lighter tone is lowered and the darker raised (§325, pp. 123-124) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Painter ignorant of contrast copies the apparent orange on a red border and green on a blue border; since contrast would produce these anyway, the copy is exaggerated. He should paint plain red and blue and let contrast do it; a grey pattern on yellow ground looks lilac/violet and can be painted simply as grey (§330-332, pp. 126-127) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Paper-stainer example (§333, p. 127): two flat grey bands showed the edge effect. To obtain the intended flat look Chevreul advised lightening the darker band toward the edge and darkening the lighter one: to copy the model faithfully "we must copy it differently from what we see it" → local-vs-perceived-color
- Light tone beside a deep tone of the same scale takes on the complementary: white patterns on a chrome-yellow ground (printing flaw) look violet, on orange chrome blue, on green rose; masking the ground with perforated paper shows their true tint (§291-293, p. 110). Likewise paper placed over the lights of a blue drapery in a Gobelins tapestry looked light blue, but in context they looked orange (§294, pp. 110-111) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Six principles for painters (§335-340, pp. 128-130): (1) each colour put on canvas tints the surrounding space with its complementary, fading outward (red circle: green halo, etc.); (2) white beside a colour raises its tone; (3) black lowers it and can impoverish some yellows; (4) grey makes it more brilliant and is itself tinted with the complementary; (5) a dark colour beside a lighter, different colour raises the first and lowers the second, and this tone effect can cancel or reverse the hue effect (a very dark blue beside yellow can make the yellow look greenish); (6) two flat tones of one colour side by side produce chiaroscuro (a gradation of light) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Weaver's version (§381-385, pp. 151-153): a weaver ignorant of contrast copies the apparent effect of a painted pattern and exaggerates it; for shadows on pink flesh he would use green-grey, but a pure grey suffices because contrast supplies the green → local-vs-perceived-color
- A picture shows its modifications of colour fixed from the painter's viewpoint; in the model they change with the spectator's position (e.g. the blue coat's orange-grey part) (§299, p. 112) → local-vs-perceived-color
- In flat-tint painting the colours are not modified by coloured rays from neighbouring objects; if neighbours are chosen by the law of contrast the colour effect is greater than in chiaroscuro painting (§301, p. 114) → local-vs-perceived-color

### Light and shadow colour

- The colour of one body changes in hue as well as intensity depending on direct sunlight, diffused daylight or reflected diffused light; this must never be overlooked when defining colours of objects (§280, pp. 105-106). Lit part vs shaded part: red lit is more orange, shaded more crimson; yellow shaded is duller and greener; green shaded is bluer; blue shaded is less green (§272-279, pp. 103-105) → light-and-shadow-color
- Coloured lights on coloured stuffs (§261-269, pp. 99-103): lists for red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet light, e.g. red light on yellow gives orange, red on light blue gives violet, green light on red gives brown, blue on yellow gives green → light-and-shadow-color
- Four modifications of a coloured object (§282-286, pp. 106-108): (1) maximum white light: a polished sealing-wax cylinder shows a white stripe where its colour disappears; (2) parts sending less white light in proportion to coloured light look deeper (red wax from scarlet to crimson, gold vase interior red-orange, silk folds); (3) parts sending little light look black or dark; (4) the complementary of the object's colour appears in darker grey parts, by simultaneous contrast → light-and-shadow-color
- Fourth modification, example (§287-290, pp. 108-110): the back of a new blue coat seen against a window: the lit nap is fine blue and the part reflecting almost no light looks orange-grey; deep green gives reddish grey, claret gives yellow. It appears only with dark, sombre colours; bright red, yellow, orange or light blue do not show it (§288, p. 109) → light-and-shadow-color
- Flesh-tint shadows in many pictures show a green tint from contrast of rose with grey (when painters used no green) (§385, p. 153); the green shadows on a painted rose arise from red juxtaposed with normal grey (§338, p. 129) → light-and-shadow-color
- With a colour identical to the model plus black and white, a painter can reproduce all modifications of a monochrome object; complementary shadows come from placing normal grey next to the object's colour (§338, p. 129) → light-and-shadow-color
- Rumford's coloured shadows (§125, pp. 38-39): a shadow lit by white light, beside an equal shadow lit by a red ray, looks green; a grey band beside a coloured band looks tinted with the complementary → light-and-shadow-color
- Predominating coloured light (§361-363, pp. 135-136): a painter who adopts a dominant colour must adjust the others; under orange light purples go redder, reds more scarlet, yellows more orange, greens lose blue and become yellower, light blues become light grey, deep indigo goes marron → light-and-shadow-color

### Judging colour

- Knowing the law, a painter quickly appreciates the true colour of each part and the modifications from neighbours, and sees weak modifications he would otherwise miss (§325-326, pp. 123-124) → judging-color
- Painter's eye fatigue (§327-328, pp. 124-125; §366, pp. 137-138): looking at the model too long produces mixed contrast, so the colour seen next is wrongly judged. He describes three states of the eye (simple perception; attentive viewing in which contrasts show; fatigued, tending to see complementaries) and attributes part of the "dull colouring" of some careful painters to over-long viewing and repeated reworking → judging-color
- Practical test (§387, pp. 154-155): when unsure of the true colour of a part of the pattern, isolate it with a cut-out paper and compare directly with the thread colour → judging-color
- Meaning of "colouring" (§298, p. 112; §305-322, pp. 117-122): "absolute" colouring is faithful reproduction of how light modifies the model's colours. A picture can be faithful but exaggerated, which is often more agreeable (§311); have a dominant colour not in the model, as if seen through tinted glass (§312); or please by harmony of local colours without fidelity (§313). People weigh these qualities differently, so judgments of who is a "colourist" differ (§316-317); yellowed varnish and ageing prevent judging old masters' original colouring (§320-322) → judging-color
- Reasoning why exaggeration appeals (§311, p. 119): he compares the eye's liking for an excess of the exciting cause to the taste for strongly flavoured food (cf. §174); judge a picture considering the light of its intended place and viewing distance → judging-color
