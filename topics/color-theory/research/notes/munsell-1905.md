## Hue, value, chroma (definitions)
- A colour has three measurable qualities: hue, value and chroma. If one is left out, the colour is not defined. (¶8, ¶20) → hue, value, chroma
- Hue is the quality that tells one colour from another (red from yellow) and says nothing about lightness or strength. Physically he links it to wavelength. (¶21) → hue
- Value is how light or dark a colour is. He links it to wave amplitude, and a photometer can measure it. (¶22) → value
- "Tint" and "shade" are commonly misused. "Shade" should mean only a darkened value, and "value" is the better term for lightness. (¶22, Appendix I) → value
- Chroma is the strength of a colour. In physical terms he calls it "purity", which is the degree of departure from grey. (¶23) → chroma
- Fading mainly lowers chroma, though it often alters the other two qualities as well. Mixing in other wavelengths lowers chroma, and balanced daylight looks white. (¶23) → chroma
- A weaker-chroma colour needs a larger area to balance a stronger one. (¶77, ¶109) → chroma, color-schemes
- Training order for children: hue first, then value, then chroma. Most children can handle about ten value steps, 20 to 35 hue steps and five chroma steps. (¶41–43) → hue, value, chroma

## Terminology: chroma vs intensity, purity, strength
- Ordinary language has no word for the quality of strength, so people borrow terms from other fields. "Tone" and "key" come from music, "intensity" from other contexts, and these muddy colour talk. (¶4–6) → saturation-and-colorfulness
- "Intensity" is misleading when chroma is meant. It depends on the light of spectral hues and names a degree rather than a quality. (Appendix I) → saturation-and-colorfulness
- "Purity" should be avoided for pigments, because no pigment reflects a single pure spectral ray. (Appendix I) → saturation-and-colorfulness, chroma
- He wants tint, tone, shade, purity and intensity replaced by measured terms (hue, value, chroma). He proposes "color-spot" for a single brush mark and "color-envelope" for an overall harmony. (Appendix I) → saturation-and-colorfulness
- ⚠️ "Saturation" does not appear in the digests of the passages read. He offers no chroma/saturation/colorfulness distinction in the modern sense. → saturation-and-colorfulness

## Hue circle and principal hues
- Hue runs around the equator of the colour sphere, with value on the vertical axis and chroma outward from the axis. (¶24–25) → hue, munsell-system
- Five principal hues: R, Y, G, B, P. Five intermediates are written with joined initials: YR, GY, BG, PB, RP. Together they make a ten-step hue circle. (¶31, ¶40, ¶56–60) → hue, color-wheel, munsell-system
- Ten hue steps are enough for early schooling. (¶59–60) → color-wheel
- Hand mnemonic: thumb is red, forefinger yellow, middle finger green, ring finger blue, little finger purple. Fingertips are white, the wrist is black and the palm centre is grey. (¶54–55) → color-wheel, munsell-system
- Neighbouring hues mix into the intermediates. He glosses YR as orange, PB as violet and RP as plum. (¶56–58) → hue, color-wheel
- Mixing hues one step apart gives a coloured grey, such as a yellowish grey. (¶64) → chroma, hue
- In the score, green sits at the centre between warm hues (yellow, red) and cool hues (blue, purple). (¶136–137) → color-wheel, color-schemes

## Opposites and complements
- Opposite hues mix to neutral grey. Side by side, they make each other look stronger. (¶61–63) → complementary-colors, color-wheel
- His opposite pairs: R with BG, Y with PB, G with RP, B with YR, P with GY. (¶61–62) → complementary-colors, color-wheel-models
- Complements are colours equally distant on opposite sides of the neutral centre. That centre is the natural balancing point. (¶76) → complementary-colors, color-schemes
- Practical test: pigment pairs are tempered to equal value and chroma, then painted on spinning discs (Maxwell discs). If the blend looks neutral grey, the pair is balanced. A leftover tint shows which hue is in excess. (¶105–116) → complementary-colors, color-wheel-models, munsell-system
- A ring of ten middle hues around the equator should spin to neutral grey. (¶112–116) → color-wheel, munsell-system

## Critique of RYB / Brewster
- Many schools teach red, yellow and blue as primaries with the corresponding opposites as complements. He calls this false. (Appendix III; also the 1907 preface warns against teaching RYB as primaries) → color-wheel-models
- He says Brewster's theory was dropped once colour vision was shown to rest on red, green and violet-blue sensations. (Appendix III) → color-wheel-models
- Tests with mixed coloured light or Maxwell discs show the RYB complements do not neutralise. A full-circle test shows too much orange, meaning red and yellow take up too much of the circle. (Appendix III) → color-wheel-models
- His complements instead: red with blue-green, green with red-purple, violet-blue with yellow. (Appendix III) → color-wheel-models, complementary-colors
- Yellow is not primary, because mixed red and green light produces it. Green is fundamental and is not made from yellow and blue. (Appendix III) → color-wheel-models
- Pigments are impure, so painters' practice does not validate RYB. Vermilion mixed with emerald green gives a brownish grey, not yellow, and painters therefore use yellow pigments. Following RYB produces unbalanced colour. (Appendix III) → color-wheel-models
- Runge's sphere and Chevreul's cylinder put R, Y, B equidistant. Yellow is much lighter and blue much darker, so their value scales do not line up. He links the overextended warm colours to Brewster's mistake. (Appendix to Chapter V, "Color schemes based on Brewster's mistaken theory"; checked against the text 2026-11-01) → color-wheel-models, value
- In the spectrum he treats only red, green and violet-blue as primary, with yellow, blue-green and purple as secondary. He argues for dropping "indigo", "orange" and "violet" as vague names. (¶87–90) → color-wheel-models
- Recombined spectral light gives white, whereas pigment mixtures give grey. (¶92–93) → color-wheel-models
- Spectral hues can exceed pigments in chroma, so the spectrum's slanted circuit extends beyond the pigment sphere. (¶15–19, ¶96–97) → color-wheel-models, munsell-system
- ⚠️ In practice he joins the red and purple ends of the spectrum into red-purples, though he says science does not support this junction. (¶96–97) → color-wheel-models

## Value scale and measurement
- The grey axis runs black to white and is numbered N1–N10 in the passages summarised. Elsewhere the value scale is given as 0 (black) to 10 (white). (¶31, ¶65) → value, munsell-system
- ⚠️ Digest inconsistency: the 0–10 scale is attributed to ¶31, and N1–N10 to ¶65. Verify the exact end labels before citing. → value
- Judging middle grey by eye is unreliable. A sample judged "middle" reflected only 42% of light, and individuals and even each eye differed by more than 10 percent. He argues for measured scales, not feeling. (¶65) → value
- The photometer has two halves, one fixed and one with a valve dimming the light. A dial reads the fraction of light, and the sample is compared with a standard white. (¶65) → value, munsell-system
- A value number applies equally to greys and colours, so R7 is as light as N7. (¶66) → value, munsell-system
- Mixing a light and a dark hue gives a midpoint in both hue and value. Y9 with R5 gives YR7. (¶68–71) → value, hue
- Pigment values are checked with the photometer again after graying. (¶109–110) → value, munsell-system
- Yellow sits highest in value on the tree, with red and blue lower and purple near black. Red has the greatest chroma and purple the least. (¶94–95) → value-structure, chroma
- ⚠️ ¶34 lists tree-branch height as yellow, green, red, blue, purple. ¶94–95 refers to spectral hues. Both come from the digest, so check the original order. → value-structure

## Munsell system: notation, tree, solid, instruments
- Notation: hue initial at left, value numeral above the line at right, chroma numeral below. Example: R5/9, which he says matches vermilion. (¶40) → munsell-system
- ⚠️ The 1905 form is an initial plus a fraction. The modern "5R 5/9" form with hue steps is a later development, and this source does not show it. → munsell-system
- Sphere model: white at the north pole, black at the south pole, middle hues on the equator, and perpendiculars to the grey axis measure chroma. (¶12, ¶24–25) → munsell-system
- Two-dimensional diagrams omit chroma. Pigments differ widely in chroma, so a "colour tree" with uneven branches is needed. (¶13–14) → munsell-system, chroma
- Tree: the value axis is the trunk and chroma branches are horizontal arms. They extend to about chroma 6–9, past the sphere's limit of 5, and the tips mark the strongest available pigments. (¶31–34) → munsell-system, chroma
- Maximum chroma is unequal by hue and value. The full solid is irregular, with "mountains" beyond the sphere. Decimal notation such as R5.1/4.9 records the differences. (¶123–128) → munsell-system, chroma
- Example of unequal chroma: chromium sesquioxide (blue-green) has about half the chroma of its complement, sulphuret of mercury (red). (Appendix IV) → munsell-system, complementary-colors
- Reference standards: five permanent enamel pigments of middle value and chroma (red, yellow, green, blue, purple), called a "chromatic tuning fork". (¶28–29, ¶117) → munsell-system
- Instruments: a spinning colourless globe, a photometer for values, matt permanent pigments, and spinning (Maxwell) discs for balance. (¶103–108) → munsell-system
- The sphere is repeated at every value level. Meridians give a hue's value scale and parallels give a hue scale. Oblique paths suit "Luminist" or "Colorist" styles. (¶118–122) → munsell-system, value-structure
- The "score" is a flat chart of the solid, with value vertical, hue horizontal and chroma as a numeral beside each dot. It records single colours, pairs and groups, and shows whether a composition is warm or cool, light or dark, strong or weak. (¶133–145) → munsell-system, color-schemes

## Balance and harmony (prescriptive)
- Light and dark values of opposite hues can balance in grey only if they are equally distant from the equator (YR7 with B3). (¶72) → color-schemes, value-structure
- Buttercup YR7/9 and violet B3/4 do not balance in grey, because the stronger buttercup draws more attention. Balance is therefore judged across hue, value and chroma together, and a larger area of violet compensates. (¶73–77) → color-schemes, chroma
- Artists' judgments (too light, too weak, too warm) correspond to departures in value, chroma and hue. Balance resists simple rules, and measured notation can record successes and failures. (¶75, ¶83–86) → color-schemes
- ⚠️ Prescriptive: he says no settled harmony laws exist. Older formulas failed because hue, value and chroma were undefined, and musical analogies do not transfer well. (¶146–153) → color-schemes
- Three typical paths: vertical (value only), lateral (hue) and inward through grey to the opposite hue. Others combine these. (¶158–163) → color-schemes, value-structure
- Small strong patches can balance large weak fields. Repeated accents create rhythm. (¶164–166) → color-schemes
- ⚠️ Prescriptive and taste-bound: refined taste moves from loud chroma to tempered, low-chroma colours. Quiet colour signals good taste, balance matters more than strong chroma, and "tuned" colour should not be left to personal whim. (¶175–181, Appendix IV) → color-schemes, color-meaning
- Green sits between warm and cool, so the score can show the warm or cool lean of a composition. (¶136–140) → color-schemes

## Colour education
- Teaching sequence: recognition, imitation, invention. Course stages: perception, expression, harmony. (¶39, ¶48–50) → color-meaning
- Course of study, grades 1–9: hue (grades 1–2), value (3–4), chroma (5–7), balance by area (8–9). (Part II) → color-meaning
- Start children with middle hues and avoid extremes. Colour names are inconsistent and need a measured system, as music has for sound. (¶1–3, ¶7, Appendix IV) → color-meaning
