## light-spectrum
- Visible light is usually taken as wavelengths of about 400 to 750 nm; the borders with infrared and ultraviolet are not sharp (§24.3, Fig. 24.15) → light-spectrum
- Fig. 24.15 shows the visible strip as seven rainbow bands from red (longest wavelength, lowest frequency) to violet (shortest wavelength, highest frequency); the text gives no wavelength per intermediate colour (§24.3) → light-spectrum
- Visible light lies between infrared ("below red") and ultraviolet; all EM waves obey c = fλ, so higher frequency means shorter wavelength (§24.3, Fig. 24.8, Eq. 24.8) → light-spectrum
- Sunlight peaks in the visible range but is more intense in red than in violet, which is why the Sun looks yellowish (§24.3) → light-spectrum, illuminant-color
- Pure-wavelength light is seen as a single colour. White light is a fairly even mixture of all visible wavelengths; sunlight looks slightly yellow but contains all of them (§25.5) → light-spectrum
- A rainbow shows about six colours (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet; indigo sometimes added), each a wavelength range. The distribution is continuous, not discrete bands (§25.5, Fig. 25.21) → light-spectrum
- Dispersion is the spreading of white light into its full spectrum of wavelengths. More generally it occurs whenever a process changes the direction of a wave depending on its wavelength (§25.5) → light-spectrum
- Cause in prisms: the refractive index n depends on wavelength, rising as wavelength falls, so violet bends most and red least. Water example: n = 1.331 (red, 660 nm) to 1.342 (violet, 410 nm) (§25.5, Table 25.2, Fig. 25.22) → light-spectrum
- Rainbow in a drop: refraction on entry, reflection at the back, refraction on exit; dispersion happens only in the refractions, since the law of reflection does not depend on wavelength. Each colour leaves at a different angle (§25.5, Figs. 25.23, 25.24) → light-spectrum
- Secondary rainbow: two internal reflections, appears above the primary with reversed colour order (§25.5, Fig. 25.24c) → light-spectrum

## photoreceptors
- Rods: about 120 million, roughly 1000 times more sensitive than cones, used for night and peripheral vision and motion detection, give no colour information; sensitivity peaks near 500 nm (§26.3, Figs. 26.10, 26.11) → photoreceptors
- Cones: about 6 million, work best in bright light, give high resolution and colour vision, concentrated in the fovea, which has no rods (§26.3, Figs. 26.10, 26.11) → photoreceptors
- Three cone types, each sensitive to a different wavelength range. Fig. 26.11 shows peaks near 420 nm (blue-sensitive, lowest relative sensitivity), 520 nm (green) and 590 nm (labelled yellow) (§26.3, Fig. 26.11) → photoreceptors

## trichromacy
- Simplified theory: three primary colours correspond to three cone types, and all other hues arise from different combinations of cone stimulation (§26.3) → trichromacy
- Examples: red plus green light is seen as yellow; all three give white; colour TV uses red, green and blue phosphor dots (§26.3) → trichromacy, additive-mixing
- Caveats given: there is no unique set of primaries (yellow, green, blue also work), different stimuli can give the same hue, and the three-cone idea is well established but incomplete (§26.3) → trichromacy
- Pure wavelengths give a few basic colours, but many more hues exist (brown, teal, pink); the simple theory ascribes them to wavelength combinations, though perception is subtler (§26.3) → trichromacy

## light-matter-interaction
- An object's "true" colour is determined by its absorptive and reflective properties: a blue object reflects blue and absorbs the other colours; a black object absorbs nearly all light; pink can come from weak absorption of all colours except red (§26.3, Fig. 26.12) → light-matter-interaction
- Not covered here: reflectance as a quantity (curve), and transmitted-light colour (only the tablecloth/filter-style examples; the take-home activity with overlaid coloured sheets viewed against white light, §26.3) → light-matter-interaction

## illuminant-color
- Observed colour depends on illumination: a pure blue object under pure red light looks black, although its true colour is still blue (§26.3, Fig. 26.12) → illuminant-color
- Source colour depends on the emitted wavelengths (Fig. 26.13): He-Ne laser pure red, Sun broad yellowish spectrum, fluorescent lamps bluish-white, incandescent lamps reddish-white (§26.3) → illuminant-color

## color-constancy
- Colour constancy is the ability to perceive an object's true colour under different lighting (e.g. a white tablecloth in sunlight, fluorescent light or candlelight), by comparison with surroundings (§26.3) → color-constancy
- Retinal processing compares adjacent receptors and emphasises edges; Fig. 26.14 shows uniform gray stripes that look lighter or darker near their edges (§26.3) → color-constancy
- Land's retinex theory: cone signals are compared across systems, explaining constancy; his red- and blue-filter slide experiment yields full colour from black-and-white images. The text says the theory is not fully accepted (§26.3) → color-constancy

## additive-mixing
- Additive mixing of coloured light appears only via RGB displays/TV and the red+green=yellow, all three=white statements (§26.3) → additive-mixing

## Scattering (for atmospheric-perspective, only partly)
- §27.8 "Polarization by Scattering": light scattered by air is partially polarized (sunglasses rotation makes the blue sky brighten and dim); air-molecule electrons oscillate perpendicular to the ray and re-radiate, giving polarization perpendicular to the original ray (Fig. 27.46); multiple scattering makes it only partial; polarizing filters darken the sky in photos → atmospheric-perspective (polarization only, not colour)
- ⚠️ This section does not mention Rayleigh scattering, wavelength dependence of scattering, or red sunsets; those claims cannot be cited from this source as read.

## Added for color-mixing
Accessed (this extension): §26.3 re-read in full via a summarizing fetch tool, plus one search of openstax.org for subtractive mixing, pigments, filters and complementary colours; the search found them only in other OpenStax books (not this source), so nothing from those is used here.

- The book's "simple theory of color vision" posits three primary colours that correspond one-to-one to the three cone types (§26.3, "Simple theory" / Fig. 26.11) → primary-colors
- Primaries are defined here by cone physiology and light, not by pigments; the book gives red, green and blue as its working set (colour TV phosphors) (§26.3) → primary-colors
- The primary set is explicitly not unique: yellow, green and blue can also serve as primaries (§26.3) → primary-colors
- The three-primary idea is described as well established; more sophisticated theories build on it rather than replace it (§26.3) → primary-colors
- Equal numbers of red, green and blue phosphor dots on a colour TV produce the whole range of hues viewers see (§26.3) → additive-mixing
- Yellow is perceived when red and green light are present in the right intensity ratio; white when all three primaries are lit (§26.3) → additive-mixing
- The same perceived yellow can come from true yellow light, from red plus green, or from white light with violet removed, because different stimuli can excite the cones in the same pattern (§26.3) → additive-mixing
- How the eye "adds": hue results from the pattern of stimulation across the three cone types, so different light combinations that stimulate the cones identically give the same hue (§26.3) → additive-mixing
- Mixing red, green and blue lights is suggested through a PhET simulation activity, which yields a full range of colours (§26.3, end-of-section activity) → additive-mixing
- Absorption basis of object colour: the colour of an object is set by what it absorbs or reflects; blue object in white light looks blue, black absorbs all, a blue object in pure red light looks black because it absorbs the red (§26.3, Fig. 26.12) → subtractive-mixing
- White is an equal mixture of all visible wavelengths; black is the absence of light (§26.3) → subtractive-mixing
- Take-home experiment: overlay coloured plastic sheets, paper, fabric or crayon colours and view against white light, then explain with the simple theory; the book gives no explanation or rule (§26.3, "Take-Home Experiment: Exploring Color Addition") → subtractive-mixing
- ⚠️ The book never states that mixing pigments, dyes or stacking filters removes more wavelengths (subtractive rule), never names cyan/magenta/yellow, and uses the heading "color addition" for the overlay experiment. Subtractive mixing therefore cannot be cited from this source beyond the absorption bullets above.
- Complementary colours: not defined or mentioned anywhere in §26.3 (and none found elsewhere) → complementary-colors (not covered)
