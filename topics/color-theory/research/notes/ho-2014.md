## color-temperature

- Convention: red = warm/hot and blue = cool/cold is common practice in industrial and interior design in many parts of the world; the example given is hot and cold taps (Introduction). → color-temperature
- Prior literature: the association has long been reported, mostly with subjective measures: rating colour patches as warm or cool, or naming the colour a thermal stimulus reminds one of (Introduction, citing Tinker, Wright, Berry, Morgan et al., etc.). → color-temperature
- Novelty of this paper: the authors claim the first objective performance test (reaction times) of the hue–temperature correspondence. Their results agree with the earlier subjective findings (Discussion, para. 1). → color-temperature
- Exp. 1 (Implicit Association Test, 11 participants, red/blue patches and the words "warm"/"cold"): responses were faster when red shared a key with warm and blue with cold than for red-cold / blue-warm (F(1,10)=12.69, p=.005; Results). → color-temperature
- Interpretation of Exp. 1: the red-warm / blue-cold link is present at the level of response selection, so it is a measurable automatic association and not only a stated opinion (Discussion, Conclusions). → color-temperature
- Exp. 1 detail: the effect appeared for both colour and word targets, but congruency helped colour targets more (interaction p=.036) (Results). → color-temperature
- Exp. 2B (priming, 17 participants, real 34.2 °C vs 17.3 °C Peltier stimuli): a red or blue patch shown 2 s before touch sped up temperature judgments when congruent (red-warm, blue-cold) (F(1,16)=9.06, p=.008) (Results). → color-temperature
- Limits of the effect (Exp. 2): colour did not affect judging the colour of a target, so the priming effect was one-directional, from colour to temperature. The authors link this to colours being used to signal temperature and not the reverse (Exp. 2B results, Discussion). → color-temperature
- Limits of the effect (Exp. 2A): there was no priming when the thermal target was a word. The authors suggest words are strongly tied to their response category and are processed fast (~500–600 ms), so colour cannot influence them. They suggest the effect shows up mainly when the target is weakly tied to its category or slow to process (physical temperature ~2000 ms) (Discussion, para. 3). → color-temperature
- Origin (learned): the authors say the association is probably learned from environmental correlations. Fire and sun are warm, so their colours become linked to warmth. Water and forests are cool, so blue and green become linked to coolness. Skin also reddens when warm and turns bluish when very cold (Discussion, para. 5). The paper cites others for the skin point. → color-temperature
- Origin (cultural convention): the results were similar in Japan (Exp. 1, 2A) and Australia (Exp. 2B). The authors attribute this to shared cues such as red/blue taps in both places. They report that in some African countries the mapping is reversed (blue = hot, red = cold; Tomaselli 2001, cited). They cite Morgan et al. that the correspondence reflects "loosely held cultural norms" (Discussion, para. 6). → color-temperature
- Not hardwired: the authors cite Ernst (2007) to say people can be trained to integrate unrelated signals. They still treat correspondences as a way the brain decides which signals belong together (Discussion, last para.). → color-temperature
- ⚠️ Green is mentioned as "cool" (Discussion, para. 5) only as an environmental inference (forests), not tested. The paper gives no data for hues other than red and blue, so a full warm/cool hue division (yellow, orange, green, violet) is not supported by this source. → color-temperature
- ⚠️ Small samples (11, 14, 17), red and blue only, no luminance/saturation manipulation beyond matching. Evidence of strength is statistical, not a measure of how large the effect is in practice. → color-temperature

## color-meaning

- Colour–temperature is presented as an example of a crossmodal correspondence, a systematic link between features in different senses. Other examples cited: pitch–elevation, pitch–size, loudness–size (Discussion, para. 5, citing Spence 2011 review). → color-meaning
- Such correspondences are said to be often learned by internalizing natural environmental correlations (Discussion, para. 5). → color-meaning
- Direction can be asymmetric: the cited work found a colour–pitch association was judged more plausible when asked as pitch→colour than as colour→pitch. The authors compare this to colour-temperature being one-directional (Discussion, para. 2). → color-meaning
- Correspondences are distinguished from synaesthesia, but possibly with shared neural mechanisms (Discussion, para. 2, citing Deroy & Spence, Ward et al.). → color-meaning
- Cultural variability: because associations are learned, culture can change them. The reversed African mapping is the example (Discussion, para. 6). → color-meaning
- Reference list also points to Taft & Sivik (1992) on cross-national colour meaning, Michael et al. on colour-induced nasal thermal sensations, and Williams & Bargh 2008 on physical warmth and interpersonal warmth. These are only cited, not summarized in the paper. → color-meaning
