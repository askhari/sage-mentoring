## Method
- Survey of 4598 participants (final sample, 1114 male, mean age 35.4) from 30 nations on all continents but Antarctica, in 22 native languages, aged 15-87, self-reported normal colour vision (p. 9, p. 10-11). → color-meaning
- Participants saw 12 colour terms, explicitly not colour patches (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, turquoise, purple, pink, brown, black, grey, white), and picked any number of 20 emotion concepts from the Geneva Emotion Wheel, rating intensity from weak to strong (p. 11-13). → color-meaning
- The 20 emotions vary in valence (positive/negative) and power (high/low) and are not a warm/cool or arousal scale (p. 9, p. 11). → color-meaning
- The task measures the conceptual link between colour words and emotion words (p. 29). → color-meaning
- Participants were online, literate and computer-literate, recruited largely through the co-authors' networks (p. 13, p. 30). → color-meaning

## Results: which emotions go with which colour terms
- Prominent global associations (probability of 0.4 or more): black with sadness, fear and hate; red with love and anger; pink with love, joy and pleasure; grey with sadness and disappointment; yellow with joy; orange with joy and amusement; white with relief (p. 19-20, Fig 3). → color-meaning
- Red and black were the most emotion-laden colour terms in every nation, and brown the least (p. 23, p. 27). → color-meaning
- Mean number of emotions chosen per colour term was about 3 (3.05) (p. 14). → color-meaning
- Participants also agreed on relative tendencies, e.g. love and anger go with red more than shame does (p. 27). → color-meaning

## Results: universality
- Nation-to-global pattern similarity averaged r = .88 (range .684 Egypt to .941 Spain); nation-to-nation similarity averaged r = .78 (range .501 to .951) (p. 21). → color-meaning
- Per colour term, similarity with the global pattern ranged from r = .66 (purple) to r = .93 (pink). Purple and yellow varied most between nations; pink, green, turquoise and black varied least (p. 21). → color-meaning
- Men's and women's patterns were almost identical (r = .987), and there were no age-related pattern differences (p. 27). → color-meaning
- The authors conclude that colour-emotion association is a robust candidate psychological universal. They say it is modulated, not shared at 100%, as no universal is (p. 27-30). → color-meaning

## Results: culture, language, geography
- A classifier identified a participant's nation from the 240 ratings in 34.4% of cases (chance 9.7%, AUC .85). This shows real nation-specificity. Nigeria was easiest to predict and Spain hardest (p. 23-24). → color-meaning
- Shorter linguistic distance (β = -.37) and shorter geographic distance (β = -.13) both predicted more similar association patterns. Together they explained about 15% of variance, so linguistic proximity mattered more than geographic (p. 25-26). → color-meaning
- Nation-specific examples: Nigerians linked red also with fear; Chinese linked white also with sadness; Egyptians did not link yellow with joy; Greeks linked purple with sadness (p. 21). → color-meaning
- The authors attribute the white and purple cases to funeral traditions (white worn in China, dark purple in Greece) (p. 28-29). → color-meaning
- Finland, Lithuania and New Zealand endorsed associations more than the global average, and Azerbaijan and Egypt less (p. 23, p. 28). → color-meaning
- Possible sources of local variation: how emotion or colour terms are understood in each language, and physical environment. They cite sunshine exposure modulating yellow-joy in another study (p. 28). → color-meaning
- The introduction gives cultural examples. Envy can be yellow (Germany) or red (Poland); Westerners wear white to weddings and black to funerals, while the Chinese prefer red for weddings and white for funerals. Red is ambivalent, linked to both positive and negative emotions (p. 7-8). → color-meaning

## Limitations stated by the authors
- Colour words, not colours seen. People may link red with anger as a concept without feeling angry when seeing red. Whether the same modulation holds for direct experience is untested (p. 29). → color-meaning
- Online, literate samples may reflect globalisation. Small-scale societies are not tested (p. 30). → color-meaning
- Data are correlational and the sources of the local differences are not identified (p. 28). → color-meaning
- Not preregistered (p. 32). → color-meaning

## Not covered
- color-temperature: the paper has no warm/cool or arousal content. Its only dimensions are valence and power, in the emotion wheel. The only hue-sunshine link is the yellow-joy remark (p. 28, citing another study).
- Per-nation and per-colour probability tables are in the supplement and were not read.
