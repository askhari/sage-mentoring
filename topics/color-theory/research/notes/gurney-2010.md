## color-schemes
- Most schemes rest on three component colours (a triad); the area inside the triangle is the gamut. The traditional painter's wheel uses red-yellow-blue; the digital wheel uses cyan-magenta-yellow. (blog, 'The Shapes of Color Schemes', 2008-02-03) → color-schemes
- Complementary: opposites, with the neutral at the wheel centre, so fairly stable; split complementary is vibrant but a bit unsettled. (blog, 'The Shapes of Color Schemes', 2008-02-03) → color-schemes
- Any scheme can be seen as a shape on the wheel, and each shape has its own "personality" regardless of its colours: an equilateral "atmospheric triad" is moody; a triad shifted toward red moves the neutral too; a near-complement mask feels dissonant; a mask covering one side of the wheel suggests purity or weirdness. (blog, 'The Shapes of Color Schemes', 2008-02-03) → color-schemes
- Gurney keeps a fondness for the RYB wheel even though commenters call it obsolete/arbitrary; calls the digital wheel more useful for screen work but less familiar to painters. ⚠️ opinion/commentary. (blog, 'The Shapes of Color Schemes', 2008-02-03) → color-schemes
- He does not systematically critique the classic schemes; he treats them as mask shapes: a long diamond across the wheel is "basically" simple complementary colours; a triangle to one side of the wheel is a biased alternative. (blog, 'Part 2: Gamut Masking Method', 2011-09) → color-schemes
- A mask does not have to be a triangle (squares, diamonds also work); a triangle is handy because it gives three starting colours. Gamuts are three-dimensional; the wheel is a 2-D slice, so the centre may be grey, white or black. (blog, 'Part 2: Gamut Masking Method', 2011-09) → color-schemes, gamut-planning
- Any three colours can serve as "subjective primaries" (e.g. orange, green, purple, as in early autochrome photos); a less saturated pigment (yellow ochre vs cadmium yellow) keeps the corner inside the wheel's margin and reduces the gamut. (blog, 'The Shapes of Color Schemes', 2008-02-03) → color-schemes, gamut-planning
- Pigment-mixing complements may be nearer red/green than the digital red/cyan. ⚠️ Gurney's aside in a comment reply. (blog, 'How do you choose limited-palette colors?', 2021-02) → color-schemes

## gamut-planning
- Gamut masking: place a shape (usually a triangle) over the colour wheel; what is inside is the palette for the painting, the rest is left out. Premix a set of "color strings" from the mask and use only those. (blog, 'Part 2: Gamut Masking Method', 2011-09) → gamut-planning
- The triangle's corners are "subjective primaries", the purest colours within the gamut, not necessarily full-intensity; smaller shape = narrower range, a tiny one = monochromatic. (blog, 'Part 2: Gamut Masking Method', 2011-09) → gamut-planning
- Purpose: controlled, consistent colour for sequential work (book illustration, comics, concept art, film colour scripting); goal is harmony, "not playing all the instruments at once". (blog, 'Part 2: Gamut Masking Method', 2011-09) → gamut-planning
- Deliberately omitting sectors of the wheel produces complete-feeling but limited, subjective colour environments; example mapping the schemes of two Dinotopia paintings, and a swamp scene of dull yellow-greens/browns inside a small triangle. (blog, 'Color Wheel Masking Basics', 2022-05) → gamut-planning
- Glazing, imprimatura and coloured filters also compress a gamut, but Gurney finds premixing more controlled and flexible. ⚠️ preference. (blog, 'Part 2: Gamut Masking Method', 2011-09) → gamut-planning
- Caveat (reader noticed, Gurney agreed in reply): colours inside the triangle may not all be mixable from the three corner paints, since pigment mixes do not follow straight lines. ⚠️ from comment thread, seen via search summary only. (blog, gamut masking posts, 2011) → gamut-planning
- With gamut masking harmony is a given, so effort goes into reaching for accents. ⚠️ paraphrase seen only in a search summary. (blog, gamut masking posts) → gamut-planning, dominance-and-accent

## dominance-and-accent
- A reduced gamut has one dominant full-intensity hue and two weaker, subordinate primaries; in the shifted triad the secondaries come from mixing the dominant hue with the weaker ones. (blog, 'The Shapes of Color Schemes', 2008-02-03) → dominance-and-accent
- "Mood and accent": most of the picture in one colour mood, with a single accent from across the wheel and no intermediate mixtures; an offset accent (not the exact complement) looks less natural and may draw more attention. (blog, 'The Shapes of Color Schemes', 2008-02-03) → dominance-and-accent
- Proportion is expressed mainly through the size of the mask relative to the wheel; the posts give no numeric proportion rule (e.g. 60-30-10). ⚠️ gap. (blog, 'Part 2: Gamut Masking Method', 2011-09) → dominance-and-accent
- Quoting Harold Speed: a picture with prevailing unity of hue but subtle colour varieties is among the most beautiful schemes; beware overusing blue as a unifier. ⚠️ Speed's view, relayed by Gurney, seen via search summary. (blog, 'Harold Speed Discusses Color and Taste', 2016-04) → dominance-and-accent, colored-light-unity

## color-temperature
- Warm/cool is relative: judge a colour by comparing with neighbours; painting from a white plaster cast removes local colour. Gurney's advice: be sceptical of fixed rules, observe, "compare, compare". (blog, 'Relative color temperature on skin tones', 2017-07) → color-temperature
- "Warm light, cool shadows" has limits: warm reflected/secondary light can make shadows warmer than the lights; shadow colour varies with plane direction; in a north-light studio the lights may be cool relative to shadows (warm bounce from floors/rugs). (blog, 'Relative color temperature on skin tones', 2017-07) → color-temperature, light-and-shadow-color
- Reader reformulation (Ketchek): warm light tends to give cooler shadows, cool light warmer shadows; shadows need only be cooler than the lit side, not cool in hue. ⚠️ reader comment, not Gurney. (blog, 'How to Apply the Warm-and-Cool Approach', 2018-06) → color-temperature
- A more precise rule: shadow planes facing up are cool (sky), planes facing down are warm (ground bounce); the shift can occur at nearly equal value; exceptions exist (e.g. strong warm light from above reversing it). (blog, 'Color temperature in the shadow out-of-doors', 2014-05; 'Warm below, cold above', 2012-05, the latter via search summary) → color-temperature, light-and-shadow-color
- Halftones are often painted cool/greenish by old masters; can look convincing or not depending on handling. (blog, 'Relative color temperature on skin tones', 2017-07) → color-temperature
- A warm-cool study is essentially a value study taking a first step towards colour; a warm pigment can stand for warm light or for an intrinsically warm local colour (e.g. orange building). A simple warm/cool pair (ultramarine + raw sienna) can suit finished work, giving quietude or austerity. (blog, 'How to Apply the Warm-and-Cool Approach', 2018-06) → color-temperature, limited-palette
- Limits raised by readers: values matter more than temperature; a two-pigment palette is not the same as a warm-cool bias. ⚠️ reader comments. (blog, 'How to Apply the Warm-and-Cool Approach', 2018-06) → color-temperature

## value-structure
- Value planning as its own step, usually in charcoal/pencil before colour (Rockwell, Cornwell, Lovell cited); "Color adds a lot, but it can't save a bad value design." (blog, 'Value Planning', 2011-03-29) → value-structure
- Gurney's method: tonal design in two or three values, then a limited-palette block-in (yellow ochre, burnt sienna, ultramarine) fixing the tonal statement, large warm/cool relationships and colour mood, holding back the darkest darks. (blog, 'Value Planning', 2011-03-29) → value-structure
- Colour can turn a form (e.g. a bluer hue at equal value) and can distort perceived value when chroma differs greatly (intense orange vs dull blue-grey); desaturating the image shows how value reads. (blog, 'Value Planning', 2011-03-29) → value-structure
- Value groups: sort tones into light and dark groups; a restricted range guards against dullness of all-middle values. Swatch set of six notes (light/dark x warm/neutral/cool). (blog, 'Painting Within a Value Gamut', 2015-06) → value-structure
- Loomis: four tones (white, light grey, dark grey, black); letting one tone dominate gives four arrangements, said to fit nearly any picture. Gurney finds four almost too many and prefers two or three main tonal areas; the dominant tone need not be the background; not all pictures (misty, night) fit. (blog, "Loomis's Scheme for Tonal Organization", 2012-06) → value-structure, dominance-and-accent
- "Simplicity of tone is one of the hardest things to accomplish in composition, but it makes a huge difference." (blog, "Loomis's Scheme for Tonal Organization", 2012-06) → value-structure
- The terms "tonal key" and "value groups" are not used by Gurney in the posts read; "tonal key" not covered. ⚠️ gap. → value-structure

## local-vs-perceived-color
- The colour of any spot depends on four factors: local colour, colour of the light, amount of light, atmosphere in between; beginners tend to see only local colour because perception filters out the rest. Example: a white hat under blue skylight, dim, with much atmosphere reads as a medium-value blue. (blog, 'How do you mix a color you're looking at?', 2020-06) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Colour constancy: we perceive local colours as stable despite changes in light, shadows, modelling; this is unconscious and hard to override (illustrations with the same paint reading as different colours). (blog, 'Color Constancy', 2010-01) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Related to chromatic adaptation (the visual system's "white balance"); a picture keyed heavily to one colour family tends to look photographic. ⚠️ Gurney's interpretation. (blog, 'Color Constancy', 2010-01) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Suggested exercise: paint a backlit white object (e.g. a volleyball) in sunlight to see the four factors at work. (blog, 'How do you mix a color you're looking at?', 2020-06) → local-vs-perceived-color, judging-color
- Skin colour = local colour + light colour + subsurface scattering; local skin colour varies across the face. (blog, 'Relative color temperature on skin tones', 2017-07) → local-vs-perceived-color

## light-and-shadow-color
- The colour seen is a mix of surface colour and the colour of all light striking it; a shadowed white wall picks up ground, sky and nearby lit surfaces; "up" and "down" are relative to which illuminated surfaces a plane faces. (blog, 'Color temperature in the shadow out-of-doors', 2014-05) → light-and-shadow-color
- Shadow light is mainly warm ground bounce from below and blue skylight from above: up-facing shadow planes cool, down-facing warm; nearby sunlit surfaces and water add tints. (blog, 'Reflected light in shadow', 2014-11) → light-and-shadow-color
- Golden hour: low sun passes through more atmosphere, remaining sunlight is dimmer and warmer, the sky above is richer blue, and shadows facing away from the sun look bluish. (blog, 'The Golden Hour', 2008-01) → light-and-shadow-color, colored-light-unity
- Noon sunlight is generally neutral white but looks warm beside skylight and cool beside firelight: light colour is also relative. (blog, 'How do you mix a color you're looking at?', 2020-06) → light-and-shadow-color, color-temperature
- Warm reflected light bouncing into an orange local colour increases its chroma (shadow example). (blog, 'Chroma in the Shadows', 2012-03; via search summary) → light-and-shadow-color

## colored-light-unity
- Two or more coloured lights on one form: lit regions interact unexpectedly; overlapping yellow-green and magenta light mix to a pale yellowish white brighter than single-light areas; on a shiny head each light makes its own highlights. (blog, 'Colored Light and Form', 2008-04) → colored-light-unity
- Colour helps the brain group highlights into sets of parallel planes and thus build form; Gurney argues colour is not "extra frosting" (citing vision researcher Kathy Mullen); a reader disagreed. Departs from the traditional "value defines form" rule as a special case. (blog, 'Colored Light and Form', 2008-04) → colored-light-unity
- The posts read do not discuss using coloured light to unify a picture; unity enters via gamut masking (harmony is built in), a dominant hue (Speed) and colour-family keying (constancy post). ⚠️ gap; partial. → colored-light-unity
- Reader idea (not Gurney): using the same colour in two places so it reads differently in each can unify a painting. ⚠️ reader comment. (blog, 'Color Constancy', 2010-01) → colored-light-unity

## atmospheric-perspective
- Gurney's long-held assumption of a linear value shift with distance may be wrong; he proposes a geometric model where each layer of air lightens darks by a fixed proportion (10, 5, 2.5, 1.25...). ⚠️ explicitly preliminary ("still trying to figure this one out"). (blog, 'Atmospheric distance and value', 2013-07) → atmospheric-perspective
- Saturation falling with distance is "a good rule of thumb in general", but light sources such as the setting sun can become more saturated with distance, and clouds can pick up orange. (blog, 'Atmospheric distance and value', 2013-07) → atmospheric-perspective
- Painters such as Hibbard, Wendt, Payne used hue and chroma to strengthen distance; Gurney calls this an important tool. (blog, 'Atmospheric distance and value', 2013-07) → atmospheric-perspective
- Sky gets lighter from zenith to horizon because we look through more atmosphere. (blog, 'Sky Blue', 2008-04; via search summary) → atmospheric-perspective
- Atmosphere is one of the four factors in a colour (see local-vs-perceived-color); the model assumes evenly lit, evenly distributed air, which readers say is unrealistic. ⚠️ (blog, 'Atmospheric distance and value', 2013-07) → atmospheric-perspective

## limited-palette
- A limited palette can be built from almost any two or three colours apart on the wheel, ideally one warm and one cool; two colours plus white are fast, reduce choices to light/dark and warm/cool, and suggest unusual combinations. (blog, 'How do you choose limited-palette colors?', 2021-02) → limited-palette
- Choose the colours that matter most in the scene and decide what to leave out; interesting schemes often come from omissions; an underpainting (e.g. yellow) can add colour indirectly. (blog, 'How do you choose limited-palette colors?', 2021-02) → limited-palette, gamut-planning
- Zorn palette: white, yellow ochre, vermilion, black; Gurney defends it as a valid teaching shorthand though critics (Bahr, Sandström: 17 cobalt tubes in his studio) say Zorn used more colours; Gurney concedes only chemical analysis would settle it. ⚠️ historical attribution disputed. (blog, 'The Zorn Palette', 2011-07) → limited-palette
- Limitation reflects resourcefulness; colour relationships do the work (grey reads blue, black+ochre reads green by context). Commenter: needs strong value structure, suits advanced painters. (blog, 'The Zorn Palette', 2011-07) → limited-palette
- Limited palettes give harmonious, memorable results and push painters out of default mixing habits; "four or five colors" can paint almost anything in nature. ⚠️ seen via search summary only. (blog, 'Why Limit Your Palette?', 2015-11) → limited-palette

## judging-color
- A single colour spot is nearly impossible to judge in isolation; always compare with other colours in the scene ("Compare, compare!"). (blog, 'How do you mix a color you're looking at?', 2020-06) → judging-color
- Techniques (mostly from Gurney's replies/commenters): see colour as shapes and ignore object names (Monet quote), view a patch through a hole in paper, sample with an eyedropper tool from photos, study masters' shadows. ⚠️ several are reader suggestions. (blog, 'How do you mix a color you're looking at?', 2020-06) → judging-color
- Gurney notes cameras capture shadow colour less well than the eye, and high-contrast scenes blow out or crush in photos. (blog, 'Reflected light in shadow', 2014-11) → judging-color
