## Aim and design
- The authors propose that painters' colour choices in outdoor scenes carry time-of-day information, so that viewers can decode it from low-level colour and luminance statistics, independently of content cues such as people, shadow length or sun position (Abstract; Introduction) → colored-light-unity
- Turner's pair "The Morning After the Deluge" and "The Evening of the Deluge", and Monet's Rouen Cathedral series (different palettes titled with different times of day), are given as examples of colour tied to time of day. The article says these suggest the question but are not data (Introduction, Fig. 1) → colored-light-unity
- Fig. 1 caption: Monet's series amplifies the colour-appearance changes that a "typical color-constant viewer" would perceive, and reflects his personal experience and visual abilities (Fig. 1 caption) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Data set: 17th–20th-century Western European oil paintings, mostly outdoor scenes. Paintings with explicit human-activity cues were avoided. Images came from the Materials in Painting (MIP) and National Gallery open-access sets. Most are 19th century (Methods, Stimuli; General discussion, Fig. A4) → colored-light-unity
- Experiment 1: 104 paintings (50 MIP, 54 NG) plus 4 night-scene catch trials. 7 response options: sunrise, morning, noon, afternoon, evening, sunset, night. Online raters on Amazon Mechanical Turk; 51 participants, and 25 who scored below 80% on the catch trials were excluded. For ratings, 7 categories were merged into 4: morning+sunrise, noon+afternoon, evening+sunset, night (Methods: Stimuli, Observers, Statistical analysis) → colored-light-unity
- Experiment 2: a new set of 90 MIP paintings whose titles named the time of day (sunrise 10, morning 17, sunset 36, evening 27), i.e. 27 morning-type and 63 evening-type. Forced choice morning vs evening, 61 participants, 3 blocks of 41 trials (Methods: Stimuli, Observers, Procedure) → colored-light-unity
- Image statistics: 30 measures per painting, from sRGB converted to CIE XYZ/LAB. They include mean, variance and skewness of luminance and chromaticity, RMS contrast, ΔE00 between the brightest and darkest pixels, luminance-weighted correlated colour temperature (CCT), luminance–chromaticity correlations, and photoreceptor activations. An airlight colour was estimated with the dark channel prior (Methods: Color specifications, Statistical measures, Image airlight color) → gamut-planning

## Physics of daylight (the authors' explanation)
- Solar elevation is the main determinant of the illuminance and chromaticity of natural light (Characteristics of terrestrial illumination) → light-and-shadow-color
- Early morning and late afternoon light is more diffuse and less intense than midday. The causes are the longer atmospheric path (more scattering) and the cosine law for light on upward-facing surfaces (Characteristics of terrestrial illumination) → light-and-shadow-color
- The short-to-long wavelength proportion falls as Rayleigh scattering increases, so the direct beam is skewed toward reddish hues at sunrise and sunset (Characteristics of terrestrial illumination) → light-and-shadow-color
- Terminology: diurnal = daylight, crepuscular = twilight (skylight plus artificial light, sun below the horizon), nocturnal = moon, stars and light pollution (Characteristics of terrestrial illumination) → light-and-shadow-color
- If solar elevation alone mattered, morning and evening skies would look identical. Gombrich wrote of Corot's "mellow light of morning or evening". Beurs (1692) noted that sunrise tends to use cooler colours and more mist, and sunset warmer colours that "hold the warmth of the day" (Sunrise–sunset asymmetry) → light-and-shadow-color
- Proposed explanation of the asymmetry: evening air cools and larger water particles scatter the long wavelengths, giving an orange-red sky. Morning air is laden with overnight moisture and smaller particles, and mist, haze and fog give a bluish, milky airlight (Sunrise–sunset asymmetry) → atmospheric-perspective
- Airlight is light scattered toward the viewer by atmospheric particles. It gets brighter with path length, and its hue depends on particle size (Sunrise–sunset asymmetry) → atmospheric-perspective
- Cast shadow length and shadow direction are cues painters use for sun position and time of day, even when the shadows are simplified beyond physical plausibility (Introduction) → light-and-shadow-color

## Experiment 1 results
- People can readily read the depicted time of day. Ratings differed significantly across paintings (ANOVA F(103, 2600) = 15.23, p < 0.00001). Including or excluding the participants who failed the catch trials made no difference (p = 0.82) (Results, Experiment 1) → colored-light-unity
- Agreement was high for only a few paintings: the maximum modal response was 77% for morning, 81% for noon/afternoon, 77% for evening and 100% for night. About 12% of paintings had a bimodal morning–evening split, and many others had mixtures of 3 or 4 categories. This is the morning/evening ambiguity that motivated Experiment 2 (Results, Experiment 1; Intermediate discussion) → colored-light-unity
- Mean chromaticities of the paintings cluster along the daylight locus, from bluish to orangish. Almost all lie slightly above it (positive Duv, a green shift), which the authors attribute to the many landscape features (green vegetation) (Image analysis; Intermediate discussion, Fig. 6) → gamut-planning
- The image statistics are redundant. A principal component analysis (PCA) gave 5 components with eigenvalue > 1. The first two explain 71.1% of the variance of the statistics, and 5 components explain 90.4% (Results, Image analysis) → gamut-planning
- PC1 is a luminance and contrast dimension: brightness, cone/rod/melanopic input, contrast and luminance-weighted CCT. Luminance skewness loads the opposite way. Brighter images are more contrasted and skewed toward bright pixels, "consistent with depictions of strong daylight". PC2 is a chromaticity dimension: saturation, chroma, b*, colourfulness (Results, Image analysis) → gamut-planning
- All 30 statistics correlate significantly with the mean time-of-day score (p < 0.0001). The strongest (|r| > 0.6) are luminance skewness r = +0.82, saturation–luminance correlation +0.62, mean luminance −0.81, L-cone input −0.82, RMS luminance contrast −0.81, b* SD −0.72, ΔE00 −0.67, luminance-weighted CCT −0.69. Negative values mean a brighter, higher-contrast painting is rated earlier in the day (Results, Relationship between observer responses and image metrics) → gamut-planning
- Multiple regression of the mean rating on PCs with forward selection and AIC. The best model uses only PC1 and PC2 (mean rating = 0.583 + 0.034·PC1 + 0.017·PC2). R² = 0.760 (adjusted 0.755); PC1 alone gives R² = 0.706. Adding PC3–PC5 adds nothing significant (Table 1, Eq. 1, Fig. 9) → gamut-planning
- Summary of the Experiment 1 direction: "morningness" goes with higher brightness, contrast and saturation and with bluer/darker-yellow hues. "Eveningness" goes with lower brightness, contrast and saturation and with yellower/darker-blue hues (Abstract; General discussion) → colored-light-unity
- Sunrise and sunset paintings occupy the largest areas in PC space and load on both components. Night paintings occupy the smallest area, and noon/afternoon load mainly on PC1 (Results, Fig. 8B) → gamut-planning
- The authors link the low saturation of evening paintings to the Hunt effect: colours in darker surroundings look less saturated (General discussion) → light-and-shadow-color

## Experiment 2 results (morning vs evening)
- Ratings differed significantly between title-labelled morning and evening paintings (Mann–Whitney p < 0.001). The χ² agreement of the title category with the majority rating was χ²(1) = 13.235, p < 0.001. Individual paintings ranged from morning paintings rated "morning" by 95% of raters to evening paintings rated "evening" by 100% (Results, Experiment 2, Fig. 10) → colored-light-unity
- Mean CCT of title-labelled paintings: morning 5233 K, evening 4692 K. For paintings classified by rater majority (top third of each): morning 5686 K, evening 3945 K. Morning chromaticities cluster near D50–D55 (neutral white), while evening ones spread over a wide range weighted toward the warm end (Results, Experiment 2, Fig. 14) → light-and-shadow-color
- Luminance differences between morning and evening are subtle (not significant for title labels, significant for rater classification). Chromaticity (CCT) differences are the more significant ones (Results, Fig. 13; Conclusions) → light-and-shadow-color
- Estimated airlight is cooler than the mean image colour. Airlight CCT (title labels): morning 5482 K, evening 5116 K. Airlight CCT (rater classes): morning 6131 K, evening 4305 K. Morning paintings are bluer than evening ones in both mean image and airlight colour (Results, Fig. 14C-D) → atmospheric-perspective
- The authors suggest that depicted haze or mist may be a cue separating morning from evening. Haze causes more diffuse light, lower colour differences, and a more even distribution of white in the image. The blue-shifted airlight in the morning relative to the evening follows real atmospheric filtering (General discussion) → atmospheric-perspective
- Sunset skies are described as having large spatial and angular colour variation, from orange-red to deep blue (General discussion; Panorgias et al. 2012 cited) → light-and-shadow-color
- In Experiment 2, some correlations (mean saturation and chroma) reversed in sign compared with Experiment 1: higher saturation went with higher eveningness. The authors attribute this to the narrower range (only morning/evening) (Results, Experiment 2) → light-and-shadow-color
- The overlap between title-labelled morning and evening clusters in PC space was larger than between rater-classified clusters. The authors say observers perceive more marked differences than the metadata specify (Results, Experiment 2, Fig. 12) → colored-light-unity

## Interpretation and relevance for the concepts
- Conclusion: the image statistics of paintings vary systematically with depicted time of day, reflecting terrestrial illumination. The authors say this suggests that artists used different palettes and patterns for different times of day, and that viewers hold consistent assumptions about the variation of natural light (Abstract; Conclusions). This is correlational evidence of a coherent choice of overall luminance/chroma distribution, not direct evidence of painter intent → gamut-planning
- The global colour distribution acts as one shared light signal for the whole picture. The authors read the mean image chromaticity as the estimated illuminant chromaticity under a "gray world" assumption, i.e. the average colour of the scene stands for the light (General discussion) → colored-light-unity
- Colour constancy and illuminant vs surface: the article rests on the viewer reading the light (illumination) from the picture. The only explicit constancy statements are the Monet caption ("color-constant viewer") and the gray-world assumption. There was no experiment separating illuminant from surface colour or testing constancy directly → local-vs-perceived-color
- Confound with the known mean-colour drift: the authors checked varnish yellowing. The correlation of year of creation with rating was weak and negative (Exp. 1 r = −0.27, p = 0.0051; Exp. 2 r = −0.16, p = 0.13, n.s.), opposite to what yellowing-as-morning would predict. They also considered the lighter canvas grounds of the 19th century, and found no support (General discussion) → local-vs-perceived-color

## Limitations stated or evident
- Online ratings on uncalibrated screens, via MTurk. The sRGB (D65 white) model is assumed for all, but results held under alternative white points (Fig. A5) and laboratory calibrations (Methods; General discussion) → gamut-planning
- Reproductions rather than originals, and effects of colour degradation (e.g. varnish) on the actual paintings are not controllable (General discussion) → local-vs-perceived-color
- Metadata reliability: titles were mostly chosen by artists, but not before the 19th century. Titles may reflect curators' interpretation (General discussion) → colored-light-unity
- Large inter-individual differences; morning/evening ambiguity is not resolved. Experiment 2 results partly depend on the narrow stimulus range. The paintings are Western European, 17th–20th c., chosen as landscape-dominated (Intermediate discussion; Methods) → colored-light-unity
- The analysis is correlational: it does not say how painters mix paints or plan a palette, only what the final image statistics look like (whole article) → gamut-planning
- Experiment 1 effective sample is implied to be about 26 raters after exclusions (ANOVA df 2600 over 104 images); this is inferred, not stated (Results, Experiment 1) → colored-light-unity

## Not covered / level
- Not covered: nothing on painting techniques for glazing, local colour or shadow colour (e.g. blue/violet shadows) as such. Direct evidence on colour constancy in paintings is minimal. Atmospheric perspective in the classic sense (colour and contrast loss with distance) is only addressed indirectly through airlight and haze.
- Level: university/specialist (vision science, empirical paper). Reliability B.
