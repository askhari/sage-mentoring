## qubits-and-circuits

### qubit
- A pure quantum state is a unit vector in a complex vector space; the course sticks to discrete systems where a measurement has finitely many outcomes (Lec3, p. 1) → qubit
- A qubit is the simplest interesting quantum system: a two-level system with labels 0 and 1 and an amplitude on each. A one-state system would be trivial (Lec3, p. 1) → qubit
- Quantum mechanics gives a hybrid picture: a continuum of possible states, but each measurement has a discrete outcome. The continuum is never observed directly, only used to compute probabilities of discrete outcomes. He notes the same is true of classical probability distributions (Lec3, p. 1) → qubit
- Global phase is unobservable (multiplying the whole state by a scalar changes nothing), but relative phase is observable. His analogy: shifting the entire universe 20 feet left (Lec3, p. 4) → qubit
- Two states differing in relative phase (the |+⟩/|−⟩ pair) can be told apart by rotating by 45 degrees and then measuring in the standard basis (Lec3, p. 4) → qubit
- Hadamard maps the {0,1} basis to the {+,−} basis; the two are complementary: being maximally certain in one means being maximally uncertain in the other (Lec4, p. 1) → qubit
- Measuring in an orthonormal basis gives each outcome with probability equal to the squared projection of the state on that basis vector; the {0,1} basis in the Born rule was a special case chosen by convention (Lec4, p. 1) → qubit
- Measurement is irreversible, probabilistic and discontinuous, unlike unitary evolution, which is invertible, deterministic and continuous. Reconciling the two is the Measurement Problem (Lec4, pp. 2-3) → qubit
- Even one qubit has a richer behaviour than a classical bit. Example: a qubit can be distinguished from another only if their states are orthogonal; for non-orthogonal states the best basis for equal-prior guessing bisects the two vectors (Lec5, pp. 1-2) → qubit
- Aaronson-Drucker coin problem: deciding whether a coin is fair or biased takes about log-many bits of memory classically, but 1 qubit suffices, by rotating the qubit by a small angle per flip and measuring at the end. His remark: there is still low-hanging fruit even in the mechanics of one qubit (Lec5, p. 1) → qubit

### bloch-sphere
- The Bloch sphere is a geometric picture of all single-qubit states. The usual circle wastes half its points, since a state and its negative give the same density matrix. Redrawing so that opposite directions mean orthogonal states gives the sphere (Lec7, p. 1) → bloch-sphere
- Points on the surface are pure states; antipodal points are orthogonal states (Lec7, p. 1) → bloch-sphere
- The maximally mixed state is the origin: it is the sum of any pair of antipodal pure states (Lec7, p. 1) → bloch-sphere
- Any mixed state is a point inside the sphere. A mixture of two pure states is a point on the segment between them (Lec7, p. 1) → bloch-sphere
- Geometric argument that every mixed state of one qubit is a mixture of only two pure states: draw a line from any chosen pure state through the interior point, and the other point where it exits the surface is the second pure state (Lec7, p. 1) → bloch-sphere
- Experimentalists like the Bloch sphere because it mirrors spin-1/2 particles: you can measure spin along any axis, and the true "state" is just a point on the sphere. He calls it remarkable that the sphere's three dimensions match physical space (Lec7, pp. 1-2) → bloch-sphere
- Gates as rotations: the X, Y, Z gates are half-turns about their axes, and the square-root and eighth-root gates (such as the phase/T-type gates) are quarter- and eighth-turns (Lec7, p. 2). ⚠️ The extraction lost the symbols, so which gate names go with which turn fraction is not recoverable from this text → bloch-sphere

### single-qubit-gates
- Quantum states change by linear transformations; a transformation is unitary if it preserves the 2-norm (equivalently it preserves inner products). The rows (or columns) of a unitary form an orthonormal basis (Lec3, pp. 2-3) → single-qubit-gates
- Identity and permutation matrices are the only matrices that are both unitary and stochastic (Lec3, p. 2) → single-qubit-gates
- A real unitary is an orthogonal matrix, a product of rotations and reflections. A rotation by an angle is a basic one-qubit unitary; a phase gate maps |0⟩ to |0⟩ and |1⟩ to i|1⟩ (Lec3, pp. 2-3) → single-qubit-gates
- The NOT gate has a square root (√NOT), which needs complex numbers (or an extra dimension) to exist. That is one reason complex amplitudes matter: unitaries can then be applied continuously in time (Lec4, pp. 1-2) → single-qubit-gates
- Hadamard is ubiquitous because it converts between the {0,1} and {+,−} bases; the Hadamard on a basis can be used to measure in another basis (Lec4, p. 1) → single-qubit-gates
- Unitaries are invertible, so unitary evolution never destroys information (his example: burning a book, in principle, preserves its information in the smoke and ash) (Lec4, p. 2) → single-qubit-gates
- Interference example: applying Hadamard twice returns |0⟩ with certainty, because the two paths to |1⟩ cancel (one positive, one negative amplitude). He says anything interesting in quantum mechanics can be explained by interference (Lec3, p. 4; Lec4, p. 3) → single-qubit-gates
- He calls quantum mechanics an "island in theory space": norm-preserving theories other than the 2-norm give nothing interesting, and complex amplitudes work better than reals or quaternions (Lec4, p. 3) → single-qubit-gates

### multi-qubit-states
- Tensor products build bigger vectors from smaller ones; a gate acting on one qubit among many (e.g. "Hadamard the third qubit") means the tensor product of that gate with the identity on all other qubits (Lec3, p. 1; Lec5, p. 3) → multi-qubit-states
- A general 2-qubit state has four amplitudes (on 00, 01, 10, 11); probabilities of outcomes are the squared magnitudes (Lec5, p. 2) → multi-qubit-states
- Partial measurement rule: if you measure only the first qubit, discard the amplitudes incompatible with the outcome and renormalize the rest; the state collapses just as needed to fit the measurement and outcome. He calls it the last basic rule of quantum mechanics in the course (Lec5, p. 2) → multi-qubit-states
- There is no distance limit between qubits: one on Earth and one on the moon form a joint state; only the local qubit can be measured by each party (Lec5, p. 2) → multi-qubit-states
- An n-qubit state needs a vector of 2^n amplitudes. Simulating this classically is hard because the number of amplitudes grows exponentially (Feynman's 1982 motivation). With 300 qubits there are more amplitudes than atoms in the observable universe (Lec16, pp. 1, 3) → multi-qubit-states
- An unentangled pure n-qubit state is a tensor product of n single-qubit states, so it needs only about 2n numbers. A quantum computer whose state never had entanglement would be easy to simulate classically and give no speedup (Lec16, p. 3) → multi-qubit-states
- A classical computer can simulate an n-qubit quantum computer by storing the 2^n amplitudes and updating them for each unitary, so quantum computers do not solve anything uncomputable; they could at most falsify the Extended (not the original) Church-Turing Thesis (Lec16, p. 2) → multi-qubit-states

### entanglement
- A Bell pair (also called singlet or EPR pair) is made by Hadamard on qubit 1 then CNOT from 1 to 2, starting from |00⟩ (Lec5, p. 4) → entanglement
- For pure states, "entangled" simply means not decomposable as a tensor product; "unentangled", "separable" and "product state" are synonyms at that level. Most states are entangled (Lec5, p. 4) → entanglement
- Entanglement is forced by the basic rules and was noticed early. It worried Einstein ("spooky action at a distance"): particles must be close to become entangled, but stay entangled over any distance. It has been demonstrated experimentally over about 150 miles and, via Chinese satellite experiments, a couple of thousand miles (Lec5, p. 4) → entanglement
- Measuring the Bell pair in the standard basis only gives a correlation of two random bits, which is also classical. EPR's sharper point: Alice could measure in another basis (Hadamard first), which leaves Bob's qubit in a different pair of states, and this looks like faster-than-light signalling (Lec5, pp. 4-5) → entanglement
- Resolution hint: Bob's measurement statistics are the same whichever basis Alice measures in (equal probabilities); this leads to the density matrix description and the No-Communication Theorem, so Alice cannot send a signal (Lec5, p. 5; Lec7, p. 3; Lec14, p. 2) → entanglement
- The GHZ state is a 3-qubit analogue of the Bell pair. Unlike three classically correlated bits, whose correlation is visible to any two parties, in GHZ the entanglement is visible only with all three qubits: Alice and Bob alone see only classical correlation (Charlie could have measured unnoticed, by no-communication). Analogy: Borromean rings (Lec10, pp. 4-5) → entanglement
- Monogamy of entanglement: if Alice's qubit is maximally entangled with Bob's, it cannot also be maximally entangled with Charlie's. In the W state, neither pair is maximally entangled, though there is some entanglement between Alice-Bob and Alice-Charlie (Lec10, p. 5) → entanglement
- Two qubits need not interact directly to become entangled (an intermediary qubit, or teleportation, can do it). In practice, experiments usually create the entangled pair between Alice and Bob and send one qubit to each (Lec10, p. 4) → entanglement
- Role of entanglement in computing: one can develop the theory without mentioning it, but any interesting quantum computation has it "along for the ride" (Lec16, p. 3) → entanglement

### two-qubit-gates
- Controlled-NOT (CNOT) flips the second (target) bit if and only if the first (control) bit is 1 (Lec5, p. 3) → two-qubit-gates
- Gates built from tensor products (e.g. NOT on one bit, identity on the other) cannot make one qubit affect another; the CNOT is the example where the first qubit affects the second, and such operations are required for interaction (Lec5, p. 3) → two-qubit-gates
- Hadamard on qubit 1 followed by CNOT (control 1, target 2) starting from |00⟩ gives the Bell pair, so CNOT turns a product state into an entangled one (Lec5, p. 4) → two-qubit-gates
- CNOT is not a counterexample to no-cloning: it copies only when the control is a basis state |0⟩ or |1⟩. On a superposition it creates a Bell pair, an entangled "copy", which differs from two independent copies (Lec7, p. 4) → two-qubit-gates
- CNOT alone is classically reversible but only expresses functions that are affine mod 2 (no AND or OR); no 2-bit reversible gate is universal classically (Lec16, p. 6) → two-qubit-gates
- The set {CNOT} cannot create superposition, since it maps basis states to basis states (Lec16, p. 6) → two-qubit-gates
- {CNOT, Hadamard} can create entangled superpositions but has only real entries, so it is not universal (Lec16, pp. 6-7) → two-qubit-gates
- Every unitary on any number of qubits decomposes into 1- and 2-qubit gates (a theorem not proved in the lecture) (Lec16, p. 3) → two-qubit-gates

### quantum-circuits
- Circuit notation tracks which qubits there are and what operations act on them; example: start in |0⟩, Hadamard, Hadamard, then measure (implicitly in the {0,1} basis) (Lec4, p. 3) → quantum-circuits
- Wires are never branched into several wires, since that is not a unitary operation; to enlarge the system, add a new qubit, called an ancilla (Lec4, p. 3) → quantum-circuits
- Example circuit with control and target: start with two |0⟩, Hadamard on qubit 1, CNOT with qubit 1 as control and qubit 2 as target, giving the Bell pair (Lec5, p. 4) → quantum-circuits
- A quantum computer is, in picture, a quantum circuit on n qubits (maybe a million or more) with gates acting on 1 or 2 qubits each, followed by measurement of some or all qubits (Lec16, p. 2) → quantum-circuits
- Why gates act on few qubits: physics gives local interactions, as classical circuits use small gates (AND, OR, NOT). A giant unitary that solves 3SAT exists formally, but it has to be built from small components (Lec16, p. 2) → quantum-circuits
- Any unitary can be built with exponentially many simple gates; the real question is which can be implemented with polynomially many, taken as the "feasible" ones (Lec16, pp. 2-3) → quantum-circuits
- Quantum Zeno effect: repeated measurements in slightly rotated bases can drag a qubit from |0⟩ to |1⟩ with the failure probability (by union bound) made arbitrarily small by using more steps. Attributed to one discoverer being Alan Turing (Lec4, pp. 3-4) → quantum-circuits
- Watched pot effect: a qubit that would drift from |0⟩ to |1⟩ is kept at |0⟩ with high probability if it is measured often enough in the {0,1} basis (Lec4, p. 4) → quantum-circuits
- Elitzur-Vaidman bomb (early 1990s): with a qubit probe and repeated small rotations, you can learn whether a bomb (which measures the probe in the {0,1} basis) is present while setting it off with only tiny probability; the catch is that the object must be "quantumly interrogable" (Lec4, pp. 4-5) → quantum-circuits
- Interference is the central quantum resource: the aim is to arrange amplitudes so wrong answers cancel and the right answer reinforces; without interference one could use a classical randomized computer (Lec16, p. 3) → quantum-circuits

### no-cloning
- No-cloning theorem: a procedure that reliably copies an unknown quantum state cannot exist. He calls it easy to prove but fundamental (Lec7, p. 3) → no-cloning
- Clarification: a known procedure that prepares a state can simply be rerun; the theorem concerns a state that is given and otherwise unknown (Lec7, p. 3) → no-cloning
- Proof 1 (linearity): a cloner would map a state with a blank ancilla to two copies, which is quadratic in the amplitudes, so it is not linear and cannot be unitary (Lec7, p. 3) → no-cloning
- Proof 2 (inner products): unitaries preserve inner products, but cloning squares the inner product; this works only if the inner product is 0 or 1. So a cloner can only copy states from one orthonormal basis; for any known orthonormal basis, its vectors can be cloned (Lec7, p. 4) → no-cloning
- Motivation: if Bob could make unlimited copies, he could do state tomography to find which basis Alice measured, giving faster-than-light signalling, contradicting no-communication. So this is also an indirect proof (Lec7, p. 3) → no-cloning
- Classical analog: you cannot simulate a second independent flip of a coin of unknown bias given only the outcome of the first, since that map would not be stochastic (it is non-linear) (Lec7, p. 4) → no-cloning
- Consequences: classical information can be copied; no arbitrary copying of a physical system (science-fiction teleporting oneself) if relevant information is encoded in states outside a known orthogonal basis (Lec7, pp. 4-5). In teleportation, a consequence is that only one copy of the qubit exists (Lec10, p. 1) → no-cloning
- Quantum money (Wiesner, proposed 1969, published in the 1980s) is an application: each bill has a classical serial number and n unentangled qubits, each in one of four states (two bases); the bank keeps a database of the intended states. Verification means measuring each qubit in its proper basis. A counterfeiter guessing bases fails with exponentially small chance. Security against any counterfeiter was only proved much later, by Molina, Vidick and Watrous, after Aaronson asked on Stack Exchange (Lec7, pp. 5-6) → no-cloning
- Practical problem for Wiesner money: qubits decohere quickly (an hour at best in labs), far faster in a wallet. Money needs both unclonability and verifiability (Lec7, p. 5) → no-cloning

### universal-gate-sets
- A classical gate set is universal if it can express every Boolean function; NAND alone is. {AND, OR} is not (only monotone functions) and {NOT, XOR} is not (only affine mod 2 functions) (Lec16, p. 5) → universal-gate-sets
- A reversible gate set is universal if it can express any reversible transformation, using ancilla bits that are returned to their initial state. Toffoli (controlled-controlled-NOT) is universal and can simulate NAND. Fredkin (controlled-SWAP) can also simulate NAND but preserves Hamming weight, so it is not universal in the stronger sense. No 2-bit reversible gate is universal; at least 3 bits are needed (Lec16, pp. 5-6) → universal-gate-sets
- Any classical reversible gate is also a quantum gate, so a quantum circuit can compute anything a similarly sized classical circuit can, by using Toffoli gates (Lec16, p. 6) → universal-gate-sets
- A quantum gate set is universal if composing its gates can approximate any unitary on any number of qubits to any precision. With a finite gate set only approximation is possible, since unitaries are uncountable and circuits countable (Lec16, p. 6) → universal-gate-sets
- Four ways to fail universality: no superposition (e.g. {CNOT}); no entanglement (e.g. {Hadamard}, or any one-qubit gates); only real gates (e.g. {CNOT, Hadamard}); contained in the stabilizer set {CNOT, Hadamard, Phase}. The last is non-obvious: by the Gottesman-Knill theorem, stabilizer circuits on |0...0⟩ can be simulated classically in polynomial time (Lec16, pp. 6-7) → universal-gate-sets
- Whether there are other ways to fail is an open question, one of his favourites, though few care since many universal sets exist (Lec16, p. 7) → universal-gate-sets
- Universal examples: replacing Hadamard in the stabilizer set by almost anything else gives universality; {Toffoli, Hadamard, Phase} is universal (Yaoyun Shi, 2002); a random 2-qubit gate is universal with probability 1 (Lec16, p. 7) → universal-gate-sets
- Counting argument (Shannon, 1949, classical): there are too many n-bit Boolean functions and too few small circuits, so almost every function needs exponentially large circuits, without giving an example. The quantum version compares dimensions: even diagonal n-qubit unitaries need about 2^n parameters, while a circuit with few gates has few continuous parameters. So almost all unitaries need exponentially many gates, even approximately (Lec16, pp. 3-4) → universal-gate-sets
- Solovay-Kitaev theorem: for a universal gate set closed under inverses, any unitary can be approximated to precision epsilon with a number of gates only polylogarithmic in 1/epsilon (so a power of log(1/epsilon)), found by a reasonably fast algorithm. Whether closure under inverses is needed is open; special number-theoretic gate sets achieve the information-theoretic optimum (Lec16, p. 8) → universal-gate-sets

### quantum-teleportation
- Teleportation (1991): Alice and Bob use a pre-shared entangled pair plus classical communication to send a qubit perfectly. It does not move qubits instantaneously; that would violate causality. Cost: one ebit plus two classical bits per qubit (Lec10, p. 1) → quantum-teleportation
- Protocol: Alice has the unknown qubit and shares a Bell pair with Bob. She applies CNOT (unknown qubit as control, her half of the pair as target), Hadamards the unknown qubit, and measures both her qubits. She tells Bob the two result bits, and he applies X or Z corrections depending on them (Lec10, p. 2) → quantum-teleportation
- Before hearing from Alice, Bob's qubit is maximally mixed, in line with the No-Communication Theorem, so classical communication is necessary (Lec10, pp. 1-2) → quantum-teleportation
- Alice does not need to know the state, and her measurement is destructive, so she is left without it, in agreement with no-cloning. The Bell pair is used up (Lec10, pp. 1, 3) → quantum-teleportation
- Aaronson on "infinite information": the amplitudes could be arbitrary complex numbers, but Bob obtains only what he could measure; amplitudes act more like probabilities than like lengths. The same holds for classical coin biases that encode Shakespeare (Lec10, p. 3) → quantum-teleportation
- The teleported qubit can be half of another Bell pair, so an arbitrary n-qubit entangled state can be teleported qubit by qubit using n ebits (Lec10, pp. 3-4) → quantum-teleportation
- Entanglement swapping: Alice with two entangled qubits and two Bell pairs shared with Bob can teleport both qubits; they become entangled at Bob's end though never in causal contact. It has been used in loophole-free Bell tests, and teleportation itself has been shown in many experiments (Lec10, p. 4) → quantum-teleportation
- He notes protocols like this are hard to discover: quantum mechanics dates to 1926, teleportation to the 1990s. Sometimes trying to prove impossibility leads to a protocol (Lec10, p. 3) → quantum-teleportation

### superdense-coding
- Superdense coding is the first protocol that needs entanglement: Alice sends two classical bits to Bob by sending one qubit, provided they share entanglement beforehand (Lec9, p. 1) → superdense-coding
- Without prior entanglement, Holevo's theorem limits Alice to one classical bit per qubit (intuition: Bob can measure only once in one basis, losing the rest of the state). Not proved in the lecture (Lec9, p. 1) → superdense-coding
- Key idea: by acting on her half of a shared Bell pair, Alice can reach four mutually orthogonal states (using identity, NOT, phase flip, or both), which form a basis, so Bob can distinguish them (Lec9, p. 1) → superdense-coding
- Encoding: for two bits, she applies NOT depending on one bit and the phase gate depending on the other, then sends her qubit to Bob (Lec9, p. 1) → superdense-coding
- Decoding: Bob applies the inverse of the Bell-state preparation, CNOT (second qubit controlling the first) then Hadamard on the second qubit, and reads out the two bits in the standard basis (Lec9, p. 2) → superdense-coding
- Optimality: with any amount of shared ebits, one qubit still carries only two classical bits, no more. A factor 2 is a modest speedup, but it breaks the information-theory bound Shannon seemed to give (Lec9, p. 2) → superdense-coding
- Teleportation's resource inequality is nearly the converse of superdense coding's: one ebit + two classical bits gives one qubit (shown optimal) (Lec10, p. 1) → superdense-coding

### bell-inequalities
- Hidden variable theories try to add extra ingredients to the state so that it "merely" predicts what the universe has already fixed for the measurement result (Lec13, p. 1) → bell-inequalities
- Bohmian mechanics (Bohm, 1950s; earlier de Broglie, 1920s) keeps the wavefunction and adds real particle positions guided by a deterministic rule; there are infinitely many such rules reproducing quantum predictions, so experiments can't choose among them. With entangled particles, acting on Alice's particle changes Bob's particle's position instantaneously: non-local, though with no usable signal. Einstein disliked this (Lec13, pp. 1-3) → bell-inequalities
- Einstein wanted local hidden variables: values for all possible measurements fixed at creation, carried locally (like entangled qubits secretly agreeing in advance). Around 1963 Bell drew attention to the non-locality of Bohmian mechanics, and proved all hidden variable theories must be non-local (Lec13, p. 3) → bell-inequalities
- CHSH game (Clauser, Horne, Shimony, Holt, 1969): the referee gives uniformly random bits x and y to Alice and Bob, who are separated and return bits a and b; they win iff a + b = xy mod 2. They may agree on a strategy and share randomness beforehand (Lec13, pp. 3-4) → bell-inequalities
- Classical optimum: 75% (e.g. always output 0, losing only when x=y=1). Proof: randomized strategies are mixtures of deterministic ones (convexity), and no deterministic a(x), b(y) satisfy all four cases. Bell's inequality, in this framework, is exactly this 75% bound (Lec13, p. 4) → bell-inequalities
- With a pre-shared Bell pair and measuring in different bases chosen by x and y, they win with probability about 85% in all four input cases. Because the argument for the 75% bound holds for any local hidden variable story, winning more than 75% rules out local realism, without assuming quantum mechanics (Lec14, pp. 1-2) → bell-inequalities
- Alice can be assumed to measure first by the No-Communication Theorem. Bob's density matrix is unchanged by Alice's measurement, so there is no signalling, though conditioning on her result gives Bob a pure state, which is also true classically for conditioned predictions (Lec14, p. 2) → bell-inequalities
- Aaronson's hierarchy: classical local realism at the bottom, a faster-than-light "science-fiction utopia" at the top; quantum mechanics sits in between (no local realism, no FTL communication). A classical simulation of our universe would need FTL communication, which he calls key evidence that the world is quantum (Lec14, pp. 2-3) → bell-inequalities
- Tsirelson's inequality (1980s): about 85% is optimal even with unlimited entanglement. The full proof is not given; he shows it for strategies of the same type by bounding with unit vectors and a parallelogram inequality. Some theories beyond quantum mechanics (violating Tsirelson but with no FTL) have been studied, with e.g. one-bit communication solving scheduling (Lec14, pp. 3-4) → bell-inequalities
- Experiments: Aspect and others in the 1980s ran CHSH-type tests consistent with quantum mechanics; they did not reach 85%, but showed over 80% with high confidence. Two loopholes: detector inefficiency, and locality (communication at light speed before results are recorded). Each was closed separately by the 2000s; in 2016 several teams closed both at once (Lec14, p. 5) → bell-inequalities
- Superdeterminism is the leftover way to keep classical local realism: a cosmic conspiracy rigs measurement choices. 't Hooft supports it; Aaronson is "on board with entanglement" (Lec14, pp. 5-6) → bell-inequalities
- Other nonlocal games where entanglement wins: odd cycle game (the quantum strategy beats the classical best) and Mermin's magic square game (a quantum strategy with 2 ebits wins with probability 1, classically impossible by a parity argument) (Lec14, pp. 6-7) → bell-inequalities

## quantum-algorithms

### query-model-and-oracles
- Two ways to measure the complexity of quantum algorithms: circuit complexity (number of gates in the smallest circuit for a unitary) and query complexity (number of calls to an oracle or black-box function) (Lec17, p. 1) → query-model-and-oracles
- Circuit complexity is extremely hard to pin down (only upper bounds, lower bounds conjectured from hardness assumptions); the difficulty is not quantum-specific, e.g. the smallest circuit for Boolean satisfiability is a similarly hard question tied to P vs NP. Query complexity gives a more precise picture in a more limited model (Lec17, p. 1) → query-model-and-oracles
- A quantum oracle for a Boolean function cannot simply map |x⟩ to |f(x)⟩ since that is not unitary; it needs an answer (target) register: the input register is left unchanged and f(x) is XORed into the answer register, which with an answer register starting at 0 mimics the classical oracle (Lec17, pp. 1-2) → query-model-and-oracles
- When f is Boolean, it is often most convenient to use a "phase oracle" that writes f(x) into the sign of the amplitude of |x⟩ (optionally controlled by an extra bit that decides whether the query happens). It has no classical counterpart but is very useful for setting up interference patterns (Lec17, p. 2) → query-model-and-oracles
- The XOR oracle and the phase oracle are equivalent: either simulates the other with no extra queries (Lec17, p. 2) → query-model-and-oracles
- In the query model one abstracts a problem as learning a property of a function f (does some input give 1? is f 1 for a majority of inputs? is it periodic or far from periodic?) using only evaluations, not the internals of how f is computed. Gates before or after queries are treated as free (Lec17, pp. 2-3) → query-model-and-oracles
- Why the black-box model is studied: a black box is basically a huge input string, a query is a lookup of the i-th entry; or, a subroutine that one may call on a superposition of inputs without looking at its code (Lec17, p. 3) → query-model-and-oracles
- Justification of the model: reversibility means a reversible map |x⟩ to |f(x)⟩ requires f to be injective. Even for an efficiently computable injective f, the map |x⟩ to |f(x)⟩ need not be efficient, since for an injective one-way function, running the circuit backwards would invert f. The XOR-style map |x,a⟩ to |x,a⊕f(x)⟩ keeps x, so it does not break the one-way function (Lec17, pp. 3-4) → query-model-and-oracles
- Garbage from simulating a non-reversible circuit with Toffoli gates is a real problem for quantum algorithms because garbage entangled with the useful qubit prevents the interference patterns (looking at the useful qubit alone gives a maximally mixed state) (Lec17, p. 4) → query-model-and-oracles
- Uncomputing (Bennett, 1970s): run the circuit forward, CNOT the answer into a fresh register, then run the circuit in reverse; this leaves the answer and removes the garbage. Copying is allowed despite no-cloning because the answer is classical. Hence if f is computable by a small circuit, the XOR query is implementable too (Lec17, p. 5) → query-model-and-oracles
- Quantum access to f (the ability to apply the unitary taking |x,a⟩ to |x,a⊕f(x)⟩) is a real assumption; it mattered little in Shor's algorithm since only one query was made and the result discarded, but matters for Grover (Lec22, p. 1) → query-model-and-oracles
- Queries are a good yardstick for lower bounds: BBBV shows about √N queries are needed for unordered search, and a whole theory of quantum query lower bounds grew from it (hybrid argument, polynomial method) (Lec23, p. 4; Lec24, p. 4) → query-model-and-oracles
- Polynomial method (Beals et al. 1998): the acceptance probability of a t-query quantum algorithm is a real polynomial in the input bits of degree at most 2t, so a lower bound on the degree of any polynomial approximating f gives a query lower bound (at least half the degree). This turns quantum query lower bounds into pure math about polynomials (Lec24, p. 4) → query-model-and-oracles
- Using it, any quantum algorithm for n-bit Parity needs at least about n/2 queries (matching Deutsch-pairs approach), and Majority needs order n queries (no asymptotic speedup) (Lec24, p. 4) → query-model-and-oracles

### phase-kickback
- Phase-oracle trick: if the answer register is put in the state (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2 before an XOR query, the query leaves the answer register unchanged and multiplies the input basis state |x⟩ by (−1)^f(x), turning the XOR oracle into a phase oracle. If the register is instead in |0⟩+|1⟩, nothing happens (Lec17, p. 2) → phase-kickback
- Conversely, a phase oracle with its target in a state like |0⟩ or |1⟩ in a controlled setting simulates a XOR oracle, swapping |0⟩ and |1⟩ iff f(x)=1 (Lec17, p. 2) → phase-kickback
- Deutsch's algorithm explicitly uses this: "we're using the result from earlier in this lecture, that we can use a single 'ordinary' query to simulate the effect of a single phase query" (Lec17, pp. 5-6) → phase-kickback
- The term "phase kickback" itself does not appear in these lectures; the idea is presented as the XOR-oracle/phase-oracle equivalence, and the answer qubit is often ignored by "moving the phases around" (Lec17, p. 2) → phase-kickback
- Aaronson uses phase oracles in Bernstein-Vazirani (Lec18, p. 1) and Grover (flipping the amplitude of the marked item; "phase oracles are more convenient for the purposes of Grover's algorithm") (Lec22, p. 3) → phase-kickback

### deutsch-jozsa
- Deutsch's algorithm (mid-1980s, first quantum algorithm proposed): given two unknown bits, computes their parity (are they different?) with one superposed query, where classically two queries are needed. Hadamard |0⟩, apply a phase query, factor out a global phase, Hadamard again, measure: outcome depends on whether f(0)=f(1) (Lec17, pp. 5-6) → deutsch-jozsa
- Parity of an n-bit string can be obtained with n/2 queries by splitting into 2-bit blocks, using Deutsch on each block, and computing the final parity classically (Lec17, p. 6; Lec24, p. 4) → deutsch-jozsa
- Deutsch-Jozsa problem: black box for a Boolean function on n bits, promised constant (all 0 or all 1) or balanced (equally many 0s and 1s); decide which (Lec17, p. 6) → deutsch-jozsa
- Classically, deterministically, one needs just over half the inputs examined (an exact answer) in the worst case; with randomness a constant number of samples (like 5 or 6) gives small error (Lec17, p. 6) → deutsch-jozsa
- The quantum algorithm solves it perfectly (zero error) with one query, which is impossible classically. But since randomized classical algorithms are nearly as fast and fine in practice, the speedup is unimpressive; this explains why until 1994 most people did not care about quantum computing (Lec17, pp. 6-7) → deutsch-jozsa
- Circuit pattern: put everything in superposition (Hadamard on all qubits), query f as a phase oracle, apply a change of basis (another round of Hadamards), measure. "If you can't figure out what to do next ... a round of Hadamards is always a good guess" (Lec17, p. 7) → deutsch-jozsa
- Key fact used: Hadamard on all n qubits maps basis state |x⟩ to an equal superposition with a −1 phase for each position where x and y are both 1, i.e. phase (−1)^(x·y) on |y⟩ (Lec17, p. 7) → deutsch-jozsa
- Analysis: look at the amplitude of the all-zero outcome. If f is constant it is +1 or −1 (so we see all-zeros with certainty); if f is balanced it is 0 (all contributions cancel). So all-zeros means constant, any other outcome means balanced (Lec17, pp. 7-8) → deutsch-jozsa

### bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Bernstein-Vazirani problem: f(x) = s·x mod 2 for a secret string s; find s. Classically, querying the strings of Hamming weight one gets one bit per query, and no classical algorithm can do better since each query gives one bit about s (Lec18, p. 1) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Quantum algorithm uses one query: n qubits in |0⟩, Hadamard all, phase-query f, Hadamard all, measure in the standard basis, which outputs s exactly (Lec18, pp. 1-2) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Why it works: after the query the state is a uniform superposition with signs (−1)^(s·x), which is exactly the Hadamard transform of |s⟩, so a second Hadamard maps it back to |s⟩. Only for outcome s do all 2^n contributions point the same way; all others interfere destructively. A similar story can be told for almost every quantum algorithm (Lec18, pp. 1-2) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Aaronson admits problems like BV were designed around what a quantum computer can do ("this is actually pretty common in this field"); the 1-query vs n-query gap is also modest in gate count, and the classical problem (n queries) is still easy (Lec18, pp. 2-3) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Simon's problem (1994): oracle f from n bits to n bits with a hidden XOR mask s, f(x)=f(y) iff y = x XOR s; find s. Simon aimed to prove that no true exponential quantum speedup exists, and instead found one (for an artificial black-box problem), which was central to Shor's work (Lec18, p. 2) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Only whether two inputs give the same output matters, not the output values; the problem is hard classically for e.g. 1000-bit inputs (Lec18, p. 3) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Classical upper bound: a deterministic algorithm needs about 2^(n-1) queries, a randomized one about 2^(n/2) via the birthday paradox (23 people give about 50% for two sharing a birthday; the number of pairs matters, which grows quadratically) (Lec18, p. 3) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- The birthday attack is used in cryptanalysis to find hash collisions with a number of queries about the square root of the range size (Lec18, p. 3) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Classical lower bound by an adversary argument: the adversary picks a random s and random consistent f; by Yao's minimax principle one can assume a deterministic algorithm; unless a collision is found, the remaining candidates for s are equally likely, and with union bound no collision appears in the first ~2^(n/2) queries. So the randomized lower bound is about 2^(n/2) (Lec18, p. 4) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Simon's algorithm: Hadamard all n input qubits, query f into a separate n-qubit answer register, measure the answer register (the answers themselves are not what we care about; they only create the interference pattern). The input register is left in an equal superposition of the two inputs x and x XOR s consistent with the observed output (Lec18, pp. 4-5) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Measuring that state directly gives only one of the two inputs, which tells nothing; repeating gives a different pair. Instead, Hadamard all qubits and measure: the outcome is a uniformly random n-bit string y with y·s = 0 (mod 2). One run gives one linear equation on s (Lec18, pp. 5-6) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Repeat about n times to get a system of linear equations over the field with two elements; solve by Gaussian elimination classically (polynomial time). After slightly more than n-1 equations, w.h.p. the only nonzero solution is s. Total: about n queries plus polynomial side computation, versus about 2^(n/2) classical queries (Lec18, pp. 6-7) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Simon's algorithm can be made deterministic (succeed with certainty), details not given (Lec18, p. 7) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- It is unclear that there is any natural application: if the black box is implemented by a concrete function like a rank-(n-1) Boolean matrix, a classical algorithm that knows the matrix can compute its nullspace in polynomial time; no known straightforward class of efficiently computable functions satisfies the Simon promise and is plausibly hard classically even given the code (Lec18, p. 7) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Anecdote: Simon's paper was rejected, but Peter Shor saw that a change would give period finding, leading to Shor's algorithm (Lec18, p. 7) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon

### quantum-fourier-transform
- Why Fourier: any time one wants the period of a periodic signal, the Fourier transform is the tool. The Q-dimensional Fourier transform is the Q×Q matrix whose (i,j) entry is ω^(ij)/√Q, ω a Qth root of unity (Lec20, p. 1) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Intuition (clocks and thumbtacks): for someone with a 26-hour day, a clock of matching period (or divisor) always moves the thumbtack the same direction (constructive interference); other clocks cancel out (destructive). The QFT is the same with amplitudes instead of thumbtacks (Lec20, pp. 1-2) → quantum-fourier-transform
- F_2 is the Hadamard gate; for F_4 and F_8 the explicit matrices are shown (Lec20, pp. 2-3) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Classical FFT: applying a general Q×Q matrix takes about Q² operations, but the Fourier matrix can be applied in O(Q log Q) via a recursion: F_Q is made of blocks F_{Q/2}, A F_{Q/2}, with A a diagonal phase matrix (powers of ω), after rearranging columns into even and odd (Lec20, pp. 3-4) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Quantum goal: apply F_Q to a state of log2 Q qubits in time polynomial in log Q. This is less ambitious (the result need not be written out in memory, only encoded in amplitudes) but more ambitious (polylog rather than Q log Q) (Lec20, p. 5) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Circuit by the FFT recursion: recursively apply F_{Q/2} on all but the least significant bit, apply a controlled-A (a rotation depending on the bits, each bit contributing a rotation half as big as the previous one, with a linear number of gates), then a Hadamard on the last bit. The recursion behaves linearly on quantum states (Lec20, pp. 4-5) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Qubits come out of the circuit in reverse order, to be reordered once at the end (Lec20, p. 6) → quantum-fourier-transform
- The exponentially tiny rotations can be omitted (a theorem): this makes the circuit O(q log q) instead of O(q²) gates, answering the objection that Shor's algorithm is "unphysical" for needing tiny rotations (Lec20, p. 5) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Applied to the period-finding state, an equal superposition of |r⟩, |r+s⟩, |r+2s⟩, …, the QFT gives amplitudes that are sums of roots of unity; whether the contributions interfere constructively or destructively depends on whether js is a multiple of Q (Lec20, pp. 6-7) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Easy case (s divides Q): outcomes seen are exactly integer multiples of Q/s; a few samples and their gcd give Q/s and thus s (Lec20, p. 7) → quantum-fourier-transform
- General case (s does not divide Q): interference is imperfect but good enough; outcomes j nearest an integer multiple of Q/s show mostly constructive interference, others mostly destructive (Lec20, pp. 7-8; Lec21, p. 1) → quantum-fourier-transform

### shor-algorithm
- Shor's algorithm factors N = pq in time polynomial in log N. RSA, one of the most widely used public-key systems, relies on the assumption that factoring is hard (Lec19, p. 1) → shor-algorithm
- RSA sketch: a site picks two huge secret primes p and q (say a thousand digits each), publishes N = pq. Anyone can encrypt with the public key; knowing p and q gives the order of the multiplicative group mod N, which allows efficient decryption (e.g. extracting roots mod N). An eavesdropper seems to face an exponentially hard problem, though it is proven neither that factoring is hard nor that breaking RSA is as hard as factoring (Lec19, p. 1) → shor-algorithm
- Some number theorists conjecture factoring is in P or are agnostic; the problem has been seriously studied for only about 40 years. If you can factor, you can break RSA (Lec19, p. 1) → shor-algorithm
- Trial division is exponential time in the number of digits; the Quadratic Field Sieve (1981) is a milder exponential and the Number Field Sieve better still (its correctness depends on an unproven conjecture). This is why 512-bit, 768-bit and maybe even 1024-bit keys are no longer quite secure against well-funded attackers (Lec19, p. 1; p. 2) → shor-algorithm
- Shor's algorithm also solves discrete logarithm in polynomial time, breaking Diffie-Hellman (Lec19, p. 2; Lec21, p. 3) → shor-algorithm
- Under the hood it is period finding: given oracle access to f with an unknown period s (f(x)=f(y) iff x−y is a multiple of s), find s. Shor observed that a fast period-finding algorithm gives fast factoring/discrete log by classical number theory unrelated to quantum mechanics (Lec19, p. 2) → shor-algorithm
- Classically, period finding needs about √s queries (a birthday-style collision search); with collisions, the gcd of differences yields the period; gcd is computed in polynomial time by Euclid's algorithm. Quantumly, the period-finding core uses O(1) queries and polynomial extra steps (Lec19, pp. 2-3) → shor-algorithm
- Two ingredients: a classical reduction from factoring to period finding and a quantum period-finding algorithm (Lec19, p. 3) → shor-algorithm
- Reduction uses the multiplicative group mod N (elements coprime to N); its size is Euler's totient φ(N), which for N=pq is (p−1)(q−1). Every element's order divides the group size; Fermat's little theorem and Euler's theorem follow (Lec19, pp. 3-4) → shor-algorithm
- If one knew φ(N) = N − (p+q) + 1, one would know pq and p+q and could solve a quadratic for p and q. But the period of a^x mod N only divides φ(N), so one proceeds differently (Lec19, p. 4) → shor-algorithm
- Pick a random a coprime to N (if gcd(a,N)≠1, Euclid already factors N). The function x → a^x mod N is efficiently computable by repeated squaring (polynomial in log N, not exponentially many multiplications) and is periodic. Ironically, the same number theory behind RSA gives Shor's algorithm (Lec19, p. 4) → shor-algorithm
- Let r be the period. If r is even, a^r − 1 = (a^(r/2) − 1)(a^(r/2) + 1) is a multiple of N; if neither factor is a multiple of N, then gcd of N with one of them gives a nontrivial factor. Both "luck" conditions hold with probability at least a constant (at least half, stated as a bound for random a coprime to N) over the choice of a; so a few different a's suffice (Lec19, pp. 4-5) → shor-algorithm
- Quantum part: Hadamard the first register to make an equal superposition over integers below Q (Q the smallest power of 2 above the chosen bound, ~N²), compute a^x mod N into ancilla qubits using a circuit of Toffoli gates (built by repeated squaring; unlike Simon, the function is not an abstract black box), measure the second register and discard it (Lec19, pp. 5-6; Lec21, p. 2) → shor-algorithm
- What remains is an equal superposition of |r⟩, |r+s⟩, |r+2s⟩, ... with an unknown offset r. Measuring directly gives nothing useful, since repetition gives a different offset; the QFT is needed to turn this into information about the period (Lec19, p. 6) → shor-algorithm
- After the QFT and measurement, each outcome l is close to an integer multiple k·Q/s; the continued-fraction algorithm recovers k/s from l/Q (Lec21, pp. 1-2) → shor-algorithm
- Continued fractions: repeatedly pull out the integer part and invert the remainder until the leftover is small enough to discard (example: 3.14 ≈ 3 + 1/(7+...) ). The expansion approximates l/Q by a rational with small denominator (Lec21, pp. 1-2) → shor-algorithm
- Why it works: l/Q is within about 1/Q of k/s; s ≤ N because the order of any element is at most the group size, and Q ≈ N² is chosen so that the rational approximation with denominator at most N is essentially unique and efficiently findable (Lec21, p. 2) → shor-algorithm
- If k and s share a factor, only the reduced fraction is seen; repeat several times and take the least common multiple of the denominators to get s with high probability (Lec21, pp. 2-3) → shor-algorithm
- Complexity claim: the period-finding speedup (from about √s queries to O(1)) is provable; the speedup for factoring itself is only conjectural, as no one has proved P≠NP and the best classical factoring methods (QFS, NFS) exploit extra structure and beat the √s approach. What can be proven is an exponential speedup over classical factoring algorithms that go through black-box period finding (Lec20, p. 1) → shor-algorithm
- Pop-science misconception: "quantum computers factor by trying all divisors in parallel" is wrong; the algorithm is subtler and exploits interference (Lec21, p. 3) → shor-algorithm
- Other uses: it breaks elliptic-curve cryptosystems (modified shortly after discovery), and in general any problem of finding hidden structure in abelian groups; almost all current public-key systems rely on such structure (Lec21, p. 3) → shor-algorithm
- Attempts to extend to non-abelian groups were aimed at graph isomorphism (would follow from a symmetric-group version) and lattice problems (Regev 2005: dihedral group). Many have given up as it is very hard. Graph isomorphism no longer admits an exponential quantum speedup since Babai (2016) gave a quasipolynomial classical algorithm (Lec21, pp. 3-4) → shor-algorithm
- RSA and Diffie-Hellman were first discovered in secret by Clifford Cocks at GCHQ (Lec19, p. 2) → shor-algorithm

### grover-search
- Grover's algorithm (1995, Bell Labs, soon after Shor): quadratic rather than exponential speedup, but for a much wider range of problems (Lec22, p. 1) → grover-search
- Problem: oracle f from {1..N} to {0,1}; is there an x with f(x)=1, and find one (unordered search). Classically Ω(N) queries are needed; deterministically even N−1 empty boxes does not settle it; with a guaranteed treasure about N/2 on average (Lec22, p. 1) → grover-search
- Grover uses O(√N) queries, O(log N) qubits and about √N log N gates, assuming quantum access to f as a unitary (Lec22, p. 1) → grover-search
- Application 1: NP-complete problems; with N = 2^n and f(x)=φ(x) for a formula φ, time about 2^(n/2) poly(n). It is a conjectural speedup (P=NP would remove the point) and at most quadratic. For CircuitSAT nothing beats brute force classically; for structured problems like 3SAT classical algorithms (about 1.3^n) beat brute force, so one must combine Grover with classical tricks (Lec22, pp. 1-2) → grover-search
- Application 2: searching a physical database; the speedup is provable (no hardness assumptions), but it needs a quantum RAM (superposed queries), a separate hardware problem, and it is unclear whether building one without n active parallel elements is possible (Lec22, p. 2) → grover-search
- Algorithm (single marked item x*): Hadamard n qubits for a uniform superposition; repeat: apply the phase oracle (flips the sign of the marked item's amplitude), then the Grover diffusion operator D (reflects all amplitudes about their mean). One iteration raises the marked amplitude to roughly 3/√N, then 5/√N, 7/√N, ... (adding about 2/√N each time); about √N steps reach amplitude about 1 (Lec22, pp. 2-4) → grover-search
- Classically after t queries the success probability is t/N; in Grover's algorithm it is on the order of t²/N, hence the quadratic speedup (Lec22, p. 4) → grover-search
- Source of the √N: amplitudes are square roots of probabilities; each query adds about 1/√N amplitude instead of 1/N probability (Lec22, p. 4) → grover-search
- Implementing D: in the Hadamard basis D is a diagonal phase flip on every basis state except all-zeros, implementable with ancillas and uncomputing; D is that operation conjugated by Hadamards (Lec22, pp. 4-5) → grover-search
- Geometric picture: the uniform state |Ψ⟩ and the marked state |x*⟩ are nearly orthogonal (overlap 1/√N) and span a 2D plane that the whole algorithm stays in. The query reflects about the axis orthogonal to |x*⟩ (inverts its component) and D reflects about |Ψ⟩; two reflections make a rotation by about 2/√N per iteration. After about (π/4)√N iterations the state is almost |x*⟩ (Lec22, pp. 5-6) → grover-search
- Overshooting: running too long makes the success probability fall again; it is sinusoidal (sin² of the angle), like a soufflé that rises and falls repeatedly. Stopping early and restarting is the dilemma; minimizing the expected queries is optimal slightly before certainty (Lec22, pp. 6-7) → grover-search
- With k marked items the cycle is faster: peak at about (π/4)√(N/k) queries (Lec22, p. 7) → grover-search
- Unknown number of marked items: run a random number of iterations between 0 and about 2√N so that a constant success probability (say 40-60%) results, repeat O(1) times; the candidate can be verified classically by evaluating f. Also finds a marked item in O(√(N/k)) by guessing k = N, N/2, N/4, ... (geometrically increasing cost), or by a classical trick of sampling N/k items to get exactly one marked item with probability about 1/e (Lec22, pp. 8-9) → grover-search
- If there are no marked items, nothing happens and the state stays uniform; the decision version can never be 100% certain but after O(1) repetitions failing to find an item implies, with high confidence, none exists, again with O(√N) queries (Lec22, p. 8) → grover-search
- BBBV theorem (Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard, Vazirani 1994): Grover is asymptotically optimal; it predates Grover, so the algorithm was proven optimal before it was discovered. BBBV originally aimed to show no quantum search speedup is possible (Lec23, p. 1) → grover-search
- Hybrid-argument proof sketch: run the algorithm on the all-zero oracle; the sum over all items of the "query magnitudes" is T (the number of queries), so some item x* has query magnitude at most T/N. Make x* marked in the last queries; by Cauchy-Schwarz, linearity and norm preservation, the final state changes by at most about 2T/√N in Euclidean distance, so the success probability is at most O(T²/N), which forces T ≈ √N. The same proof works with intermediate measurements and unlimited workspace (Lec23, pp. 1-4) → grover-search
- Consequence: any approach that treats an NP problem as a black box can only get the Grover speedup (Lec24, p. 5; Lec25, p. 1) → grover-search
- Applications: OR of ANDs (square table, is some row all 1s?): classically near N; nested ("recursive") Grover gives about √N (with log factors for error reduction; later removed); lower bound proven by Ambainis's new method. Generalized to game trees (chess, Go), where Grover's advantage seemed to shrink with depth, but Farhi, Goldstone, Gutmann (2007) and others showed any game tree with N leaves can be evaluated in O(√N) on a quantum computer (optimal). Shannon's ~10^43 chess positions would reduce to ~10^21.5 (Lec23, pp. 4-6) → grover-search
- Collision problem (given a two-to-one f, find x≠y with f(x)=f(y)): classical randomized Θ(√N) via the birthday attack; quantumly BHT (Brassard, Hoyer, Tapp 1997) gets about N^(1/3): query about N^(1/3) random inputs classically and sort, then Grover over N^(2/3) further inputs, marking x if f(x) matches a stored value; balancing the two phases gives total N^(1/3) (Lec24, pp. 1-2) → grover-search
- Collision lower bound ~N^(1/3) (Aaronson's thesis, later tight by Yaoyun Shi) cannot use BBBV, since turning one-to-one into two-to-one changes half the entries; it used polynomial approximation and symmetry of the problem (Lec24, p. 2) → grover-search
- Element distinctness (is f one-to-one?): classically about N queries; with Grover over blocks about N^(3/4) queries (Buhrman et al. 2000); lower bound ~N^(2/3) by Shi from the collision bound (a reduction using the birthday paradox); matched by Ambainis (2003) with quantum walks, though using about N^(2/3) workspace qubits (Lec24, pp. 2-4) → grover-search
- Counting-type application: approximating the Hamming weight of an n-bit string within ±εn takes about 1/ε² classical samples (tight), but about 1/ε queries quantumly (a quadratic speedup) using Grover (Lec24, p. 5) → grover-search
- Parity and Majority are cited as problems without large speedup or with the exact 'n/2' bound, in contrast to Grover-type speedups (Lec24, p. 4) → grover-search

### hamiltonian-simulation
- Hamiltonians are the instantaneous generators of unitaries: leaving a Hamiltonian "running" for some time gives a unitary. They are Hermitian matrices (H = H†), but unlike density matrices need not be positive or have trace 1 (Lec25, p. 2) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Schrödinger's equation, i·d|Ψ⟩/dt = H|Ψ⟩ (with ħ set to 1), describes the evolution of an isolated pure state in continuous time; its solution is |Ψ(t)⟩ = e^(−iHt)|Ψ(0)⟩ (Lec25, p. 2) → hamiltonian-simulation
- The exponential of a matrix is defined by the Taylor series. A diagonal matrix is exponentiated entrywise; a diagonalizable matrix A = UDU⁻¹ gives e^A = U e^D U⁻¹ (Lec25, pp. 2-3) → hamiltonian-simulation
- For Hermitian H: eigenvalues are real, H is diagonalizable (spectral theorem: H = UDU†), hence e^(−iHt) is unitary. Conversely any unitary can be written as e^(−iHt), but the Hamiltonian is not unique (eigenphases are defined only up to adding 2π) (Lec25, pp. 3-4) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Eigenvalues of a Hamiltonian are energies; eigenvectors are energy eigenstates, which under evolution only pick up a phase e^(−iλt). An arbitrary state is a superposition of energy eigenstates, each rotating at a speed proportional to its energy; he suggests defining energy as the rate of phase pickup. The expected energy is conserved (Lec25, pp. 4-5) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Ground state is the lowest-energy eigenstate (ground state degeneracy if the lowest energies coincide); then first excited state, etc. Systems in nature are often in their ground states because interactions carry off excess energy (as with a ball rolling in a basin, via entropy). Quantum algorithms are by contrast systems that do not just sit in ground states. Hydrogen atom example with photon emission/absorption (Lec25, pp. 5-7) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Hamiltonians can be added (unitaries cannot): H = H0 + H1 means two things going on at once, e.g. the Standard Model Hamiltonian as kinetic + EM + strong + weak terms (Lec25, p. 7) → hamiltonian-simulation
- e^(A+B) = e^A e^B in general fails, but holds if A and B commute (Lec25, p. 8) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Trotterization: approximate e^(A+B) by repeated alternation of tiny steps e^(εA) e^(εB), about 1/ε times; the approximation improves as ε decreases and is exact in the limit. This lets one apply a sum of Hamiltonians using only the ability to apply each term (Lec25, p. 8) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Recipe to simulate a physical system on a quantum computer: (1) discretize non-discrete degrees of freedom, (2) write the total Hamiltonian as a sum of simple terms acting on 1 or 2 particles, (3) Trotterize to get a product of simple unitaries (Lec25, p. 8) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Typical model: a lattice of qubits with H = sum of single-qubit terms plus nearest-neighbour two-qubit terms. e^(−iHt) generically has effects between all qubits for any t (decaying exponentially with distance), an apparent faster-than-light puzzle he resolves by saying this non-relativistic picture is only an approximation; quantum field theory gives exactly zero (Lec25, pp. 8-9) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Finding the ground state of a Hamiltonian that is a sum of few-qubit terms is NP-hard: encode a 3SAT instance as a Hamiltonian with one penalty term (energy 1) per violated clause; ground-state energy is 0 iff the formula is satisfiable. The hardness has nothing to do with quantum mechanics (Lec25, pp. 9-10) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Adiabatic algorithm (Farhi, Goldstone, Gutmann, Sipser 2000): an attempt to exploit NP-complete problem structure for a possibly even exponential quantum speedup. Unlike Shor and Grover, it has no rigorous guarantee of speed on all cases, and it is known not to be fast always; for some optimization instances it might give a huge advantage, for others little or none or is beaten classically. Its practical value is unknown until big quantum computers exist (Lec25, p. 1) → hamiltonian-simulation
- The lecture says the adiabatic algorithm will be set up in the next lecture; the mechanism (slowly interpolating between Hamiltonians) is not described in the lectures provided (Lec25, p. 9 end) → hamiltonian-simulation

### quantum-complexity
- Why the power of quantum computing is not attributable to a single feature: huge number of amplitudes, interference (complex amplitudes, not probabilities), entanglement, and being outside stabilizer operations; remove any one and QM is easy to simulate classically. Together they make full QM hard to simulate, which opens up exponential speedups (Lec17, p. 1) → quantum-complexity
- Classical classes: P (poly-time decision problems; examples linear programming, graph connectivity), NP (yes-answers verifiable in poly time; factoring as a decision problem), NP-hard, NP-complete (examples TSP, 3SAT, Max Clique, Sudoku); P=NP is open (Lec24, p. 5) → quantum-complexity
- BQP (Bernstein-Vazirani 1993): bounded-error quantum polynomial time; decision problems solvable in polynomial time by a quantum computer (Lec24, p. 5) → quantum-complexity
- P ⊆ BQP since Toffoli gates simulate classical computation; Factoring (as a decision problem) is in BQP by Shor and not known to be in P (Lec24, p. 5) → quantum-complexity
- It is not known whether NP ⊆ BQP; whether quantum computers can solve NP-complete problems in polynomial time is a big open problem. BBBV shows no easy proof treating NP as a black box (Lec24, pp. 5-6) → quantum-complexity
- Whether BQP ⊆ NP is also asked; there might be quantum-easy problems with no short classically checkable proof, but no example is known (Lec24, p. 6) → quantum-complexity
- Upper bound: BQP ⊆ PSPACE, since a quantum computer can be simulated in exponential time and polynomial memory (sum over exponentially many contributions to an amplitude, reusing space). So P ⊆ BQP ⊆ PSPACE, and proving P≠BQP would also prove P≠PSPACE, so it is no easier than P≠NP. Better upper bounds exist but use other classes (Lec24, p. 6) → quantum-complexity
- If quantum computers could solve NP-complete problems in polynomial time it would be world-changing; but any approach ignoring problem structure yields only the Grover quadratic speedup (BBBV). Many arXiv papers claiming polynomial-time NP-complete algorithms violate this theorem (arXiv has no peer review) (Lec25, p. 1) → quantum-complexity
- To beat Grover one must exploit structure; if any NP-complete problem were in BQP, all of NP would be. For small speedups the choice of NP-complete problem matters since reductions can eat the gain (Lec25, p. 1) → quantum-complexity
- Misconceptions flagged: "factor by trying all divisors in parallel" is wrong (Lec21, p. 3); a quantum search speedup for pure brute force exists but is quadratic, not exponential (Lec21, p. 3; Lec22, p. 1); Grover's NP speedup is only conjectural (Lec22, p. 2) → quantum-complexity
- Why Shor/Grover differ: Shor uses a "global" interference effect revealing a period, Grover only adds about 1/√N amplitude per query (Lec23, p. 1) → quantum-complexity
- Shor's factoring speedup over classical algorithms is not proven; only the black-box period-finding speedup and the speedup over algorithms that go through it are (Lec20, p. 1) → quantum-complexity
- Quantum speedups in the query model are often modest and artificial for Deutsch-Jozsa (randomized classical is nearly as fast) and Simon (hard to find natural instances), yet they are provable, exponential separations in the oracle model (Lec17, pp. 6-7; Lec18, pp. 2, 7) → quantum-complexity

### post-quantum-cryptography
- RSA relies on hardness of factoring; Diffie-Hellman on discrete log. Shor's algorithm solves both (factoring and discrete log) in polynomial time, so breaks RSA and Diffie-Hellman (Lec19, pp. 1-2; Lec21, p. 3) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Shor's algorithm can also be modified to break elliptic-curve cryptosystems, and more generally problems of finding hidden structure in abelian groups; almost all public-key cryptosystems in current use rely on such structure (Lec21, p. 3) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Classically, 512-bit, 768-bit and maybe even 1024-bit RSA-style keys are no longer quite secure against well-funded attackers with the number field sieve; Snowden documents show money allocated for this. (Classical context for key sizes; no quantum timeline is given) (Lec19, pp. 1-2) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Generic one-way functions seem unthreatened by quantum computers: quantum computers break some supposed one-way functions (factoring, discrete log based) but there are many more "generic", less structured ones that do not seem threatened (Lec17, p. 4) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Lattice-based cryptography: public-key systems built on lattice problems (e.g. finding the shortest nonzero vector, or one within about a √n factor of shortest) that nobody knows how to break even with a quantum computer. Regev (2005): they could be broken if Shor's algorithm were generalized to the dihedral group; nobody has done so (Lec21, p. 4) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Lattice-based crypto is an attractive alternative to RSA and Diffie-Hellman for those worried about quantum computers, and also enables fully homomorphic encryption (computing on encrypted data), first proposed by Gentry in 2009; not known to be breakable by quantum computers. Practical drawback: huge keys, messages and computation times, but steadily improving and many schemes are practical or nearly (Lec21, p. 4) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Grover's algorithm gives only a quadratic speedup for brute-force search over keys (e.g. key search over decryption keys; he frames it with a story of running Grover on all possible decryption keys); the doubled-key-length rule is not stated (Lec22, pp. 1, 7) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Hash functions: collision resistance is attacked classically by the birthday attack (about square root of the range size queries); quantumly the BHT algorithm finds collisions in about N^(1/3) queries in the black-box model (Lec18, p. 3; Lec24, pp. 1-2) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Timeline: no statement in these lectures about when a cryptographically relevant quantum computer will exist, and no discussion of NIST standardization or migration; only that the algorithm requires large-scale reliable quantum computers (Lec25, p. 1 for adiabatic; none for Shor) → post-quantum-cryptography
