Accessed: full text of arXiv:2111.03011v2 (28 Aug 2022; PRL 129, 090502), main text pp. 1-4 and Supplemental Material pp. 6-17. Pages cited are arXiv pages. Authors are at the Institute of Theoretical Physics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (academic, not Google/hardware-vendor).

## Claim being tested
- Google's 2019 Sycamore experiment (53 qubits, 20 cycles) drew noisy samples with fidelity f about 0.002 in about 200 s. Google estimated that classical supercomputers would need 10,000 years (p. 1) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- The authors point out that this 10,000-year figure rests on one specific classical method, the Schrödinger-Feynman algorithm. It is not a theoretical lower bound over all algorithms, so a better algorithm could in principle undo the claim (p. 1) → quantum-advantage-experiments

## Prior classical attacks (as described by the authors)
- IBM estimated that the full 53-qubit state vector could be stored and evolved using all the RAM and disks of the Summit supercomputer. The authors call this unrealistic in practice (p. 1) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Earlier tensor-network work [15] contracted the network 2000 times, each time getting a batch of 64 correlated bitstrings. It mixed 2000 perfect samples with 998,000 random bitstrings to reach XEB of about 0.002. The authors say this was too costly and had not been carried out (p. 1) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- The "big-head" approach [16] used 60 GPUs for 5 days to produce 10^6 correlated samples with XEB 0.739, which passes the XEB test. Those samples are correlated, though, unlike the independent samples in Google's experiment (p. 1) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Producing uncorrelated samples by repeating batch contractions thousands of times was judged unaffordable (p. 1; Supp. p. 6) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- A cited work [19] shows methods that reach high average XEB with low true fidelity. The authors take this to show that XEB has limits as a fidelity measure (p. 1) → quantum-advantage-experiments

## Method
- The circuit is treated as a 3D tensor network: single-qubit gates are matrices and two-qubit gates are 4-way tensors. The network is a Feynman path-integral sum over many paths (pp. 2-3) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Fidelity is traded for cost by "drilling holes". The authors break K edges, which deletes K/2 two-qubit gates. Each broken edge halves the fidelity, giving F about 2^-K. Equivalently, only a fraction 2^-K of the paths is summed (p. 2) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- A "sparse state" is requested: amplitudes for L randomly chosen groups of l correlated bitstrings each. One contraction yields all of them, and one bitstring per group is then drawn by importance sampling. This gives L uncorrelated samples from the approximate distribution (pp. 1-2, Supp. p. 6-7) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Only the groups need to be independent. Bitstrings inside a group may be correlated. In practice 6 qubits are left fully open (l = 64) and the other 47 are random (pp. 3, 8) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- The big-head idea [16] is extended to the sparse state. The network is split into a head, which dominates the cost, and a tail containing the final-state qubits. The head result is reused to compute all requested amplitudes (p. 2) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- A new "zigzag" contraction order is used for the tail. It works greedily from both boundaries to cut space and time cost (p. 3) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Google tuned the fSim two-qubit gate (θ near π/2) so that its decomposition rank is 4 with a flat spectrum. This makes classical simulation harder than with CZ gates (rank 2) (p. 3) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- The authors exploit the low-rank structure in two ways. When both input indices are cut, the gate becomes rank one exactly, with no fidelity loss. When one index is fixed (slicing), the squared singular values (sin²θ+1 and cos²θ) are very imbalanced. A rank-one approximation then costs only a factor (sin²θ+1)/2 in fidelity (p. 3; Supp. pp. 10-12) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Sampling uses Metropolis MCMC. Frugal sampling also works because the approximate distribution fits Porter-Thomas, and l = 64 suffices (pp. 3-4; Supp. p. 8) → quantum-advantage-experiments

## Resources and results (Sycamore 53 qubits, 20 cycles, sequence ABCDCDAB)
- Parameters: K = 8 broken edges (4 two-qubit gates removed) and 21 further companion-edge approximations, for a 2^-8 × 0.9565 reduction. Estimated fidelity F ≈ 0.0037 (p. 3, Eq. 2) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Output: 2^20 (about 10^6) uncorrelated bitstrings from 2^26 computed amplitudes (Fig. 1 caption, p. 2; p. 4) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Cost: 2^16 subtasks of about 427 s each (about 112 s head and 315 s tail). Total time about 15 h on 512 V100 GPUs, written in PyTorch with Complex64 (p. 4, p. 4 Discussion) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Total time complexity is about 3.5×10^18, slightly below earlier batch-method work. Overall GPU efficiency was only 18.85% (pp. 3-4; Table I, p. 10) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Fidelity 0.0037 compares with Google's 0.002. The authors say the sampling problem is for the first time solved classically with fidelity above Google's hardware samples (p. 2) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- The samples, contraction code and contraction order were released publicly (Abstract; p. 4) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Extrapolation: the authors estimate about 2.79×10^19 floating-point operations without branch merging. They claim an ideal, highly efficient run on an ExaFLOPS machine would take "a few dozen seconds", faster than Google's hardware. This is a projection only, not performed. They expect further speedups from cuQuantum (p. 4) → quantum-advantage-experiments

## Validation and caveats
- Fidelity is estimated analytically, not measured with XEB on the 53-qubit samples. The authors say that checking 10^6 samples via XEB at that size would cost too much computation to afford (Supp. p. 12) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Sanity checks on the full-size run: the sparse-state norm tracks the fraction of paths summed, and the probability histogram fits Porter-Thomas (Fig. 3, p. 4) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Validation was done on smaller Sycamore-type circuits (30, 38 and 45 qubits, 14 cycles, EFGH sequence), where exact states can be stored. Estimated fidelity, exact fidelity and XEB agree, XEB values exceed 0.002, and l = 64 is enough (Supp. pp. 12-15, Figs. 8-11) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- The target of the simulation is XEB above 0.002 with at least 10^6 samples (Supp. p. 7-8) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- The 15 h run is a classical sampling at matched, slightly higher, XEB fidelity. The paper itself does not frame this as a claim that quantum advantage is refuted in general; it claims only to solve the Sycamore sampling problem as defined (pp. 1-2, 4) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- ⚠️ The paper does not discuss energy use. Its "faster than Google's hardware" statement is an ExaFLOPS extrapolation, not a measurement. Energy comparison is absent (caveat for the caller: do not attribute energy claims to this source) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- The authors cite the later Zuchongzhi 60-qubit 24-cycle circuit sampling [9, 10] and other advantage claims only as references. They do not address those larger circuits (p. 1, refs. 9-10) → quantum-advantage-experiments
