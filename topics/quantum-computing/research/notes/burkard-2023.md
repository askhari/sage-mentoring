## Qubit types and encodings
- Spin qubits are grouped by how many spins encode one qubit: the single-spin Loss-DiVincenzo (LD) qubit, the two-spin singlet-triplet (ST0) qubit and the three-spin exchange-only (EO) qubit. A fourth type, donor qubits, uses the nuclear or electron spin of a dopant atom. (§II, p. 4; Fig. 2, p. 5) → spin-qubits
- LD qubit: |0>,|1> are spin down/up of one electron. A static field sets the splitting and a transverse ac magnetic field drives rotations. Exchange J between neighbouring dots, tuned with gate voltages over orders of magnitude, gives two-qubit gates. (§II.A, p. 4-5) → spin-qubits
- Every spin qubit uses exchange in some way: when two electrons' wavefunctions overlap, the singlet is lowered relative to the triplets by J. (§II, p. 4) → spin-qubits
- Typical LD readout (Elzerman) is spin-selective tunnelling to a reservoir. It needs a large field (about 10 T in GaAs), with Zeeman energy much larger than k_B*T_e. Larmor frequencies are tens of GHz. (§II.A p. 4-5; §V.A.1 p. 26) → spin-qubits
- Kane donor qubit: the 31P nuclear spin is the qubit. Its gyromagnetic ratio is about 2000 times smaller than the electron's, which makes it very coherent. Exchange-mediated coupling between donors is hypersensitive to donor placement (valley interference). (§II.B p. 5-6; §V.B.3 p. 31) → spin-qubits
- ST0 qubit: |S> and |T0> of two electrons in a double dot. It sits in a decoherence-free subspace with respect to global magnetic fields and is controlled with baseband voltage pulses (J), but a field gradient dB_z is needed for the second axis. It is still sensitive to local field fluctuations. (§II.C, p. 7-8) → spin-qubits
- ST0 readout uses Pauli spin blockade (PSB), which converts the singlet/triplet difference into a charge configuration that a nearby charge sensor reads. (§II.C p. 8; §V.C.1 p. 32) → spin-qubits
- EO qubit: three spins with total spin 1/2. Exchange alone gives universal control, with two control axes 120 degrees apart on the Bloch sphere. All levels are degenerate when idle (J=0), and no oscillating fields or micromagnets are needed. Gates are sequences of exchange pulses. (§II.D, p. 8-9; Fig. 2) → spin-qubits
- Resonant-exchange (RX) variant: both exchanges are held on constantly, which opens a gap that allows ac-driven control. This exposes the qubit more to charge noise. Sweet-spot variants are ARX and AEON. (§II.D p. 9) → spin-qubits
- Leakage in EO qubits: inhomogeneous magnetic fields or gradients can move the state out of the S=1/2 subspace to S=3/2. PSB cannot tell this leaked state from the encoded |1>. (§II.D p. 9; §V.D.1 p. 35) → spin-qubits
- Hybrid variants with charge character: the flopping-mode qubit, the charge quadrupole qubit, and the hybrid and spin-charge qubits. They gain a stronger electric dipole and faster electrical control, at the price of more charge-noise exposure. (§II.E, p. 9-10) → spin-qubits

## Materials and devices
- GaAs: spinful nuclei everywhere (all Ga and As isotopes have I=3/2); electron effective mass 0.067 m; g about -0.44. Silicon: mostly I=0 isotopes (28Si, 30Si) and about 4.7% 29Si, which can be removed by enrichment. Ge: 7.8% 73Ge (I=9/2). (§II.B p. 6; §III.A.1 p. 10; §III.D p. 16; §VI.B p. 43) → spin-qubits
- Silicon device architectures are Si-MOS (electrons at the Si/SiO2 interface), Si/SiGe (strained Si quantum well, undoped heterostructures now standard), FinFET and SLEDGE (single-layer etch-defined gates). Overlapping top gates on undoped Si/SiGe are called a paradigm shift. (§III.A.2, p. 11-12; §III.C p. 14; Fig. 3, p. 11) → spin-qubits
- Industry-compatible subtractive fabrication (as opposed to academic lift-off) is being adopted. CMOS nanowire devices from industrial research foundries show single-electron, single-qubit operation. (§III.C, p. 15) → spin-qubits
- Valleys: bulk Si has six conduction-band minima. In Si/SiGe wells and MOS devices, strain or confinement raises the four in-plane valleys and leaves two. Interfaces split those two (the valley splitting), and this is usually the lowest excitation in a Si dot, so it must be large. (§III.E, p. 17) → spin-qubits
- Valley splitting values: 50-500 ueV in Si-MOS (tunable with the vertical field); up to about 200-300 ueV in Si/SiGe, depending on interface quality and well width. (§III.E, p. 18) → spin-qubits
- Valley-spin hot spots: when the Zeeman energy equals the valley splitting (about 100 ueV at tesla-scale fields), spin relaxation is strongly enhanced. (§VI.C, p. 44-45) → spin-qubits
- Holes (Si, Ge/GeSi): stronger spin-orbit coupling gives fast electrical control without a micromagnet or ESR line, weaker hyperfine coupling, and no valleys. Drawbacks are band mixing and strong device dependence. Planar Ge dots are larger (light effective mass, about 0.06 m0), which relaxes fabrication. (§V.A.4 p. 29-30; §V.E.3 p. 38-39) → spin-qubits
- Ge hole milestones (as of 2021): single-qubit fidelity 99.3% with about 20 ns rotations; a 2x2 array that produced a four-qubit GHZ state; T2* up to about 1 us and T1 > 32 ms. Whether nuclei or charge noise limits dephasing is not yet settled. (§V.A.4 p. 29-30; §V.E.3 p. 39) → spin-qubits

## Gates and fidelities
- Randomized benchmarking (RB) is the main comparison metric. Random Clifford sequences plus a recovery gate twirl the errors into one exponential decay, whose rate gives an average gate infidelity. A specific gate such as CZ is measured by interleaving it. (§V intro, p. 26) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Doing RB (thousands of calibrated operations) shows that cryogenics, control hardware, wiring and qubits work together. Tomography and gate set tomography (GST) give complementary information. The Fig. 24 y-intercept and saturation levels indicate SPAM error and leakage. (§V p. 26; Fig. 24 p. 39) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Authors' conclusion: all spin-qubit technologies have passed the test of showing real quantum gates, and fidelities above 99% for basic gates are firmly established across the community. (§V.F, p. 39) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Single-qubit RB in Si (to 2021): 99.6% (28Si-MOS, Veldhorst 2014); 99.3% and 99.7% (Si/SiGe, Zajac 2018); above 99.9% (isotopically enriched Si/SiGe, Yoneda 2018, limited by charge noise through the micromagnet gradient); 99.96% Clifford fidelity (SiMOS, Yang 2019); 99.69% with a cryogenic control chip (Xue 2021). (§V.A.4, p. 29) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Two-qubit fidelity in Si, shown to be much lower early on: Bell-state fidelity about 78% (Zajac 2018, Watson 2018, SPAM-corrected tomography); RB 94.7% average Clifford and 98% CROT (Huang 2019); 92% (Xue 2019, character RB); 99.5% (Xue 2021b, pulsed exchange); above 99% (Noiri 2021). (§V.A.4, p. 29) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Best combined result in a six-dot device (Mills 2021): sequential single-spin rotations above 99.9%, simultaneous rotations above 99%, CZ above 99.8%, SPAM errors below 3%. Fidelity above 99% readout in Si/SiGe. (§V.A.1 p. 27; §V.A.4 p. 29) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Two-qubit gates are less accurate than single-qubit gates across these spin platforms (Si, 2021): the best single-qubit fidelity is about 99.9+% against 99.5-99.8% for two-qubit gates, and early two-qubit values were 92-98%. The paper does not state this as a general rule. (§V.A.4, p. 29; summary of figures above) → coherence-and-fidelity
- ⚠️ The comparison in the previous bullet is my synthesis of the quoted numbers, not a sentence in the paper. Treat it as a derived observation.
- ST0 in GaAs: single-qubit RB above 99.5% (stabilized gradient). Resonantly driven ST0: about 99%. Two-qubit: Bell-state fidelity about 70% (conventional), entangling gate about 90% (resonant, process tomography). Charge noise is the stated limit. (§V.C.4, p. 34-35) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Donor: electron-spin gates above 99.95% and nuclear 99.99% (1Q RB, Muhonen 2015). GST on two donors: single-qubit up to 99.93%, two-qubit 99.2% (Madzik 2021), with coherent ZZ errors attributed to off-resonant microwave leakage. (§V.B.4, p. 31) → coherence-and-fidelity
- EO qubit: RB error per Clifford 0.35% (about half from leakage) in Andrews 2019, and 0.12% in the SLEDGE device (Ha 2021). This error is "contextual", i.e. it depends on the control sequence. Exchange-pulse error alone is well below 1e-3 per Clifford pulse (2.7 pulses per Clifford on average). Idle time raises hyperfine leakage; faster pulses raise miscalibration error. (§V.D.5, p. 37) → coherence-and-fidelity
- A two-qubit EO CNOT sequence (Fong-Wandzura) takes about 22 pulses, 6 dots and 5 calibrated exchange axes. It had not been demonstrated at the time of writing. (§V.D.4, p. 36-37) → spin-qubits
- Readout: Elzerman tunnelling readout is destructive, needs a reservoir next to the dot, and has a timescale trade-off with T1. Fidelity above 99% is reported for it in Si/SiGe. Dispersive gate sensing scales better but has been lower in fidelity and bandwidth (82.9% at 3 kHz in a donor ST experiment). Latched readout helps ST qubits. Cavity ancilla readout of an ST qubit reached 99.2%. (§V.A.1 p. 27; §V.B.1 p. 30; §V.C.1 p. 32; §VII.C p. 50) → coherence-and-fidelity

## Decoherence timescales and noise
- Definitions used: T1 (relaxation, by energy exchange with the environment), T2 (decoherence, from a Hahn-echo experiment), T2* (inhomogeneous dephasing, from a free-evolution Ramsey experiment), and rotating-frame T2,R and T1rho for decay under driving. (§VI, p. 40; §VI.A.3-4, p. 41-42) → coherence-and-fidelity
- T2* needs care. For slow noise the Ramsey decay is Gaussian rather than exponential. For 1/f noise the measured T2* depends on the total averaging time, so a T2* value is not an intrinsic property of the qubit. (§VI.A.3, p. 42) → coherence-and-fidelity
- A Hahn echo cancels noise at zero frequency and passes noise at higher frequency. Dynamical decoupling can compensate slow drifts. Long T2 is necessary but not sufficient for high-fidelity control. (§VI.A.4, p. 42; §VI.A.6, p. 43) → coherence-and-fidelity
- T1 is rarely the fidelity limit for spin qubits, but readout errors rise when the readout time approaches T1. (§VI.C, p. 45) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Hyperfine noise (nuclear spins) in GaAs gives T2* of about 10 ns and T2 about 1 us for ST0 (Petta 2005, GaAs) and T2 about 500 ns for single spins (Koppens 2008). Nuclear fluctuations are slow (about 1 ms), and the dephasing is Gaussian. It can be mitigated by isotopic purification, nuclear polarization and dynamic decoupling. (§III.F p. 18; §VI.B, p. 43-44) → spin-qubits
- Silicon coherence: ensemble T2 about 2 s for donor electrons (below 50 ppm 29Si) and more than 39 minutes for ensemble nuclear spins in enriched Si. Single-qubit Hahn-echo T2 is about 1 ms (donor), about 1.2 ms (SiMOS dot), and 30 us-1 ms (Si/SiGe dots). Donor nuclear spin coherence is above 30 s with CPMG. (§V.B.2 p. 31; §VI.B p. 44) → spin-qubits
- Hyperfine dephasing is reduced by larger dots (more nuclei averaged), but in small dots with few spinful nuclei the T2* varies strongly from device to device. (§VI.B, p. 43) → spin-qubits
- Charge noise: spins couple to electric noise through spin-orbit coupling, micromagnet gradients, or exchange sensitivity. It has a 1/f spectrum. Amplitudes of 0.1-10 ueV/sqrt(Hz) at 1 Hz are seen in many material systems. (§VI.D, p. 45) → spin-qubits
- Authors' view: 1/f charge noise cannot be completely removed from semiconductor devices, whereas hyperfine noise can be eliminated with enough isotopic enrichment. Micromagnet EDSR and RX qubits give convenient control at the cost of charge-noise sensitivity. Exchange sweet spots (e.g. zero detuning for ST0) reduce charge-noise sensitivity. (§VI.D, p. 46) → spin-qubits
- Micromagnet devices have T2 and T2* limited by charge noise rather than nuclei. Without micromagnets in Si/SiGe, they are limited by hyperfine noise, which in Si also has a 1/f shape. Hole qubits are limited by charge noise through spin-orbit coupling. (§VI.B p. 44; §VI.D p. 45-46) → spin-qubits
- Spin T1 up to about 30 s in donors and about 57 s in a GaAs dot at low field. Relaxation scales as B^5 (piezoelectric, GaAs) and B^7 (deformation potential, Si) for spin-orbit mediated one-phonon relaxation. (§VI.C, p. 44-45) → spin-qubits
- Exchange oscillations in ST0 qubits decay after about 10 periods under detuning control, because of charge noise. Pulsing the barrier (the symmetric mode) is first-order insensitive to this but needs larger voltage pulses. (§V.C.2, p. 33) → spin-qubits

## Scaling, connectivity, hybrid
- Reasoning given for scale: about 1e6 physical qubits may be needed for a fully error-corrected computer, and dots are about 100 nm in size. This allows dense arrays produced by the microelectronics industry. The challenges are wiring and addressing, and connectivity for error correction. (§I, p. 3) → spin-qubits
- Exchange is short-range and gives nearest-neighbour two-qubit gates. Long-range options are spin shuttling or SWAP chains, superexchange, spin buses (a chain of N dots gives coupling J/sqrt(N)), capacitive or dipole coupling, and microwave cavities. Spin shuttling in Si works over a 9-dot array (charge) and in SiMOS (spin). (§IV.F, p. 24-25; §VII, p. 46) → spin-qubits
- Cavity QED: spin-photon strong coupling has been reached in Si/SiGe (6.03 GHz resonator, around 100 mT, Samkharadze 2018 and Mi 2018). Two-qubit coupling through the cavity and dispersive single-spin readout are shown, but a time-domain cavity-mediated two-qubit gate was still to come at the time. Spin-transmon coupling through a resonator has been shown (Landig 2019). (§VII.B-D, p. 48-51) → spin-qubits
- Dispersive readout is the same cQED method used with superconducting qubits: the cavity frequency shifts by chi = g^2/(wa-wc) depending on the qubit state. (§VII.C, p. 50) → spin-qubits
- Spin-qubit temperature: electron temperatures are typically 50-300 mK in these experiments. Elzerman readout needs k_B*T_e much smaller than the Zeeman energy (e.g. 27.6 GHz at 1 T). The paper does not discuss operation at 1 K or above. (§V.A.1 footnote 4, p. 26) → spin-qubits
- Outlook: no single roadblock to CMOS-like yields; fidelity limits have clear reduction routes (charge-noise-aware bias regimes, isotopic enrichment). Readout speed and fidelity have improved. (§VIII, p. 51) → spin-qubits

## Hardware landscape and DiVincenzo
- The paper says spin qubits have satisfied the DiVincenzo criteria. It states five requirements: scalable register, initialization to a fiducial state, coherence much longer than gate time, a high-fidelity universal gate set, and readout. (§I, p. 2) → divincenzo-criteria
- Authors' comparison of platforms: spin qubits have the most stringent fabrication demands compared with superconducting, trapped-ion and photonic qubits because of their small size. Hence the route to large arrays has been slower. The key advantages are small size, long coherence, and compatibility with industrial Si CMOS infrastructure. (§VIII, p. 51; Abstract p. 1) → hardware-landscape
- Trade-offs: LD qubits are efficient and robust against charge noise; ST0 and EO qubits allow baseband electrical control (EO without spin-orbit coupling or micromagnets). Si offers very high coherence; Ge offers spin-orbit-engineered electrical control. Nuclear spins have extreme coherence but electron exchange is fast. The authors think it too early to say which qubit type, carrier or material will win. (§VIII, p. 51-52) → hardware-landscape
- Fully-connected processors can operate at higher fidelity than nearest-neighbour ones, citing a trapped-ion experiment (Linke 2017). (§VII, p. 46) → hardware-landscape
- Fault tolerance: it is unclear when a qubit counts as good enough, since standard surface-code threshold estimates assume geometry and error correlations that do not match present spin-qubit devices. Small NISQ demonstrations exist (analog simulation with 3-4 spins; VQE and small algorithms on two qubits). (§VIII, p. 52) → hardware-landscape
- Gate speeds: Rabi frequency of about 10 MHz in early GaAs ESR, over 100 MHz in micromagnet EDSR, 400 MHz in hole nanowires; exchange oscillations at 200 ps to nanosecond timescales; a Ge hole rotation about 20 ns. (§V.A.2 p. 27-28; §V.C.2 p. 33; §V.E.3 p. 39) → spin-qubits
