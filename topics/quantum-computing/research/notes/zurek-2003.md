## decoherence

- Decoherence is the destruction of quantum coherence between preferred states, namely those of the observables that the environment keeps "monitoring". Einselection (environment-induced superselection) is the consequence: out of the huge Hilbert space, only a small set of resilient pointer states stays effectively accessible. (§I.B, p. 3) → decoherence
- Relaxation and classical noise are the environment perturbing the system. Decoherence and einselection are the system perturbing the environment, that is, information about the system is imprinted on the environment. They often occur together but are different things. (§I.B, p. 4) → decoherence
- When the system is open, pure states turn into mixtures that rapidly become diagonal in the pointer states. The pointer states are fixed by the system-environment interaction Hamiltonian and do not depend on the initial state. What singles them out is that they survive monitoring and so remain predictable. (§IV intro, p. 14) → decoherence
- Minimal model with system S, apparatus A and environment E (one qubit each). If E's two states are orthogonal and tied to A's pointer states (⟨ε0|ε1⟩=0), tracing out E leaves a density matrix with only the diagonal terms. The cross terms of the pure state vanish, so only classical correlations remain and the basis ambiguity disappears. (§IV.A, pp. 14-15) → decoherence
- Qubit coupled to N environment spins: the off-diagonal coefficient r(t) starts at 1 and quickly "crumbles". Its typical squared magnitude is about 2^-N, so decoherence is exponentially effective in the size of the environment. Exception: if the environment starts in a special eigenstate of the interaction, coherence survives, but this is unlikely in realistic cases. (§IV.A.1, pp. 15-16) → decoherence
- Decoherence timescale (quantum Brownian motion, high-temperature limit): off-diagonal terms ρ(x,x′) decay exponentially at a rate equal to the relaxation rate γ times (Δx/λ_T)², where λ_T is the thermal de Broglie wavelength. The diagonal (x=x′) is untouched. For 1 g at room temperature and a 1 cm separation, decoherence is about 10^40 times faster than relaxation. (§V.B.1, pp. 24-25) → decoherence
- The author warns that these estimates need caution. The rate cannot exceed the inverse spectral cutoff or the rate at which the superposition is created, and it may saturate at large separations. Even so, macroscopic "cat" superpositions decohere far faster than relaxation. (§V.B.1, p. 25) → decoherence
- In the microscopic domain decoherence can be slow compared with the system dynamics, while macroscopic superpositions of einselected states disappear rapidly. This is why decoherence is hard to "catch in action". (§I.B, p. 3) → decoherence
- Unitary evolution alone would produce macroscopic cat states. A chaotically tumbling moon (Hyperion) would reach a superposition of orientations differing by 2π in about 20 years (Lyapunov time 42 days, ln(I/ħ)≈ln 10^77). Cat states are therefore not only a measurement artifact, and something like decoherence is needed to explain what we observe. (§III.A, p. 12) → decoherence
- Dephasing by classical noise gives a mixed density matrix only on average over noise realizations. In each single realization the state stays pure and could be restored if the noise were known. In real decoherence the information is imprinted on the environment, so it cannot be undone this way. For engineering the two can look alike, and error-correcting codes handle either. (§IV.C, p. 19; §VIII.B, pp. 47-48) → decoherence
- For quantum computing: strategies against decoherence are to isolate the system, use decoherence-free subspaces (degenerate pointer subspaces), dynamical decoupling, environment engineering, and quantum error correction (encoding in entangled qubits, so no cloning is needed). Fault tolerance is possible if the error per step is below a threshold (about 10^-4 quoted in 2003). (§VIII.D, pp. 49-50) → decoherence
- On the measurement problem: decoherence enforces the apparent "collapse". After a decoherence time only einselected memory states keep useful correlations. The author states that the approach needs no modification of quantum theory, and he does not call it complete, listing open conceptual issues (e.g. what counts as a "system"). He also notes that it fits both the Many Worlds and Copenhagen frameworks, where it defines the branches or the quantum-classical border. (§I.C-D, pp. 4-5; §IX, p. 51) → decoherence

## density-matrices

- The reduced density matrix ρ_AS = Tr_E|Ψ⟩⟨Ψ| of a subsystem is obtained by tracing over the environment. In the example it equals |α|²|↑⟩⟨↑|⊗|A1⟩⟨A1| + |β|²|↓⟩⟨↓|⊗|A0⟩⟨A0|, with no coherence terms. By contrast, the density matrix of a pure entangled state has off-diagonal terms in every product basis. (§IV.A, p. 15, Eqs. 4.5, 4.6) → density-matrices
- Single qubit: the reduced density matrix ρ_A has diagonal entries |a|² and |b|² and off-diagonal entries ab*·r(t). Decoherence leaves |a| and |b| unchanged and only shrinks r(t). (§IV.A.1, p. 15) → density-matrices
- Bloch-sphere picture: a qubit density matrix is a point (x,y,z) with z=|a|²-|b|², and pure states lie on the surface x²+y²+z²=1. Any quantum evolution maps the sphere into an ellipsoid inside it. Decoherence moves the point within the plane z=const toward the axis, by the fraction |r(t)|. Mixed states lie inside the sphere. (§IV.A.1, p. 15) → density-matrices
- Envariance (symmetry of entangled states under joint transformations of system and environment) is said to justify using reduced density matrices to describe a part of a larger system. Decoherence and einselection rely on them. (§I.C, p. 4) → density-matrices
- A density matrix can be mixed from two different causes. One is entanglement with an environment (the state of the whole stays pure). The other is classical ignorance of noise (an ensemble average over pure states). (§IV.C, p. 19; §VIII.B, p. 48) → density-matrices

## born-rule-measurement

- In textbooks the random outcome is attributed to a "collapse of the wavepacket", invoked when a quantum system meets a classical apparatus. Yet von Neumann showed there is no room for a real collapse in purely unitary models of measurement. This is the interpretive problem. (§II, p. 5) → born-rule-measurement
- Von Neumann-style premeasurement: Σ a_i|s_i⟩|A0⟩ → Σ a_i|s_i⟩|A_i⟩ is unitary and correlates the apparatus with the system. The result is an entangled (EPR-like) state, not one definite outcome. The same state can be rewritten in other bases (basis ambiguity), so it does not by itself say which quantity was measured. (§II.A, pp. 5-6) → born-rule-measurement
- A premeasurement is a generalized controlled-NOT (a "bit-by-bit measurement"). The information flow is basis dependent. In the conjugate basis {|+⟩,|−⟩} the roles of control and target swap, so while the apparatus records the system's observable, the system "measures" the phases of the apparatus. This is the sense in which phases are disturbed in measurement. (§II.A.1, pp. 6-7) → born-rule-measurement
- Interaction with the environment turns the system-apparatus entanglement into an effectively classical correlation. Only the pointer observable can store information with predictive power. For a microscopic, isolated measured system this yields the effective "collapse". The author's wording is a restriction on predictive utility, not a dynamical selection of one outcome. (Abstract, p. 1; §IV intro, p. 14) → born-rule-measurement
- Interpretations: Copenhagen puts a quantum-classical border "by decree", which gives two sets of laws. Many Worlds lets everything evolve unitarily but, per Zurek, leaves the "preferred basis" question unanswered, so it does not explain why a unique outcome is perceived. Decoherence supplies the preferred basis. (§I.A.1-2, pp. 2-4) → born-rule-measurement
- Born's probability rule is claimed to follow from envariance (the symmetry of entangled states), presented as new and still under research. This is said to supply the missing step in "no-collapse" derivations. (§I.C, p. 4; §I.D, p. 5; §IX, p. 51) → born-rule-measurement
