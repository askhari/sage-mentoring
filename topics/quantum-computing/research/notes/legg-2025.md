Accessed: full text of arXiv:2503.08944v1 [cond-mat.mes-hall], dated 11 Mar 2025 (6 pages; version v1, the only one read). Preprint comment, not peer-reviewed as far as this text shows. It is one critic's view (H. F. Legg, St Andrews / Basel) of the Microsoft Quantum paper Nature 638, 651 (2025) (ref. 1). The text read contains no Microsoft reply. Page numbers are arXiv pages.

## Context and scope
- Legg targets the "parity readout" reported by Microsoft Quantum in Nature 638, 651-655 (2025): a measurement of the fermion-number parity (even/odd) of a proximitised superconducting nanowire. (p. 1, Overview) → topological-qubits
- He notes that the authors imply a topological superconductor but acknowledge the effect could come from a trivial one. He says protecting parity against low-energy quasiparticles requires a superconducting (SC) gap, so he treats "is there a gap?" as the key question. (p. 1 Overview; p. 2 §I) → topological-qubits
- He explicitly does not argue whether the region is topological. He says Ref. 1 does not conclusively claim a topological superconductor, so he asks only whether the region is gapped or gapless. (p. 2, §I) → topological-qubits

## The topological gap protocol (TGP), as he describes it
- The TGP (introduced in Microsoft Quantum, PRB 107, 245423 (2023)) is presented as a "stringent" test of whether a nanowire has an SC gap and, if so, whether the gapped region is topological. In Ref. 1 it is used to tune the devices into a supposedly gapped, topological phase and to choose the gate and magnetic-field settings for the main experiment. (p. 2, §I) → topological-qubits
- A region counts as "gapped" in the TGP only if nonlocal conductance at low bias stays below about 5% of its maximum. (p. 2, §I) → topological-qubits
- The TGP antisymmetrises the nonlocal conductance in bias voltage, which cancels any symmetric component. He argues this makes the reported "gap" blind to large, symmetric low-bias conductance. (p. 4 Fig. 5 caption; p. 5) → topological-qubits

## Data re-analysed
- Legg used Microsoft's public data and code (GitHub repository, as downloaded 10 Mar 2025) and the open peer-review file of Ref. 1. His own figure code is on Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.15008728). (p. 1 Overview; p. 6) → topological-qubits
- Devices and measurements examined: Device A (measurements A1 and A2, two cutter pairs) and Device B (B1). (Figs. 1-6, pp. 2-5) → topological-qubits
- He also analyses conductance maps (local G_LL, G_RR and nonlocal G_RL, G_LR vs. bias and field) that he says are in the public repository but are not shown in Ref. 1, which shows only TGP "phase cartoons". (p. 4 §III; Figs. 4-6) → topological-qubits

## His findings (as claims by the critic)
- Finding 1, outcome depends on analysis choices. For Device A1, the readout region (B about 1.8 T) passes the TGP as gapped over a 1.5-3 T field window. It is reported gapless if the window is cut to 1.5-2.2 T, though that window still contains the readout field. (p. 2, Fig. 1) → topological-qubits
- For Device A2, the readout region (about 2.06 T) is gapped with cutter 1 but gapless with cutter 2. (p. 2, Fig. 2; p. 2 §I) → topological-qubits
- He attributes this to residual nonlocal conductance that sits near the 5% threshold, so a change of field range or cutter flips the verdict. He concludes the TGP is not a reliable gap detector. He says a similar sensitivity to ranges, resolution and cutter was already reported in his earlier comment on the 2023 TGP paper (arXiv:2502.19560). (p. 2, §I) → topological-qubits
- He adds that the bias range of the datasets is small (for A1 about -66 to +64 microvolt) and not symmetric about zero. (p. 2, §I) → topological-qubits
- Finding 2, reproducibility claim contradicted. In the peer-review file the Microsoft authors said the readout region was the only region to pass the TGP in the explored range. Legg shows that Device B has a second passing region (about B = 2.8 T, plunger voltage about 1.6725 V) and a third for other cutters. The cartoon in Ref. 1 shows only the largest region because of an option in the plotting code (zbp cluster numbers=[1]). (p. 3, §II and Fig. 3(i)-(ii)) → topological-qubits
- He argues that, if the parity readout were reproducible, it should have been tested in those other TGP-passing regions, and that this was not done. He says it remains unanswered why the presented regions were chosen. (p. 1 summary item 2; p. 3 §II) → topological-qubits
- Coding issue: the TGP code antisymmetrises by array index, not by bias value. The released datasets have an asymmetric bias grid, so the antisymmetrisation is not about zero bias. Dropping one bias point to make the grid symmetric makes even more, larger regions pass the TGP in Device B, and the studied readout field (about 2.0 T) becomes secondary. He takes this to show that a single data point can change the TGP outcome. (p. 3, §II, Fig. 3(iii) and code extract) → topological-qubits
- Finding 3, the unshown conductance data. The local conductance in the readout regions shows many low-energy states, strongest in Device A2 cutter 2, where G_RR is finite across almost all bias. The signal also differs a lot between the left and right sides. Nonlocal conductance at low bias is large, in A2 for both cutters. He reads this as highly disordered devices with no clear SC gap, so the regions are gapless. He says the TGP "gapless" outcomes were therefore the more accurate ones. (p. 4 §III, Figs. 4-5; p. 5) → topological-qubits
- He disputes a statement from the peer-review file that accidental quantum dots in the junction were not detected because particle-hole symmetry breaking is absent. He says the full data show clear particle-hole symmetry breaking (antisymmetric parts of local and symmetric parts of nonlocal conductance, e.g. Device A2, Fig. 5). (p. 1 item 3; p. 4-5 §III) → topological-qubits

## His conclusions
- The TGP is unreliable as a diagnostic for a gap, and so also for topology (Majorana bound states), because its verdict depends on field range, resolution and cutter. (p. 5, Conclusions) → topological-qubits
- The effect in Ref. 1, even if reproducible, is more likely a fine-tuned mesoscopic disorder effect than a (topological) superconducting nanowire. This follows from his reading of no well-defined gap. (p. 1 abstract; p. 5 Conclusions) → topological-qubits
- He says the core findings of Nature 638, 651 are "not reliable and should be revisited". (p. 1 abstract; p. 5) → topological-qubits

## What he says is needed
- Because the TGP is not reliable, he says the full conductance data (local and nonlocal, not antisymmetrised) in the readout regions are the only way to establish whether a gap exists. He faults Ref. 1 for showing only TGP cartoons. (p. 5, Conclusions) → topological-qubits
- Implied requirements, stated as his criticisms rather than as a list: a gap shown directly in the data of the readout region; tests of the readout in all TGP-passing regions, to show reproducibility; and correct, complete reporting of the TGP outcomes. (pp. 3-5 §II-III, Conclusions) → topological-qubits
- Parity protection needs a real SC gap. He cites Rainis & Loss 2012, Albrecht et al. 2017 and Karzig et al. 2017 for this. (p. 1 Overview, refs 4-6) → topological-qubits

## Caveats
- ⚠️ Legg's claims are a single critic's analysis of public data and are not independently confirmed in this note. Legg is not a coauthor of the original work. The v1 text contains no response from Microsoft Quantum. A later reply or revision may exist (not checked here).
- ⚠️ He argues that the data are consistent with "no clear gap", but he also states that the TGP's "topological" label is not the focus. He does not claim to show Majoranas are absent. The note's claims should not be cited as showing a topological qubit does not exist.
- The note does not cover the content of the Microsoft paper itself (parity measurement via interferometry, its measured signal sizes or timescales). Legg only discusses it indirectly.
