## math-foundations

### Complex numbers
- A complex number is c = a + bi with real a, b and i² = −1 (App. B.1, p. 211 [199]) → complex-numbers
- It can be written in polar form c = r·e^{iφ}, where r = |c| = √(a²+b²) is the magnitude (also called modulus or norm) and φ ∈ [0, 2π) is the angle with the positive horizontal axis when c is viewed as the point (a, b) (App. B.1, p. 211 [199]) → complex-numbers
- Complex numbers of magnitude 1 lie on the unit circle; they are written e^{iφ} = cos φ + i sin φ (App. B.1, p. 211 [199]) → complex-numbers
- The complex conjugate c* is a − ib, equivalently r·e^{−iφ} (App. B.1, p. 211 [199]) → complex-numbers
- Geometric sum: Σ_{j=0}^{m−1} z^j equals m if z = 1 and (1−z^m)/(1−z) otherwise; applied to a root of unity z = e^{2πir/N} with r in {1,…,N−1}, the sum over j = 0..N−1 is 0 (App. B.1, p. 211 [199]) → complex-numbers
- Identity |1 − e^{iθ}| = 2|sin(θ/2)|, seen geometrically by drawing 1 and e^{iθ} as vectors in the complex plane; also cos²θ + sin²θ = 1 and sin 2θ = 2 sin θ cos θ (App. B.1, pp. 211–212 [199–200]) → complex-numbers
- A global phase e^{iθ} multiplying a state does not change any measurement probabilities, so it has no physical significance (§1.2.2, p. 16 [4]) → complex-numbers
- The amplitudes of a quantum state are complex numbers (§1.2.1, p. 15 [3]) → complex-numbers

### Vectors and Dirac notation
- A vector space V over a field F is closed under addition and scalar multiplication; C^d is the d-dimensional complex vector space of column vectors of d complex numbers (App. A.1, p. 205 [193]) → vectors-and-dirac-notation
- Linear independence, span, basis, and dimension (all bases have the same size); fixing an ordered basis lets each vector be written uniquely by its coefficients (App. A.1, p. 205 [193]) → vectors-and-dirac-notation
- A pure quantum state is a superposition |φ⟩ = α₀|0⟩ + … + α_{N−1}|N−1⟩ of classical states, with complex amplitudes α_i; the |i⟩ form an orthonormal basis of an N-dimensional Hilbert space, and the state is the column vector of its amplitudes (§1.2.1, p. 15 [3]) → vectors-and-dirac-notation
- Dirac notation: |v⟩ denotes the vector v (ket) and ⟨v| denotes v* (bra, the conjugate-transpose row vector); an inner product ⟨v|w⟩ is a bra-ket ("bracket") product (App. A.10, p. 210 [198]; §1.2.1, p. 15 [3]) → vectors-and-dirac-notation
- A single qubit is α₀|0⟩ + α₁|1⟩ with |α₀|² + |α₁|² = 1, living in C²; |0⟩ and |1⟩ are identified with the column vectors (1,0)ᵀ and (0,1)ᵀ (§1.3, p. 19 [7]) → vectors-and-dirac-notation
- The n-qubit basis state |x⟩ is the 2^n-dimensional vector with a 1 at position x (counting from 0) and 0 elsewhere; a register is a superposition of the 2^n basis states with squared-amplitude sum 1 (§1.3, p. 19 [7]) → vectors-and-dirac-notation
- Writing guidelines: do not nest kets inside kets or bras (⟨u|(α|v⟩+β|w⟩)⟩ is meaningless); do not use "qubit" as a noun for a multi-qubit register (App. A.10, p. 210 [198]; footnote 6, §1.3, p. 19 [7]) → vectors-and-dirac-notation

### Inner products and norms
- Inner product ⟨v|w⟩ = v*w = Σ_i v_i* w_i, conjugating the first vector (physics convention; mathematicians usually conjugate the second). A vector space with this inner product is a Hilbert space (App. A.3, p. 206 [194]) → inner-products-and-norms
- Vectors are orthogonal if ⟨v|w⟩ = 0; a set is orthogonal if pairwise orthogonal, and orthonormal if in addition all vectors have norm 1 (App. A.3, p. 206 [194]) → inner-products-and-norms
- The norm is ‖v‖ = √⟨v|v⟩ = √(Σ|v_i|²), the usual Euclidean length; it induces the distance ‖v−w‖ (App. A.3, p. 206 [194]) → inner-products-and-norms
- ‖v−w‖² = ‖v‖² + ‖w‖² − ⟨v|w⟩ − ⟨w|v⟩, so for unit vectors the real part of ⟨v|w⟩ equals 1 − ½‖v−w‖²: close unit vectors have inner product near 1 (App. A.3, p. 206 [194]) → inner-products-and-norms
- Cauchy-Schwarz: |⟨a|b⟩| ≤ ‖a‖·‖b‖ (App. A.3, p. 206 [194]; App. B.1, p. 211 [199], with a short proof for real vectors) → inner-products-and-norms
- Born rule: measuring |φ⟩ = Σα_j|j⟩ in the computational basis gives j with probability |α_j|², so the amplitude vector must have Euclidean norm 1 (§1.2.2, p. 16 [4]) → inner-products-and-norms
- Measuring in any orthonormal basis {ψ_j} gives outcome j with probability |⟨φ|ψ_j⟩|²; if |φ⟩ equals a basis vector the outcome is certain (§1.2.2, p. 17 [5]) → inner-products-and-norms
- n-qubit basis states |x⟩, |y⟩ are orthogonal iff x ≠ y, shown either from the 1 at position x or from the product rule ⟨x|y⟩ = ⟨x₁|y₁⟩···⟨x_n|y_n⟩ (§1.3, p. 19 [7]) → inner-products-and-norms, tensor-products

### Matrices and linear maps
- Matrices represent linear maps between vector spaces with chosen bases; A_ij is entry (i,j), Aᵀ the transpose, I_d the identity, A⁻¹ the (unique if it exists) inverse with (AB)⁻¹ = B⁻¹A⁻¹ (App. A.2, pp. 205–206 [193–194]) → matrices-and-linear-maps
- The conjugate transpose (adjoint) A* transposes and conjugates every entry; (AB)* = B*A*. Physicists write A† for A* (App. A.2, p. 206 [194]) → matrices-and-linear-maps
- The outer product of v and w is the matrix vw* (App. A.3, p. 206 [194]); in Dirac notation |v⟩⟨w| (App. A.10, p. 210 [198]) → matrices-and-linear-maps, vectors-and-dirac-notation
- Trace: Tr(A) = Σ_i A_ii of a square matrix; it is additive, cyclic (Tr(AB) = Tr(BA)), e.g. Tr(A vv*) = v*Av, equals the sum of the eigenvalues, and Tr(A⊗B) = Tr(A)Tr(B) (App. A.7, p. 209 [197]) → matrices-and-linear-maps
- Rank = size of a largest linearly independent set of rows; rank(A) = rank(A*) = number of nonzero singular values; A is invertible iff it has full rank (App. A.8, p. 209 [197]) → matrices-and-linear-maps
- Every matrix has a singular value decomposition A = UΣV⁻¹ = Σ_i σ_i u_i v_i*, even non-diagonalizable ones such as [[0,1],[0,0]] (App. A.5, p. 208 [196]) → matrices-and-linear-maps
- Pauli matrices I, X, Y, Z (App. A.9, p. 210 [198]): each is both unitary and Hermitian, so self-inverse, with eigenvalues ±1; distinct non-identity Paulis anti-commute; Y = iXZ. They are orthonormal under the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product (1/d)Tr(A*B), so any 2×2 complex matrix is a combination α₀I+α₁X+α₂Y+α₃Z (real coefficients if Hermitian) → matrices-and-linear-maps, unitary-and-hermitian-matrices
- A quantum operation on a closed system is multiplication of the state vector by an N×N complex matrix U, i.e. it must be linear (§1.2.3, p. 18 [6]) → matrices-and-linear-maps

### Unitary, Hermitian, normal matrices and projectors
- A is unitary if A⁻¹ = A*. Equivalent: A preserves inner products; A preserves norms; A maps unit vectors to unit vectors (App. A.4, p. 206 [194]) → unitary-and-hermitian-matrices
- Eigenvalues of a unitary matrix have absolute value 1 (App. A.4, p. 207 [195]) → unitary-and-hermitian-matrices, eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors
- Quantum operations must preserve the norm of the amplitude vector (probabilities sum to 1), hence are unitary; being invertible they are reversible (apply U⁻¹), whereas measurement is not reversible (§1.2.3, p. 19 [7]) → unitary-and-hermitian-matrices
- A is normal if A*A = AA*; unitary matrices are normal (App. A.5, p. 207 [195]) → unitary-and-hermitian-matrices
- A is Hermitian if A = A*; Hermitian matrices are normal (hence unitarily diagonalizable) and have real eigenvalues (App. A.5, p. 208 [196]) → unitary-and-hermitian-matrices, eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors
- A Hermitian matrix is positive semidefinite (psd) if all eigenvalues are ≥ 0 (positive definite if > 0); equivalently A = C*C for some C. Writing A ⪰ B for "A − B is psd" gives a partial order on Hermitian matrices (App. A.5, p. 208 [196]) → unitary-and-hermitian-matrices
- A projection (projector) has all eigenvalues 0 or 1, equivalently A² = A (App. A.5, p. 208 [196]) → unitary-and-hermitian-matrices
- A projective measurement is a set of projectors P₁…P_m summing to I; they are pairwise orthogonal (P_iP_j = 0 for i≠j). Outcome j has probability ⟨φ|P_j|φ⟩ = ‖P_j|φ⟩‖² = Tr(P_j|φ⟩⟨φ|), and the state collapses to P_j|φ⟩/‖P_j|φ⟩‖ (§1.2.2, p. 16 [4]) → unitary-and-hermitian-matrices
- Measuring in the computational basis is the projective measurement with P_j = |j⟩⟨j|; measuring in another orthonormal basis uses P_j = |ψ_j⟩⟨ψ_j|; projectors may have higher rank (§1.2.2, pp. 16–17 [4–5]) → unitary-and-hermitian-matrices
- An observable M = Σ λ_i P_i (distinct real outcomes λ_i) is Hermitian, and its expected outcome is Tr(M|φ⟩⟨φ|); every Hermitian matrix has a spectral decomposition, so observables correspond one-to-one to Hermitian matrices. Z = |0⟩⟨0| − |1⟩⟨1| is the computational-basis observable (§1.2.2, p. 17 [5]) → unitary-and-hermitian-matrices, eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors

### Eigenvalues, spectral decomposition, functions of matrices
- λ is an eigenvalue of A if Av = λv for some nonzero v (the eigenvector); the set of eigenvalues is the spectrum (App. A.5, p. 207 [195]) → eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors
- Similar matrices (A = SBS⁻¹) share eigenvalues; Schur: every matrix is A = UTU⁻¹ with U unitary and T upper triangular, whose diagonal holds the eigenvalues (App. A.5, p. 207 [195]) → eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors
- A is diagonalizable (A = SDS⁻¹) iff it has d linearly independent eigenvectors, which form the columns of S; not every matrix is diagonalizable (App. A.5, pp. 207–208 [195–196]) → eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors
- If A is unitarily diagonalizable, A = UDU⁻¹, then A = Σ_i λ_i u_i u_i* = Σ_i λ_i |v_i⟩⟨v_i| (spectral decomposition), iff A has an orthonormal set of d eigenvectors (App. A.5, p. 207 [195]; App. A.10, p. 210 [198]) → eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors
- A matrix is normal iff it is unitarily diagonalizable (proved via Schur); a non-normal matrix like [[1,1],[0,2]] can still be diagonalized, but not by a unitary (App. A.5, p. 207 [195]) → eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors, unitary-and-hermitian-matrices
- The eigenvalues of A* are the complex conjugates of those of A (App. A.5, p. 208 [196]) → eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors
- Applying a function f to a normal matrix means applying f to its eigenvalues: if A = VDV⁻¹ then f(A) = V f(D) V⁻¹; with A = Σ λ_j a_j a_j*, e^{−iA} = Σ e^{−iλ_j} a_j a_j*. If A is Hermitian, e^{−iA} is unitary (footnote 1, §9.1, p. 83 [71]) → eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors, unitary-and-hermitian-matrices
- Time evolution under a time-independent Hamiltonian H (the energy observable, a Hermitian matrix) is the unitary U = e^{−iHt} applied to |ψ(0)⟩, solving the Schrödinger equation iℏ d|ψ⟩/dt = H|ψ⟩ with ℏ set to 1 (§9.1, p. 83 [71]) → eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors, unitary-and-hermitian-matrices
- Hamiltonian simulation is the task of finding a small circuit that is ε-close to e^{iHt}; the notes drop the minus sign for convenience (§9.1, p. 84 [72]) → eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors

### Tensor products
- For an m×n matrix A and m'×n' matrix B, A⊗B is the mm'×nn' block matrix with blocks A_ij·B (Kronecker product); the tensor product of two numbers is a number and of two column vectors is a column vector (App. A.6, p. 208 [196]) → tensor-products
- Properties: c(A⊗B) = (cA)⊗B = A⊗(cB); (A⊗B)* = A*⊗B* (order of factors unchanged, same for inverse and transpose); distributive over addition; associative; mixed-product rule (A⊗B)(C⊗D) = (AC)⊗(BD) (App. A.6, p. 209 [197]) → tensor-products
- Tensor product of vector spaces: if V and V' have dimensions d, d' and bases {v_i}, {v'_j}, then V⊗V' is d·d'-dimensional, spanned by all v_i⊗v'_j; applying A to V and B to V' corresponds to A⊗B on the product space (App. A.6, p. 209 [197]) → tensor-products
- Composite quantum systems: H = H_A⊗H_B has dimension NM with basis |i⟩⊗|j⟩; a general state is Σα_ij |i⟩⊗|j⟩ (a "bipartite" state; similarly tripartite etc.) (§1.2.1, p. 15 [3]) → tensor-products
- An n-qubit register lives in the tensor product of n qubit spaces, with 2^n basis states |b₁⟩⊗…⊗|b_n⟩ abbreviated |b₁…b_n⟩ (|1⟩⊗|0⟩ = |1⟩|0⟩ = |1,0⟩ = |10⟩) (§1.3, p. 19 [7]) → tensor-products
- Tensor identities in Dirac notation: (|u⟩⊗|v⟩)* = ⟨u|⊗⟨v| and |v⟩⟨v| ⊗ |w⟩⟨w| = (|v⟩⊗|w⟩)(⟨v|⊗⟨w|); omitting the ⊗ sign can be dangerously ambiguous (App. A.10, p. 210 [198]) → tensor-products, vectors-and-dirac-notation
- An observable A on the first part of a bipartite system acts as A⊗I, and one on the second part as I⊗B; measuring A and B separately differs from measuring the joint observable A⊗B, though the statistics of the product of outcomes coincide (§1.2.2, p. 17 [5]) → tensor-products
- Example: for |φ⟩ = (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2, separate Z measurements give ++ or −− (state collapses), whereas the joint observable Z⊗Z leaves |φ⟩ undisturbed because it is a +1 eigenstate of Z⊗Z (§1.2.2, p. 17 [5]) → tensor-products, eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors
- The n-qubit Paulis (n-fold tensor products of I, X, Y, Z) number 4^n, form an orthonormal set under the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, and every 2^n×2^n matrix is a unique linear combination of them (App. A.9, p. 210 [198]) → tensor-products

### Probability basics
- Union bound: Pr[A∨B] ≤ Pr[A] + Pr[B], and for T events, Pr[A₁∨…∨A_T] ≤ Σ Pr[A_i] (App. B.2, p. 212 [200]) → probability-basics
- A discrete random variable X takes value x_i with probability p_i; expectation E[X] = Σ p_i x_i, variance Var[X] = E[(X−E[X])²] = E[X²] − E[X]², standard deviation σ (App. B.2, p. 212 [200]) → probability-basics
- Linearity of expectation: E[Σ a_j X_j] = Σ a_j E[X_j]; for independent X, Y, E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]. Independence means Pr[X=x and Y=y] = Pr[X=x]·Pr[Y=y] (App. B.2, p. 212 [200]) → probability-basics
- Tail bounds (App. B.2, p. 213 [201]): Markov Pr[X ≥ kμ] ≤ 1/k for nonnegative X; Chebyshev Pr[|X−μ| ≥ kσ] ≤ 1/k²; Chernoff/Hoeffding for a sum of n i.i.d. {0,1} variables with mean np: Pr[|X−μ| ≥ αn] ≤ 2e^{−2α²n} → probability-basics
- Measurement outcomes are random: outcome probabilities are the squared amplitudes (Born rule), and observing a state induces a probability distribution on classical states (§1.2.2, p. 16 [4]) → probability-basics
- A randomized algorithm uses coin flips so its output is a random variable; "error probability ≤ 1/3" means worst case over every input: correct with probability ≥ 2/3 over the coins and/or quantum measurements (App. B.2, p. 213 [201]) → probability-basics
- Error reduction by repetition: for a 0/1-output algorithm with error ≤ 1/3, run it n = O(log(1/ε)) times (n odd, with 2e^{−2α²n} ≤ ε for α = 1/6) with fresh randomness and output the majority; the Chernoff bound gives error at most ε (App. B.2, p. 213 [201]) → probability-basics
- Union bound for T randomized subroutines each failing with probability ≤ 1/(3T): even if the failures are dependent, the probability that none fails is ≥ 2/3; if independent, the bound (1−1/(3T))^T ≥ 1 − T/(3T) = 2/3 uses the inequality in B.1 (App. B.2, p. 213 [201]) → probability-basics
- Converting expected running time T into worst-case time 3T with error ≤ 1/3 by cutting off after 3T steps, justified by Markov's inequality (App. B.2, p. 213 [201]) → probability-basics
- Useful inequalities: 1 + x ≤ e^x for all real x; for ε_j ∈ [0,1], 1 − Σε_j ≤ Π(1−ε_j) ≤ e^{−Σε_j} (App. B.1, p. 212 [200]) → probability-basics

## qubits-and-circuits

Chapters read for this section: Ch. 1 (§1.3-1.5, exercises), Ch. 2 (§2.1-2.3, exercises), §13.1, Ch. 17. Claims already in the math-foundations section (n-qubit basis states, Born rule, projective measurement, unitarity, Paulis as unitary/Hermitian) are not repeated.

### Qubit
- A qubit is the quantum counterpart of a bit: a system with two basis states |0⟩, |1⟩, which can be in any superposition of the two (§1.3, p. 19 [7]) → qubit
- The notes warn against using "qubit" for a multi-qubit register: the term refers only to the state of a 2-dimensional system; "n-qubit" is fine as an adjective only (footnote 6, §1.3, p. 19 [7]) → qubit
- Measuring just the first qubit of a larger state is a projective measurement with projectors |0⟩⟨0|⊗I and |1⟩⟨1|⊗I; for (1/√3)|0⟩|φ⟩ + √(2/3)|1⟩|ψ⟩ outcome 0 has probability 1/3 and leaves |0⟩|φ⟩, outcome 1 has probability 2/3 and leaves |1⟩|ψ⟩ (§1.3, p. 20 [8]) → qubit
- Teleportation shows a qubit's amplitudes are not simply readable as classical data: sending α₀, α₁ over a classical channel alone is deemed impossible, intuitively since they may need unbounded precision to write down (§1.5, p. 22 [10]) → qubit
- Experimentally (introduction): the first 2-qubit quantum computer was built in 1997, a 5-qubit device factored 15 in 2001, and leading platforms (superconducting, trapped-ion) act on roughly 100-200 qubits; Google's 2019 "quantum supremacy" sampling task used 53 superconducting qubits (§1.1, p. 14 [2]) → qubit
- ⚠️ No Bloch sphere appears in the portions read (no mention found in Ch. 1-2); single-qubit states are treated purely as vectors in C² (§1.3, p. 19 [7]) → qubit

### Single-qubit gates
- A gate is a unitary acting on a small number of qubits (say at most 3), in analogy to classical logic gates (§1.4, p. 21 [9]) → single-qubit-gates
- Bitflip X (also NOT) swaps |0⟩ and |1⟩, matrix [[0,1],[1,0]]; phaseflip Z puts a minus sign on |1⟩, matrix diag(1,−1); I, X, Y, Z are all 1-qubit gates (§1.4, p. 21 [9]) → single-qubit-gates
- The phase gate R_φ = diag(1, e^{iφ}) rotates the phase of |1⟩ by φ and leaves |0⟩; Z = R_π, and R_{π/4} is commonly called the T-gate (§1.4, p. 21 [9]) → single-qubit-gates
- Hadamard H maps |0⟩ to (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 and |1⟩ to (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2, matrix (1/√2)[[1,1],[1,−1]]; applied to |0⟩ or |1⟩ followed by measurement gives a fair coin (§1.4, p. 21 [9]) → single-qubit-gates
- Interference: H applied to (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 returns |0⟩ because the opposite-sign amplitudes of |1⟩ cancel; the notes compare this to interference of light or sound waves (§1.4, p. 21 [9]) → single-qubit-gates
- H is its own inverse (unitary and Hermitian, H = H* = H⁻¹); applying H to each qubit of |0ⁿ⟩ gives the uniform superposition over all n-bit strings, and H^{⊗n}|i⟩ = 2^{−n/2} Σ_j (−1)^{i·j} |j⟩ (Eq. 2.1) (§2.1.2, p. 26 [14]) → single-qubit-gates
- Rotation gate R(θ) = [[cosθ, −sinθ],[sinθ, cosθ]] rotates the amplitude vector counterclockwise by θ; exercises: HXH = Z, and H R_θ H = R_{−θ} (Ex. 1.3 and 1.5, p. 23 [11]; definition used in §17.2, p. 159 [147]) → single-qubit-gates
- Every real 1-qubit unitary is a rotation possibly preceded and followed by Z-gates, and every 1-qubit unitary equals e^{iα}·diag(1,e^{iβ})·rotation(θ)·diag(1,e^{iγ}) (exercises 4 and 5, Ch. 2, p. 32 [20]) → single-qubit-gates

### Multi-qubit states
- A 2-qubit system has the four basis states |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩ (tensor products of single-qubit basis states); an n-qubit register has 2ⁿ basis states and amplitudes with squared moduli summing to 1 (§1.3, p. 19 [7]) → multi-qubit-states
- Counting parameters: a joint classical distribution on spaces of size 2ⁿ and 2^m takes 2^{n+m} numbers, a product distribution only 2ⁿ+2^m; likewise a general (possibly entangled) n+m-qubit state needs 2^{n+m} amplitudes versus 2ⁿ+2^m for a product state (§1.3, p. 20 [8]) → multi-qubit-states
- Product states |φ_A⟩⊗|φ_B⟩ are often written |φ_A⟩|φ_B⟩; further abbreviation to |φ_Aφ_B⟩ is only safe when both are computational basis states (footnote 7, §1.3, p. 20 [8]) → multi-qubit-states
- Parallel gates on different parts of a register compose by tensor product; sequential gates by ordinary matrix product (§2.1.2, p. 26 [14]) → multi-qubit-states
- Despite the parameter-count similarity, quantum states are far more powerful than distributions because amplitudes may be negative or complex, enabling interference; amplitudes become probabilities only after squaring (§1.3, p. 20 [8]) → multi-qubit-states
- Analogy: statistical independence of random variables corresponds to non-entanglement of a product state (§1.3, p. 20 [8]) → multi-qubit-states

### Entanglement
- Entanglement means quantum correlations between qubits; a bipartite state is entangled iff it cannot be written as a tensor product |φ_A⟩⊗|φ_B⟩ (§1.3, p. 20 [8]) → entanglement
- The 2-qubit state (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2 is called an EPR-pair (after Einstein, Podolsky, Rosen); neither qubit has a classical value initially, but measuring the first and seeing 0 collapses the whole state to |00⟩, fixing the second qubit too (§1.3, p. 20 [8]) → entanglement
- Misconception noted: Alice does not hold "|00⟩/√2" and Bob "|11⟩/√2"; the right intuition is a shared random coin flip with amplitudes 1/√2 in place of probabilities 1/2 (footnote 9, §1.5, p. 22 [10]) → entanglement
- Circuit example: H on the first qubit, then CNOT, then Z on the second turns |00⟩ into (|00⟩−|11⟩)/√2, an entangled state (Fig. 2.1, §2.1.2, pp. 27-28 [15-16]) → entanglement
- Entanglement can produce "non-local" effects but cannot signal faster than light; measuring half of an EPR-pair does not change what Bob sees, and a shared coin flip (local realist model) reproduces the computational-basis statistics of that single experiment (intro §1.1, p. 13 [1]; §17.1, p. 157 [145]) → entanglement
- Exercise facts: Alice applying U to her half of an EPR-pair equals Bob applying Uᵀ to his; and (U⊗Ū)|ψ_AB⟩ = |ψ_AB⟩ for n EPR-pairs (Ex. 17.1, 17.3, p. 163 [151]) → entanglement
- State (|00⟩ − |11⟩)/√2 is obtained from an EPR-pair by local operations (Z by Alice); the singlet (|01⟩−|10⟩)/√2 likewise (§17.2, p. 159 [147]; §17.3, p. 161 [149]) → entanglement

### Two-qubit gates
- CNOT negates the second (target) qubit if the first (control) is 1: |0⟩|b⟩→|0⟩|b⟩, |1⟩|b⟩→|1⟩|1−b⟩; matrix permuting the last two basis states (§1.4, pp. 21-22 [9-10]) → two-qubit-gates
- Controlled-U for an n-qubit unitary U is the block matrix diag(I, U) of size 2^{n+1} (§1.4, p. 22 [10]) → two-qubit-gates
- The Toffoli (CCNOT) gate has two controls and negates the third bit only if both are 1 (§2.1.2, p. 26 [14]) → two-qubit-gates
- Toffoli is complete for classical reversible computation: with a fixed 0 on the third wire it gives AND, with fixed 1s on the first two wires it gives NOT, and AND+NOT suffice for any Boolean circuit (§2.1.2, p. 26 [14]) → two-qubit-gates
- In circuit drawings, a control is a dot linked to the target gate; the CNOT target is drawn as ⊕ (§2.1.2, p. 27 [15]) → two-qubit-gates
- Exercises: CNOT is Hermitian and self-inverse; a CNOT can be built from two H gates and one controlled-Z; surrounding CNOT with H⊗H swaps control and target; a SWAP is built from CNOTs; a controlled-U (det U = 1) can be built from CNOTs and A, B, C with ABC = I and AXBXC = U (Ex. 1.4, 2.1-2.3, 2.6, pp. 23, 32 [11, 20]) → two-qubit-gates

### Quantum circuits
- A Boolean circuit is a finite directed acyclic graph of AND/OR/NOT gates with input and designated output nodes; circuit families (one circuit per input size) that are uniformly polynomial correspond exactly to languages in P (§2.1.1, p. 25 [13]) → quantum-circuits
- A quantum circuit generalizes this: the gates are unitaries on at most 2 or 3 qubits; input nodes hold the classical input bits plus extra workspace qubits initialised to |0⟩ (§2.1.2, pp. 26-27 [14-15]) → quantum-circuits
- At the end, some or all qubits are measured in the computational basis to get a classical output; measurement can be treated as a special gate but may be assumed to occur only at the end of the circuit, using one auxiliary qubit per delayed measurement (§2.1.2, p. 27 [15]; Ex. 2.8, p. 32 [20]) → quantum-circuits
- General projective measurements are implemented as a unitary U followed by a computational-basis measurement of the first qubit; to keep the post-measurement state one also applies U⁻¹ afterwards; the circuit for U may be large if the measurement is complicated (§2.1.2, p. 27 [15]) → quantum-circuits
- Because unitaries are reversible, a quantum gate has as many output wires as input wires (unlike AND); the circuit for U⁻¹ is the reversed order of the inverted gates, same size (§2.1.2, p. 27 [15] with footnote 5) → quantum-circuits
- Circuits are drawn with time running left to right, one horizontal wire per qubit (§2.1.2, p. 27 [15]) → quantum-circuits
- Quantum parallelism: a Toffoli-only circuit U from a classical algorithm maps |z⟩|0⟩ to |z⟩|f(z)⟩; applied to a uniform superposition it yields a superposition containing f(z) for all 2ⁿ z with one application, but measuring gives just one random (z, f(z)), so interference and entanglement are needed for real advantage (§2.3, pp. 28-29 [16-17]) → quantum-circuits
- Uncomputation/clean workspace: workspace qubits of a subroutine must be reset to |0⟩ before running it on a superposition, because leftover entanglement between address and workspace registers destroys the intended interference; the exercise shows a phase-query with leftover workspace breaks the HOₓH interference (Ex. 2.11 and comment, p. 33 [21]) → quantum-circuits
- BQP is the class of languages decided with success probability ≥ 2/3 by polynomial-size quantum circuit families (§2.1.2, pp. 27-28 [15-16]) → quantum-circuits

### No-cloning
- Copying an unknown qubit is impossible; this is stated and left as exercise: there is no 2-qubit unitary mapping |φ⟩|0⟩ to |φ⟩|φ⟩ for all qubits |φ⟩ (§1.5, p. 23 [11]; Ex. 1.12, p. 24 [12]) → no-cloning
- Teleportation illustrates the point: the qubit is moved to Bob and destroyed at Alice's side, not copied (§1.5, p. 23 [11]) → no-cloning
- Related exercise: unitaries cannot "delete" information, i.e., there is no 1-qubit unitary mapping every |φ⟩ to |0⟩ (Ex. 1.13, p. 24 [12]) → no-cloning
- ⚠️ The source gives no proof in the text (only the exercise with a hint in App. C) and cites [265] (Wootters-Zurek, per its bibliography numbering, not checked) (§1.5, p. 23 [11]) → no-cloning

### Universal gate sets
- (1) All 1-qubit gates together with CNOT form a universal set: any other unitary can be built from them (§2.2, p. 28 [16]) → universal-gate-sets
- That set is unrealistic since there are continuously many 1-qubit gates and none can be implemented to infinite precision, so one restricts to a small finite set that approximates all 1-qubit gates efficiently (§2.2, p. 28 [16]) → universal-gate-sets
- (2) {CNOT, Hadamard, T = R_{π/4}} is universal in the sense of approximation: any unitary can be approximated arbitrarily well by circuits over these gates (called the "Shor basis" in §13.1) (§2.2, p. 28 [16]; §13.1, p. 121 [109]) → universal-gate-sets
- Solovay-Kitaev theorem: any 1- or 2-qubit gate can be approximated to error ε with polylog(1/ε) gates from the small universal set, so even exponentially small error costs only polynomially many gates (§2.2, p. 28 [16], citing Nielsen-Chuang App. 3) → universal-gate-sets
- (3) {Hadamard, Toffoli} is universal, in the approximate sense, for all unitaries with real entries (§2.2, p. 28 [16]) → universal-gate-sets
- Counting argument: with k gate types each on ≤ 3 qubits, the number of circuits with ≤ C gates is at most (k+1)^C (3C)^{3C} = C^{O(C)}, each circuit computes at most one Boolean function (bounded-error 2/3 sense) (§13.1, p. 121 [109]) → universal-gate-sets
- Consequence: to compute even 1% of all 2^{2ⁿ} Boolean functions on n bits one needs C^{O(C)} ≥ (1/100)·2^{2ⁿ}, hence C ≥ Ω(2ⁿ/n); so almost all functions have circuit complexity near 2ⁿ and few problems are efficiently solvable on a quantum computer. A variant of the Riordan-Shannon classical counting argument (§13.1, p. 121 [109]) → universal-gate-sets
- ⚠️ The §13.1 counting argument is about Boolean functions computed by circuits (with workspace, bounded-error output), not directly about counting "unitaries needing exponentially many gates"; the source does not phrase it as a statement about unitaries (§13.1, p. 121 [109]) → universal-gate-sets
- Elementary-gate-set dependence: the class EQP (exact quantum polynomial time) depends on the allowed gate set, and (footnote in Ch. 14) requiring success probability exactly 1 in QMA would also depend on it (§13.2, p. 122 [110]; footnote 1, §14.1, p. 128 [116]) → universal-gate-sets

### Quantum teleportation
- Setting: Alice holds an unknown qubit α₀|0⟩+α₁|1⟩ and wants Bob to have it; they share an EPR-pair (Alice the left qubit, Bob the right); the protocol uses a classical channel only for communication (§1.5, p. 22 [10]) → quantum-teleportation
- Step 1: Alice applies CNOT (control = her unknown qubit, target = her EPR half) then H on her first qubit; the 3-qubit state is then written as a sum over her four basis outcomes ab of |ab⟩ times a state of Bob's qubit (§1.5, p. 22 [10]) → quantum-teleportation
- Step 2: Alice measures her two qubits in the computational basis and sends the two classical bits ab to Bob; the four outcomes are equally likely (¼ each, from the displayed decomposition) (§1.5, p. 22 [10]) → quantum-teleportation
- Step 3: Bob applies X if b = 1, then Z if a = 1; e.g. for ab = 11 his qubit is α₀|1⟩−α₁|0⟩ and X then Z recovers the original (§1.5, pp. 22-23 [10-11]) → quantum-teleportation
- If Alice's qubit was entangled with other qubits, teleportation preserves that entanglement: Bob's qubit is entangled in the same way (§1.5, p. 23 [11]) → quantum-teleportation
- Teleportation moves rather than copies the qubit (Alice's is destroyed); the introduction notes teleportation has been implemented experimentally as a limited quantum-communication application, defined as sending qubits using entanglement and classical communication (§1.5, p. 23 [11]; §1.1, p. 14 [2]) → quantum-teleportation
- The protocol is given as an early example of a circuit of elementary gates (CNOT, H, measurements and classically-controlled X, Z) (§1.5, p. 22 [10]; §2.1.2, p. 26 [14]) → quantum-teleportation
- Exercise 1.16: if Alice knows the rotation angle θ of the state, one EPR-pair plus 1 classical bit suffices for Bob to obtain |φ⟩, versus 2 bits for teleporting an unknown qubit (Ex. 1.16(d), p. 24 [12]) → quantum-teleportation

### Superdense coding
- Without entanglement, sending a qubit gives Bob at most one bit about Alice's message (footnote 10 attributes this to Holevo's theorem, Ch. 15); with a shared EPR-pair, one transmitted qubit can carry two classical bits (Ex. 1.14, p. 24 [12]) → superdense-coding
- Encoding: Alice, holding bits a, b and her half of an EPR-pair, applies X to her half if a = 1 and then Z if b = 1 (neither for 00, both for 11) (Ex. 1.14(a), p. 24 [12]) → superdense-coding
- Decoding: Alice sends her qubit to Bob, who applies CNOT and Hadamard and measures in the computational basis, getting a on the first qubit and b on the second (Ex. 1.14(b), p. 24 [12]) → superdense-coding
- The four states from the four Pauli operations I, X, Y, Z on Alice's half of an EPR-pair form an orthonormal set (hence perfectly distinguishable by a 4-outcome projective measurement by Bob) (Ex. 1.15(b)(c), p. 24 [12]) → superdense-coding
- Key tool: Tr((C⊗I)|ψ⟩⟨ψ|) = ½Tr(C) for an EPR-pair |ψ⟩ (Ex. 1.15(a), p. 24 [12]) → superdense-coding
- ⚠️ Superdense coding is presented only as an exercise (with a hint-free outline), not worked in the main text (Ex. 1.14, p. 24 [12]) → superdense-coding

### Bell inequalities
- Einstein wanted a "local realist" replacement for quantum mechanics (local: no faster-than-light influence; realist: definite properties); Bell in the 1960s devised entanglement experiments whose predicted outputs no local realist theory can reproduce ("quantum non-locality") (§17.1, pp. 157-158 [145-146]) → bell-inequalities
- Aspect and others did such experiments in the early 1980s with outcomes matching quantum mechanics; they disprove local realist theories rather than prove quantum mechanics, modulo experimental loopholes; one experiment simultaneously closed the two most important loopholes (§17.1, p. 158 [146], footnotes 2-3) → bell-inequalities
- Non-locality disproves the conjunction of locality and realism, not locality alone (footnote 3, §17.1, p. 158 [146]) → bell-inequalities
- Game formulation: Alice and Bob get inputs x, y, may not communicate, may share a classical random variable (local hidden variable, covering all local realist models) or entangled states, and must output a, b with a prescribed correlation (§17.1, p. 158 [146], Fig. 17.1) → bell-inequalities
- CHSH game: win iff a ⊕ b = x ∧ y (§17.2, p. 159 [147]) → bell-inequalities
- Classical bound: a deterministic strategy is fixed by four bits (a₀, a₁, b₀, b₁), and the four winning conditions sum mod 2 to 0 = 1, so at most 3 of 4 inputs can be won; shared randomness is a mixture of deterministic strategies, so 3/4 is the best classical probability (worst-case input), and 3/4 is achievable for every input (Ex. 17.4) (§17.2, p. 159 [147]) → bell-inequalities
- Quantum strategy: share (|00⟩−|11⟩)/√2; Alice rotates her qubit by R(−π/16) if x = 0 and R(3π/16) if x = 1, Bob does the same with y, both measure in the computational basis; the state becomes (cos(θ_A+θ_B)(|00⟩−|11⟩)+sin(θ_A+θ_B)(|01⟩+|10⟩))/√2, so a ⊕ b = 0 with probability cos²(θ_A+θ_B); θ_A+θ_B = ±π/8 when x∧y = 0 and 3π/8 when x∧y = 1, giving success cos²(π/8) ≈ 0.85 on all inputs (§17.2, p. 159 [147]) → bell-inequalities
- Upper bounds on the success probability of classical strategies for a game are called Bell inequalities; this particular one is the CHSH inequality (footnote 4, §17.2, p. 159 [147]) → bell-inequalities
- Tsirelson: cos²(π/8) is optimal for quantum strategies, even with arbitrary amounts of entanglement (§17.2, p. 160 [148]); Ex. 17.7 (p. 164 [152]) derives it by writing the winning probability as ½ + ⅛⟨ψ|C|ψ⟩ with C = A₀⊗B₀ + A₀⊗B₁ + A₁⊗B₀ − A₁⊗B₁ and showing ⟨ψ|C|ψ⟩ ≤ √8 → bell-inequalities
- Other non-local games: magic square game (classical best 8/9, quantum strategy with two EPR-type pairs always wins) and Mermin/GHZ game (classical at most 3/4, quantum wins with certainty) (§17.3, pp. 160-162 [148-150]; Ex. 17.5, p. 164 [152]) → bell-inequalities

### Query model and oracles
- Virtually all quantum algorithms work with queries; the input is a "black-box" (oracle), so the exponential quantum-classical separations shown with queries do not by themselves give exponential separations in the standard circuit model (same for Simon) (§2.4, p. 29 [17]) → query-model-and-oracles
- Setting: an N-bit input x with N = 2^n, accessed like a RAM: n address qubits plus one target qubit; the standard (bit) oracle is the permutation unitary O_x: |i,b⟩ → |i, b⊕x_i⟩ (the defining form |i,0⟩ → |i,x_i⟩ alone would not fix a unitary); one application is a "query" and counting queries is the cost measure (§2.4, p. 29 [17]) → query-model-and-oracles
- A quantum computer can apply O_x to a superposition of indices i, which a classical computer cannot do (§2.4, p. 29 [17]) → query-model-and-oracles
- Common beginner mistake flagged: confusing the N bits of x with the n address bits (footnote 6, §2.4, p. 29 [17]) → query-model-and-oracles
- The phase oracle ("phase-query") is O_{x,±}: |i⟩ → (−1)^{x_i}|i⟩ on n qubits; it can be built from the standard oracle by setting the target to |−⟩ = H|1⟩, and is often more convenient (§2.4, pp. 29-30 [17-18]) → query-model-and-oracles
- Formal definition of query complexity: a T-query algorithm interleaves fixed unitaries U_0…U_T with O_x, U_T O_x U_{T−1} … O_x U_0 |0…0⟩; the query complexity of f is the minimal T for an algorithm that outputs f(x) with error ≤ 1/3 on every x; fixed unitaries are treated as free (§11.1, pp. 101-102 [89-90]) → query-model-and-oracles
- N queries always suffice (query every bit), so query complexity is at most N (footnote 2, §11.1, p. 102 [90]) → query-model-and-oracles
- Justification of the proxy: for most algorithms studied (Deutsch-Jozsa, Simon, Grover, walks), total gate count is not much bigger than query count; even Shor's period-finding is analysed via few queries to the periodic function (§11.1, p. 102 [90]) → query-model-and-oracles
- Exercise: a standard query can be implemented from one controlled phase-query; whether uncontrolled phase-queries suffice is posed as an open-ended exercise (Ex. 2.10, p. 33 [21]) → query-model-and-oracles
- For Simon's problem the entries x_i are themselves n-bit strings; one such query can be simulated by n binary queries, so the n·2^n binary-variable view changes query counts only by a factor n (§3.1, p. 35 [23]) → query-model-and-oracles

### Phase kickback
- Applying O_x to |i⟩|−⟩ gives |i⟩(|x_i⟩−|1−x_i⟩)/√2 = (−1)^{x_i}|i⟩|−⟩: the output bit ends up in the phase of the address register; the notes call this the "phase kick-back trick" (§2.4, pp. 29-30 [17-18], footnote 7) → phase-kickback
- With target |+⟩ instead, O_x(|i⟩|+⟩) = |i⟩|+⟩ for any x, so putting a control qubit in |−⟩ for chosen indices and |+⟩ for others selects on which part of the state the phase-query acts (footnote 7, §2.4, p. 30 [18]) → phase-kickback
- Phase estimation uses the same idea: applying U^j to an eigenvector |ψ⟩ with eigenvalue e^{2πiφ} gives the phase factor e^{2πiφj} on |j⟩, so the eigenvalue phase appears on the control register while |ψ⟩ is unchanged (§4.6, p. 46 [34]) → phase-kickback
- ⚠️ The notes name the trick only in footnote 7 for the oracle case; the controlled-U on an eigenvector is described in step 3 of phase estimation without the label "kickback" (§4.6, p. 46 [34]) → phase-kickback

### Deutsch-Jozsa
- Problem: x ∈ {0,1}^N with N = 2^n is promised to be either constant (all x_i equal) or balanced (N/2 zeros and N/2 ones); decide which (§2.4.1, p. 30 [18]) → deutsch-jozsa
- Algorithm: start in |0^n⟩, apply H^{⊗n}, one phase-query O_{x,±}, H^{⊗n} again, measure; as a unitary H^{⊗n} O_{x,±} H^{⊗n} (§2.4.1, p. 30 [18]) → deutsch-jozsa
- Analysis: after the first H^{⊗n} the state is the uniform superposition; after the query it is 2^{−n/2} Σ_i (−1)^{x_i}|i⟩; the final amplitude of |0^n⟩ is 2^{−n} Σ_i (−1)^{x_i}, which is ±1 if x is constant and 0 if balanced, so observing |0^n⟩ means constant and any other outcome means balanced (§2.4.1, pp. 30-31 [18-19]) → deutsch-jozsa
- Cost: 1 quantum query and O(n) other operations, with certainty; the original Deutsch-Jozsa solution used 2 queries, the 1-query solution is attributed to Cleve et al. (§2.4.1, p. 31 [19]) → deutsch-jozsa
- Classical deterministic algorithms need at least N/2 + 1 queries (after N/2 zeros the answer is still undetermined), i.e. an exponential separation in n versus the exact quantum algorithm (§2.4.1, p. 31 [19]; Ch. 3 intro, p. 35 [23]) → deutsch-jozsa
- A randomized classical algorithm that queries two random positions and says "constant" iff they are equal is always right on constant inputs and right with probability 1/2 on balanced inputs, so (with error probability allowed) the separation disappears; Ex. 2.12 asks for a 2-query algorithm with success ≥ 2/3 on every input (§2.4.1, p. 31 [19]; Ex. 2.12, p. 33 [21]) → deutsch-jozsa
- The separation therefore holds only against errorless classical algorithms (§2.4.1, p. 31 [19]; §16.3 intro, p. 150 [138]) → deutsch-jozsa
- Deutsch-Jozsa for n = 1 is an instance of the hidden subgroup problem (left as Ex. 6.1) (Ex. 6.1, p. 63 [51]) → deutsch-jozsa

### Bernstein-Vazirani and Simon
- Bernstein-Vazirani problem: x_i = (i·a) mod 2 for an unknown a ∈ {0,1}^n; find a (§2.4.2, p. 31 [19]) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- The BV algorithm is exactly the Deutsch-Jozsa circuit: after the query the state is 2^{−n/2} Σ_i (−1)^{i·a}|i⟩, and the final H^{⊗n} turns it into the basis state |a⟩, using 1 query and O(n) other operations (§2.4.2, p. 31 [19]) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Any classical algorithm, even randomized with small error, needs n queries for information-theoretic reasons (n answer bits, at most 1 bit per query); this is a polynomial (not exponential) separation versus randomized algorithms (§2.4.2, p. 31 [19]; Ch. 3 intro, p. 35 [23]) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- A recursive version of BV can be solved exactly with poly(n) quantum steps but needs n^{Ω(log n)} classical randomized queries (§2.4.2, p. 31 [19]) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Simon's problem: x_i ∈ {0,1}^n with a hidden nonzero s such that x_i = x_j iff i = j or i = j⊕s (a 2-to-1 function with mask s); find s (§3.1, p. 35 [23]) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Quantum subroutine: H^{⊗n} on the first register, one query to get Σ_i |i⟩|x_i⟩, measure the second register (collapse to (|i⟩+|i⊕s⟩)/√2, measurement is not strictly necessary), apply H^{⊗n}; the amplitude of |j⟩ is proportional to (−1)^{i·j}(1+(−1)^{s·j}), so the outcome is a uniformly random j with s·j = 0 mod 2 (§3.2, pp. 35-36 [23-24]) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Classical post-processing: repeat until n−1 linearly independent equations j·s = 0 are collected, then solve by Gaussian elimination mod 2 (about O(n³) classical gates); if the j's so far span a space of size 2^k with k < n−1, the next run gives a new independent j with probability ≥ 1/2, so expected O(n) runs/queries and polynomially many other operations (§3.2, p. 36 [24]) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Classical upper bound: a birthday-paradox algorithm queries T ≈ √(2^{n+1}) random distinct positions, expecting about one collision i_k ⊕ i_ℓ = s, so O(√(2^n)) queries suffice (§3.3.1, pp. 36-37 [24-25]) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Classical lower bound (Simon): any randomized algorithm needs Ω(√(2^n)) queries; shown for the decision version (s = 0^n or not) with a hard distribution (random permutation vs. random 2-to-1 input), arguing that until a collision is seen the two cases give identical answer distributions, and the collision probability for T queries is ≤ T²/2^n (§3.3.2, pp. 37-38 [25-26]) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- This was the first proven exponential separation between quantum and bounded-error classical algorithms, but in the query model only, not the circuit model; Simon's algorithm inspired Shor's factoring algorithm and was later used to break some classical cryptosystems (Ch. 3 intro, p. 35 [23]; §3.3.2, p. 37 [25]) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Simon's problem is the hidden subgroup problem with G = Z_2^n and H = {0,s}; the H^{⊗n} is the QFT of Z_2^n and the sampled j lie in H^⊥ = {j : j·s = 0} (§6.1.2, p. 58 [46]; §6.2.2, p. 61 [49]) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon
- Generalisation (exercise): for a hidden subspace V, one run yields a j orthogonal to all of V (Ex. 3.4, p. 39 [27]) → bernstein-vazirani-and-simon

### Quantum Fourier transform
- The (classical) Fourier transform is the N×N unitary F_N with (j,k) entry ω_N^{jk}/√N, ω_N = e^{2πi/N} a primitive N-th root of unity; for N = 2 it is the Hadamard matrix; unitarity follows from orthogonality of columns via the geometric sum (§4.1, p. 41 [29]) → quantum-fourier-transform
- F_N is symmetric, so F_N^{-1} = F_N* only flips the sign in the exponent (§4.1, p. 41 [29]) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Classical FFT (Cooley-Tukey 1965): naive matrix-vector multiplication costs O(N²); the FFT computes the transform in O(N log N) by splitting into two N/2-dimensional transforms on the even and odd entries, with recursion T(N) = 2T(N/2) + O(N) (§4.2, p. 42 [30]) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Application of the FFT: multiply two degree-d polynomials in O(d log d) steps by transforming, multiplying entrywise (convolution theorem) and inverting (§4.3, pp. 42-43 [30-31]) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Definition of the QFT: the same unitary F_N viewed as a quantum operation on amplitudes; for N = 2^n it is an n-qubit unitary |k⟩ → N^{-1/2} Σ_j ω_N^{jk}|j⟩ (§4.4, p. 43 [31]) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Difference from the FFT: the QFT acts on amplitudes of a state that are not written down anywhere; it does not output the entries on paper but only as amplitudes of the resulting state (§4.4, pp. 43-44 [31-32]) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Circuit: F_N|k⟩ is a product state ⊗_{ℓ} (|0⟩ + e^{2πi·0.k_{n−ℓ+1}…k_n}|1⟩)/√2 (a binary-fraction phase on each qubit); it is built from Hadamards and controlled-R_s gates with R_s = diag(1, e^{2πi/2^s}) (R_1 = Z, R_2 = diag(1,i)), followed by swaps to reverse the qubit order (§4.5, pp. 44-45 [32-33]) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Gate count: n qubits with at most n gates each gives at most n² gates, i.e. O(n²) = O((log N)²); omitting the controlled-R_s with s ≫ log n (Coppersmith) leaves O(log n) gates per qubit and O(n log n) gates overall with small error; the inverse circuit is the reversed circuit with adjoint gates (§4.5, p. 45 [33]; Ex. 4.4 on error, p. 47 [35]) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Comparison: O(n²) gates is exponentially faster than the FFT's O(N log N) = O(2^n n) steps, though it achieves something different (§4.4, p. 44 [32]) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Characters view: the columns of F_N are the characters of Z_N, and the QFT maps basis states |k⟩ to character states |χ_k⟩; for Z_2^n the QFT is H^{⊗n}, which is very different from the QFT over Z_{2^n} (§6.2.1, pp. 59-60 [47-48]) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Exercise facts: F_N^4 = I (Ex. 4.6b, p. 48 [36]); Fourier transform of an r-periodic vector is explored in Ex. 4.5 (p. 47 [35]) → quantum-fourier-transform

### Phase estimation
- Task: given a unitary U that can be applied and an eigenvector |ψ⟩ with unknown eigenvalue λ = e^{2πiφ} (φ ∈ [0,1)), approximate φ; originally due to Kitaev, put in a broader context by Cleve et al., now a common subroutine (§4.6, pp. 45-46 [33-34]) → phase-estimation
- Algorithm: (1) start with |0^n⟩|ψ⟩; (2) F_N (or H^{⊗n}) on the first n qubits for the uniform superposition; (3) map |j⟩|ψ⟩ → |j⟩U^j|ψ⟩ = e^{2πiφj}|j⟩|ψ⟩, done as controlled applications of U^{2^{n−1}}, …, U²,U controlled on the bits of j; (4) apply the inverse QFT F_N^{-1} to the first n qubits and measure (§4.6, p. 46 [34]) → phase-estimation
- After step 3 the first register is F_N|2^nφ⟩, so if φ has an exact n-bit binary expansion the inverse QFT returns |φ_1…φ_n⟩ with probability 1 (§4.6, p. 46 [34]) → phase-estimation
- If φ does not fit in n bits, then with high probability the output is an n-bit approximation with additive error O(2^{−n}); the notes omit the calculation (§4.6, p. 46 [34]) → phase-estimation
- Cost: 1 + 2 + … + 2^{n−1} = 2^n − 1 controlled applications of U in total; to get error ±ε use n = ⌈log(1/ε)⌉ bits, hence ≤ 2^n = O(1/ε) applications of U (§4.6, p. 46 [34]) → phase-estimation
- Phase estimation reappears as the eigenvalue-reading subroutine in HHL and in amplitude estimation, where a rotation by 2θ has eigenphase related to √a (§10.2, p. 96 [84]; Ex. 7.8, pp. 72-73 [60-61]) → phase-estimation
- ⚠️ The success probability for non-exact phases is only stated as "high probability" with no explicit constant (such as 4/π²); the notes say the calculation is omitted (§4.6, p. 46 [34]) → phase-estimation

### Shor's algorithm
- Claim: finds a factor of a composite N in roughly (log N)² steps (slightly worse, see below), polynomial in the input length log N; best known classical randomized factoring algorithms take about 2^{(log N)^α} with α = 1/3 (heuristic) or 1/2 (less heuristic) (§5.1, p. 49 [37]) → shor-algorithm
- Complexity-class remarks: the decision version of factoring is provably in BQP but not known to be in BPP; if it is not in BPP, quantum computers would be the first counterexample to the strong Church-Turing thesis (§5.1, p. 49 [37]) → shor-algorithm
- Reduction to period-finding: assume N odd, not a prime power; pick random x in {2,…,N−1}; if gcd(x,N) > 1 we have a factor; otherwise x lies in Z_N^* and has a least period (order) r with x^r = 1 mod N (§5.2, pp. 49-50 [37-38]) → shor-algorithm
- With probability ≥ 1/2 the period r is even and x^{r/2} ± 1 are not multiples of N; then (x^{r/2}+1)(x^{r/2}−1) = kN, and gcd(x^{r/2} ± 1, N) are both nontrivial factors; otherwise try another x (§5.2, p. 50 [38]) → shor-algorithm
- Period-finding problem: f with f(a) = f(b) iff a = b mod r, find r; classically r may be huge (e.g. N^{1/2}), so scanning is exponential; it is believed classical computers cannot do it efficiently. Quantum: O(log log N) evaluations of f and QFTs (reducible to O(1) per footnote 3); any classical bounded-error algorithm for a somewhat more general version needs Ω(N^{1/3}/√log N) evaluations (§5.2, p. 50 [38]; footnote 3, §5.3, p. 52 [40]) → shor-algorithm
- Quantum core: choose q = 2^ℓ with N² < q ≤ 2N²; start with |0^ℓ⟩|0^n⟩, make a uniform superposition on the first register, compute f(a) into the second, measure the second register (leaving Σ_j |jr+s⟩), apply the QFT F_q to the first register and measure (§5.3, pp. 51-52 [39-40]) → shor-algorithm
- Structure (Fourier, f-evaluation, Fourier) parallels Simon's (Hadamard, query, Hadamard); Shor was inspired by Simon's paper (footnote 2, §5.3, p. 51 [39]) → shor-algorithm
- Easy case r | q: the measured b is a uniformly random multiple c·q/r with c in {0,…,r−1}, so b/q = c/r; c is coprime to r with probability Ω(1/log log N) (via φ(r) ∈ Ω(r/log log r)), so O(log log N) repetitions suffice, and r is read off as the denominator of b/q in lowest terms (§5.3, p. 52 [40]) → shor-algorithm
- Hard case r ∤ q: with probability ≥ 4/π² the outcome b satisfies |b/q − c/r| ≤ 1/(2q); since two distinct fractions with denominators ≤ N differ by at least 1/N² > 1/q, c/r is the unique fraction with denominator ≤ N within 1/(2q) of b/q, found with continued fractions (§5.3, p. 53 [41]; footnote 4) → shor-algorithm
- Continued fractions: [a_0,a_1,…] = a_0 + 1/(a_1 + 1/(a_2+…)); convergents p_n/q_n follow p_n = a_n p_{n−1}+p_{n−2}, q_n = a_n q_{n−1}+q_{n−2}, are in lowest terms, q_n grows at least exponentially, |x − p_n/q_n| < 1/q_n², and p_n/q_n is the best approximation among fractions with denominator ≤ q_n; the expansion of x is computed by repeatedly taking the integer part and inverting the remainder (§5.4, pp. 53-54 [41-42]) → shor-algorithm
- Gate cost: f(a) = x^a mod N by repeated squaring takes O((log N)² log log N log log log N) steps (using fast integer multiplication, Ex. 5.1); the QFT takes O((log N)²); total expected O((log N)² (log log N)² log log log N) gates, slightly worse than quadratic in the input length (§5.2, pp. 50-51 [38-39]; §4.3, p. 43 [31] for Schönhage-Strassen) → shor-algorithm
- Qubits: about 3 log N (2 log N for the first register, ⌈log N⌉ for the second), reducible to slightly more than 2 log N, e.g. slightly more than 4096 perfect qubits to factor a 2048-bit integer; later using q = O(N) instead of O(N²) achieves the ~2 log N (footnote 2, §5.3, p. 51 [39]; footnote 5, §6.2.2, p. 61 [49]) → shor-algorithm
- Hidden-subgroup view: period-finding is the HSP on G = Z_{φ(N)} with H = ⟨r⟩; the general Abelian HSP "standard algorithm" (uniform superposition over G, compute f, measure the second register leaving a coset s+H, apply the QFT of G, measure) samples uniformly from H^⊥ and gives enough constraints to find generators of H (§6.1.2, p. 58 [46]; §6.2.2, pp. 60-61 [48-49]) → shor-algorithm
- Discrete logarithm: given generator γ of a cyclic group C of size N and A ∈ C, find a with γ^a = A; classical computers are believed to need time roughly exponential in log N (provable in a generic black-box group model); this underlies Diffie-Hellman key exchange and elliptic-curve cryptography (§6.1.2, p. 58 [46]) → shor-algorithm
- Discrete log as HSP: G = Z_N × Z_N, f(x,y) = γ^x A^{−y}, hidden subgroup H = ⟨(a,1)⟩, with H^⊥ = {χ_{(c,−ac)}}; sampling gives (c,−ac), so a is recovered if gcd(c,N) = 1 (repeat a few times); the QFT F_q ⊗ F_q with q a power of 2 slightly larger than N replaces F_N ⊗ F_N (§6.2.2, p. 61 [49]) → shor-algorithm
- Abelian HSP is solved efficiently by a quantum algorithm; extensions to infinite groups yield efficient algorithms for e.g. Pell's equation (§6.2, p. 59 [47]; §6.2.2, p. 62 [50]) → shor-algorithm
- Non-Abelian HSP (e.g. graph isomorphism via the symmetric group S_{2n}) has no general efficient quantum algorithm; the standard algorithm with a non-Abelian QFT does not always give enough information; only query-efficient (polylog(|G|) queries to f, but not necessarily efficient gates) and special cases (normal subgroups, solvable groups) are known (§6.3, pp. 62-63 [50-51]) → shor-algorithm

### Grover search
- Search problem: given arbitrary x ∈ {0,1}^N, find i with x_i = 1 or report none; t = number of solutions (Hamming weight). Classically a randomized algorithm needs Θ(N) queries; Grover uses O(√N) queries and O(√N log N) other gates (§7.1, p. 65 [53]) → grover-search
- Grover iterate: G = H^{⊗n} R_0 H^{⊗n} O_{x,±}, where R_0 puts −1 on all basis states except |0^n⟩ (O(n) gates, independent of x); one iterate uses 1 query and O(log N) other gates (§7.2, p. 65 [53]) → grover-search
- Algorithm: prepare |U⟩ = H^{⊗n}|0^n⟩, apply G k times, measure; each iteration moves amplitude from the indices of 0-bits to those of 1-bits (§7.2, pp. 65-66 [53-54]) → grover-search
- G is a product of two reflections: O_{x,±} reflects through |B⟩ (the non-solutions, within the plane spanned by |G⟩ and |B⟩) and H^{⊗n}R_0H^{⊗n} = 2|U⟩⟨U| − I reflects through |U⟩; a reflection through V is 2P_V − I (§7.2, p. 66 [54], with footnote 2) → grover-search
- Geometric picture: with good/bad states |G⟩ = t^{-1/2}Σ_{x_i=1}|i⟩ and |B⟩ = (N−t)^{-1/2}Σ_{x_i=0}|i⟩, |U⟩ = sin θ|G⟩ + cos θ|B⟩ with θ = arcsin √(t/N); each iteration rotates the state by 2θ, so after k iterations it is sin((2k+1)θ)|G⟩ + cos((2k+1)θ)|B⟩ (§7.2, pp. 66-67 [54-55]) → grover-search
- Success probability after k iterations is P_k = sin²((2k+1)θ); ideal k̃ = π/(4θ) − 1/2, rounded to the nearest integer; failure probability ≤ sin²θ = t/N, and k ≤ (π/4)√(N/t) queries; if t = N/4 one iteration succeeds with certainty (θ = π/6) (§7.2, p. 67 [55]; Ex. 7.3, p. 71 [59]) → grover-search
- Algebraic version (Boyer et al.): amplitudes a_k = sin((2k+1)θ)/√t on solutions and b_k = cos((2k+1)θ)/√(N−t) on non-solutions satisfy the recursion from H^{⊗n}R_0H^{⊗n} = 2J/N − I (§7.2, pp. 67-68 [55-56]) → grover-search
- If k is too large the success probability goes down again, so one must stop at the right time (§7.2, p. 68 [56]) → grover-search
- Unknown number of solutions t: run with exponentially increasing guesses for k (Boyer et al.); an expected O(√(N/t)) queries suffice; t = 0 is detected by checking outputs; with a known lower bound τ on t the expected cost is O(√(N/τ)), convertible to a worst case by cutting off at 3× expected (Markov, success ≥ 2/3) (§7.2, p. 68 [56]) → grover-search
- Exact version: if t is known exactly, the algorithm can be tweaked (smaller angle) to land exactly on the good state (§7.2, p. 68 [56]; Ex. 7.5, p. 71 [59]) → grover-search
- Error ε without knowing t: O(√(N log(1/ε))) queries, with log(1/ε) inside the square root, better than O(log(1/ε)) naive repetitions giving O(√N log(1/ε)) (§7.2, p. 68 [56]; Ex. 7.6, p. 72 [60]) → grover-search
- Amplitude amplification: for a unitary A (no measurements) with A|0^m⟩ = √p|ψ_1⟩ + √(1−p)|ψ_0⟩ ("good" and "bad" orthogonal), reflect through the bad state with R_G (e.g. a Z gate on a flag qubit) and through |U⟩ = A|0^m⟩ with A R_0 A^{-1}; each pair of reflections rotates by 2θ, θ = arcsin √p; after k rounds the state is sin((2k+1)θ)|ψ_1⟩ + cos((2k+1)θ)|ψ_0⟩, so O(1/√p) rounds suffice (§7.3, pp. 68-69 [56-57]) → grover-search
- If p is unknown, try exponentially decreasing guesses as for Grover; A must be unitary with no measurements because A^{-1} is needed (§7.3, p. 69 [57]) → grover-search
- Grover is the special case A = H^{⊗n}, p = t/N, R_G = phase-query to x (§7.3, p. 69 [57]) → grover-search
- Speed-up scope: any unitary algorithm A with success probability p and an efficient solution check can be boosted to near 1 with O(1/√p) runs of A and A^{-1}, where classically O(1/p) repetitions are needed (§7.3, p. 70 [58]) → grover-search
- Application: SAT via x_i = φ(i), with oracle built as U_φ, then Z on the answer qubit, then U_φ^{-1} (to clean workspace), giving about √(2^n) times a polynomial factor; this is only a quadratic speed-up over brute force, and for problems like TSP better classical algorithms (Bellman-Held-Karp, 2^n·poly) beat Grover-on-brute-force (§7.4, p. 70 [58]) → grover-search
- Practical caveat exercise: with quantum gates much more expensive (constant C ≫ c) a quadratic speed-up only pays off for large N (Ex. 7.2, p. 71 [59]) → grover-search
- Optimality (polynomial method): a T-query algorithm's final amplitudes are multilinear polynomials of degree ≤ T in the bits of x, so acceptance probability is a polynomial of degree ≤ 2T; an algorithm computing OR with error ≤ 1/3 induces a single-variable polynomial r with r(0) ∈ [0,1/3] and r(t) ∈ [2/3,1] for t = 1..N, which by a theorem of approximation theory (Ehlich-Zeller, Rivlin-Cheney) needs degree Ω(√N); hence T = Ω(√N) and Grover is optimal up to a constant (§11.2, pp. 102-104 [90-92]) → grover-search
- Optimality (adversary method): with relation R = {(0^N, y) : |y| = 1}, m_0 = N, m_1 = ℓ_0 = ℓ_1 = 1, the simple adversary bound Ω(√(m_0m_1/(ℓ_0ℓ_1))) gives Ω(√N); the generalized adversary bound with an (N+1)×(N+1) matrix gives ‖Γ‖ ≥ √N and ‖Γ_i‖ = 1, hence again Ω(√N) (§11.3, p. 106 [94]; §12.1, p. 112 [100]); Ex. 11.12 (p. 108 [96]) gives a self-contained hybrid-method proof T ≥ √N/4 → grover-search
- Exact Grover is impossible at O(√N): a T-query exact algorithm for OR induces a degree-≤2T polynomial with r(0) = 0 and r(t) = 1 for t = 1..N, which needs degree at least N, so T ≥ N/2 (improvable to N, Ex. 11.5), i.e. no square-root speed-up if the algorithm must succeed with probability 1 on the OR instance as defined there (§11.2, p. 104 [92]) → grover-search
- ⚠️ The note on exactness (§7.2 says an exact variant exists when t is known; §11.2 says exact OR needs N/2 queries) is consistent: the exact variant needs the number of solutions to be known, while exact OR must work for all t in 1..N (§7.2, p. 68 [56]; §11.2, p. 104 [92]) → grover-search
- Further consequences: for every symmetric total function quantum algorithms give at most a quadratic speed-up; for every total function at most a power-6 speed-up (Ex. 11.11, block sensitivity); promise problems such as Deutsch-Jozsa and Simon are excluded from this (§11.2, p. 104 [92], with footnote 6) → grover-search
- Adversary method (simple version, Ambainis): progress measure S_t = Σ_{(x,y)∈R}|⟨ψ_t^x|ψ_t^y⟩|, unchanged by the input-independent unitaries; a bounded-error algorithm needs final inner products ≤ 17/18; with parameters m_0,m_1,ℓ_0,ℓ_1 the bound is T = Ω(√(m_0m_1/(ℓ_0ℓ_1))); it gives the Ω(√N) bound for OR and an optimal Ω(√N) bound for the 2-level AND-OR tree (§11.3, pp. 105-106 [93-94]) → query-model-and-oracles
- The polynomial method also lower-bounds collision-finding at Ω(N^{2/3}), matching the quantum walk algorithm (footnote 3, §11.2, p. 103 [91]) → query-model-and-oracles
- The generalized adversary bound (Høyer-Lee-Špalek): every algorithm with error ≤ ε needs at least (1/2 − √(ε(1−ε)))·‖Γ‖/max_i‖Γ_i‖ queries; the optimised value ADV±(f) is a semidefinite program and (per the chapter's opening and §12.1) gives optimal query complexity for all Boolean functions (§12.1, pp. 111-114 [99-102]) → query-model-and-oracles

### Hamiltonian simulation
- Motivation: the Hamiltonian H is the energy observable; the Schrödinger equation gives the unitary U = e^{−iHt} (time-independent H); simulating quantum systems (quantum chemistry, materials) is hard classically because the number of amplitudes is exponential in the number of particles, and this was Feynman's main reason for proposing quantum computers (§9.1, pp. 83-84 [71-72]) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Problem statement: given H, t, ε, find a small circuit Ũ that is ε-close in operator norm to e^{iHt} (minus sign dropped), working for every input state; the system is assumed to be n qubits (other Hilbert spaces are encoded in binary, "much of the art" of quantum chemistry) (§9.1, p. 84 [72]) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Complexity-theoretic context: finding the ground-state energy of a Hamiltonian is QMA-hard even for 2-local Hamiltonians, so it is believed hard even for a quantum computer; and if factoring is classically hard, Shor implies quantum computers cannot be simulated in polynomial time classically (footnotes 2-3, §9.1, p. 84 [72]) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Structured Hamiltonians: H = Σ_{j=1}^m H_j with m = poly(n) terms each acting on few qubits (2-local = acting on two qubits); then each e^{iH_j t} is a 2-qubit gate; also s-sparse Hamiltonians (≤ s nonzero entries per column) with efficient sparse access are used, and locality roughly implies sparsity (§9.2, p. 85 [73] and footnote 5) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Product formulas: if the H_j commute then e^{iHt} = Π e^{iH_jt}, but generally e^{A+B} ≠ e^A e^B when A, B do not commute (Ex. 9.1 shows this with Paulis); the Lie-Suzuki-Trotter decomposition uses e^{A+B} = e^A e^B + E with ‖E‖ = O(‖A‖‖B‖) for small-norm A, B (§9.2, p. 85 [73]; Ex. 9.1, p. 91 [79]) → hamiltonian-simulation
- First-order Trotter (Lloyd): U = (e^{iHt/r})^r ≈ (e^{iH_1t/r}···e^{iH_mt/r})^r; per-step error O(m²t²/r²), total ≤ ε if r = O(m²t²/ε), giving O(m³t²/ε) two-qubit gates (m=2 case: O(t²/ε) gates); errors of a product of unitaries add at most linearly (§9.2, p. 85 [73]) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Dependence: quadratic in t and polynomial in 1/ε; higher-order Lie-Suzuki-Trotter formulas make the t-dependence nearly linear, e.g. the symmetric split e^{iηA/2}e^{iηB}e^{iηA/2} has error O(η³) and gives O(t^{1.5}/√ε) gates (Ex. 9.4, p. 91 [79]; §9.2, p. 85 [73]) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Practical remark: although Trotter has theoretically worse bounds in t and ε than the other methods, in practice it is quite competitive, is simple, and needs no auxiliary qubits (footnote 7, §9.2, pp. 85-86 [73-74]) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Method 2, linear combination of unitaries (LCU, Berry et al.): write H = Σ α_jV_j (e.g. a 2-local term is ≤ 16 Pauli strings), expand e^{iHt} by a Taylor series truncated at k = O(t + log(1/ε)), and implement the truncated sum with LCU plus oblivious amplitude amplification; for large t, split into blocks of time 1/‖α‖_1; the total cost is O(t‖α‖_1 log(t‖α‖_1/ε)) = O(mt log(mt/ε)) applications of the select-V unitaries, linear in t and logarithmic in 1/ε (§9.3, pp. 86-88 [74-76]) → hamiltonian-simulation
- LCU basic step: with W|0⟩ = Σ√(α_j/‖α‖_1)|j⟩ and select-V = Σ|j⟩⟨j|⊗V_j, applying W, V, W^{-1} gives (1/‖α‖_1)|0⟩M|ψ⟩ + (rest with first register ≠ 0) (§9.3, pp. 86-87 [74-75]) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Method 3, block-encodings and quantum singular value transformation (Gilyén et al., building on Low et al.): a unitary U with A in its upper-left block is a block-encoding of A; given a degree-d polynomial P (range ≤ 1/4), a block-encoding of P(A) is implemented with d applications of U and U^{-1}; for s-sparse H with sparse access, Hamiltonian simulation costs O(st + log(1/ε)) queries to H and O(n(st + log(1/ε))) two-qubit gates, i.e. additive in t and log(1/ε) (§9.4, pp. 88-90 [76-78]) → hamiltonian-simulation
- The same simulation + phase estimation + amplitude amplification toolbox underlies the HHL linear-system algorithm (eigenvalue inversion via e^{iA}), and QSVT yields improved HHL versions; the notes also give an exercise on computing ground-state energy via phase estimation with a good-overlap initial state (§10.2, p. 96 [84]; Ex. 9.13, p. 94 [82]) → hamiltonian-simulation
- Exercise insight: for H' = 100H the same unitary is obtained 100 times faster in physical time, which is why the Hamiltonian norm is normalised (‖H‖ ≤ 1) when talking about cost (Ex. 9.2, p. 91 [79]) → hamiltonian-simulation

### Quantum complexity
- Classical classes: P (deterministic polynomial time), BPP (randomized, error ≤ 1/3), NP (verifiable in polynomial time given a witness; NP-complete problems are hardest in NP, e.g. SAT, integer linear programming, TSP, graph colorability), PSPACE (polynomial space) (§13.2, pp. 122-123 [110-111]) → quantum-complexity
- BQP is the class solvable by quantum computers in polynomial time with error ≤ 1/3 on every input, the accepted formalization of "efficiently solvable by quantum computers"; formally a uniform family of polynomial-size circuits {C_n} (footnote 1, §13.2, p. 123 [111]; §2.1.2, pp. 27-28 [15-16]) → quantum-complexity
- EQP (exact quantum polynomial time) depends on the gate set and is deemed not so interesting; the quantum-witness analogue of NP with exact acceptance is likewise gate-set dependent; QMA (with error ≤ 1/3) is the robust quantum analogue of NP (§13.2, pp. 122-123 [110-111]) → quantum-complexity
- Error can be reduced to any constant ε by running O(log(1/ε)) times and taking the majority (§13.2, p. 123 [111]; App. B.2) → quantum-complexity
- BPP ⊆ BQP: a BPP machine on fixed input length is a polynomial-size reversible (Toffoli) circuit with coin flips; the quantum computer generates the coins with Hadamards (§13.2, p. 123 [111]) → quantum-complexity
- BQP ⊆ PSPACE (Bernstein-Vazirani): with only Hadamard and Toffoli gates, each amplitude ⟨i_T|ψ_x⟩ is a sum over all intermediate basis states of products of single matrix entries (a "path integral"-like sum), computable in polynomial space (but exponential time) by reusing space; the acceptance probability is then the sum of squares over outputs with first bit 1 (§13.3, pp. 124-125 [112-113]) → quantum-complexity
- Resulting chain: P ⊆ BPP ⊆ BQP ⊆ PSPACE; it is generally believed that P = BPP, while the other inclusions are believed strict; QPSPACE = PSPACE (§13.2, p. 123 [111]) → quantum-complexity
- BQP is believed to contain problems outside BPP, e.g. factoring (decision version in BQP by Shor, believed not in BPP) (§13.2, p. 123 [111]) → quantum-complexity
- Proving BQP ≠ BPP (e.g. showing factoring is not in BPP) would imply P ≠ PSPACE, a main open problem since the 1960s, so such a proof would be very hard (§13.2, pp. 123-124 [111-112]) → quantum-complexity
- NP vs BQP: NP-complete problems are generally believed not to be in BQP; the main evidence is the Grover lower bound (brute-force search gives only a square-root speed-up); this is not a proof, as clever structure-exploiting methods might exist, though none is known classically or quantumly for general SAT (§13.2, p. 124 [112]) → quantum-complexity
- BQP and NP may be incomparable, since there could be problems in BQP outside NP (§13.2, p. 124 [112]) → quantum-complexity
- Exercise result: BQP ⊆ PP (Ex. 13.4, p. 126 [114], with hint) → quantum-complexity
- QMA: NP ⊆ MA ⊆ QCMA ⊆ QMA; MA = NP is believed (like BPP = P) but QMA is believed strictly larger than NP; the 2-local Hamiltonian ground-state-energy problem is QMA-complete (§14.1, p. 128 [116]; §13.2, p. 123 [111]) → quantum-complexity
- Most functions are hard: almost all Boolean functions on n bits need circuit complexity nearly 2^n even for quantum circuits (§13.1, p. 121 [109]) → quantum-complexity
- Oracle (black-box) separations: the notes do not use the phrase "oracle separation relative to an oracle" for BQP vs BPP/NP; the query-model results (Deutsch-Jozsa, Simon, Grover) are separations of query complexity, which the notes explicitly say do not by themselves separate the circuit-model classes (§2.4, p. 29 [17]; §3.3.2, p. 37 [25]) → quantum-complexity
- Also stated: the quantum search lower bound implies (for exact algorithms) no better than N/2 queries for OR; general limit that quantum algorithms offer at most a power-6 speed-up for total functions (§11.2, p. 104 [92]) → quantum-complexity
- ⚠️ Caveat in the notes on the "oracle separation" claim: the exponential separation of Simon vs. bounded-error randomized algorithms is proven for query counts, not for the circuit-model classes BQP vs BPP (§3.3.2, p. 37 [25]) → quantum-complexity

### Post-quantum cryptography
- Most classical public-key cryptography in use today could be broken by a large quantum computer: RSA (hardness of factoring) is broken by Shor's factoring algorithm and Diffie-Hellman key exchange (hardness of discrete log) is broken by Shor's discrete-log algorithm; the notes call the consequences potentially huge for payments, signatures, and secure messaging (§18.1, p. 167 [155]) → post-quantum-cryptography
- The security of Diffie-Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography rests on the assumed classical hardness of discrete log; a discrete-log solver breaks the shared key (§6.1.2, p. 58 [46]; Ex. 6.3, pp. 63-64 [51-52]) → post-quantum-cryptography
- RSA recap (exercise): N = pq with p, q large primes (e.g. N 2048 bits), public key (e, N), secret d with de = 1 mod φ(N), encryption m^e mod N, decryption c^d mod N; anyone who can factor N can decrypt (Ex. 5.3, pp. 54-55 [42-43]) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Diffie-Hellman recap (exercise): public prime p and generator γ; Alice sends γ^a, Bob sends γ^b, shared key K = γ^{ab}; an adversary who computes discrete logs obtains K (Ex. 6.3, pp. 63-64 [51-52]) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Two defences: (1) post-quantum cryptography (PQC), classical cryptosystems not based on factoring or discrete log, relying on assumed hardness even for quantum computers of other problems, e.g. finding short vectors in integer lattices such as learning with errors (LWE); the notes call PQC "slightly confusingly" part of classical cryptography and do not cover it further (§18.1, p. 167 [155]) → post-quantum-cryptography
- (2) quantum cryptography: systems that use quantum effects, with the drawback that even honest users need a (simple) quantum computer, and the advantage that security can be information-theoretic rather than based on unproven hardness (§18.1, p. 167 [155]) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Public-key schemes like RSA are only computationally secure (the private key is in principle computable from the public key); the one-time pad C = M⊕K is information-theoretically secure, and the problem is distributing the key K (§18.2, pp. 167-168 [155-156]) → post-quantum-cryptography
- BB84 quantum key distribution: Alice sends random bits in random bases (computational or Hadamard), Bob measures in random bases, they publicly compare bases (roughly n/2 matches form the shared string), test a random subset of n/4 for errors (eavesdropping disturbs the state), then do information reconciliation and privacy amplification; communication is linear in the key length; it needs an authenticated classical channel and took over 12 years to be proven secure (§18.2, pp. 168-169 [156-157], footnote 1) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Information-disturbance tradeoff: for individual-qubit attacks Eve's best measurement identifies the bit with probability cos²(π/8) ≈ 0.85 but induces an error with probability at least sin²(π/8) ≈ 0.15 when Bob measures in the matching basis (§18.2, p. 169 [157]) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Perfect bit commitment (and hence coin flipping) is impossible in quantum cryptography: a perfectly concealing protocol cannot be binding (§18.4, pp. 171-172 [159-160]) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Quantum key distribution is much easier to realize than general quantum computing, is commercially available, but implementations have exploited loopholes (§18.5, p. 173 [161]) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Shor-scale resources: slightly more than 4096 perfect qubits for a 2048-bit integer in the version described (footnote 2, §5.3, p. 51 [39]) → post-quantum-cryptography
- ⚠️ Grover's effect on symmetric-key cryptography (key-length halving) and the details of lattice-based schemes are not discussed in the chapters read; only the Grover speed-up for NP search (square root) is given (§7.4, p. 70 [58]; §18.1, p. 167 [155]) → post-quantum-cryptography

## error-correction

Accessed (this section): full extracted text of arXiv:1907.09415v6, Ch. 21 "Error-Correction and Fault-Tolerance" (PDF pp. 195-201 [183-189], exercises pp. 201-204 [189-192]) and, for stabilizers, Ch. 20 §§20.1-20.4 (PDF pp. 185-189 [173-177]). Same "p. N [M]" convention (PDF page [printed page]). Ch. 21 is explicitly a "succinct and somewhat sketchy" introduction (§21.1, p. 195 [183]); it does not cover the surface code.

### Quantum noise (errors and their discretization)
- Quantum error-correction is harder than classical for three reasons: states cannot be copied (no-cloning), the quantum world has a continuum of possible errors rather than just bit flips, and checking a state by measuring can collapse it (§21.3, p. 196 [184]) → quantum-noise-channels
- Error model used: S qubits and T time-steps, gates in parallel on disjoint qubits; after each time-step each qubit independently suffers some unitary error with probability p; gates themselves are taken as perfect (an imperfect gate = perfect gate followed by such an error, which is not fully general) (§21.3, p. 196 [184]) → quantum-noise-channels
- The Pauli matrices I, X, Y, Z are read as errors: I no error, X bit flip, Z phase flip, Y = iXZ a phase flip plus bit flip (up to global phase) (§21.3, pp. 196-197 [184-185]) → quantum-noise-channels
- The four Paulis span all 2x2 matrices, so any single-qubit error is E = a0 I + a1 X + a2 Y + a3 Z; for a unitary error the squared coefficient moduli sum to 1 (Ex. 1). Example: a small phase error diag(1, e^{iφ}) is mostly I with a small Z component (§21.3, p. 197 [185]; Ex. 1, p. 201 [189]) → quantum-noise-channels
- Discretization of errors: by linearity, a code that corrects X, Z (and so Y) errors on a qubit corrects every possible single-qubit unitary error; the same holds for joint errors on k qubits, which are linear combinations of tensor products of k Paulis (§21.3, p. 197 [185]) → quantum-noise-channels
- Syndrome measurement itself "discretizes" the continuum: measuring the syndrome of E|codeword> collapses to one specific Pauli error, which is then undone; syndrome 0 means the measurement already removed the error (§21.4, p. 199 [187]) → quantum-noise-channels
- Non-unitary errors from interaction with the environment can also be corrected, but the notes do not treat them (footnote 2, §21.3, p. 197 [185]) → quantum-noise-channels
- Depolarizing noise (exercise): with probability 1-p nothing happens, with probability p the qubit is replaced by the maximally mixed state I/2; on a density matrix written in the Pauli basis it keeps the I coefficient (which is 1/2) and shrinks each of the X, Y, Z coefficients by a factor 1-p (Ex. 7, p. 202 [190]) → quantum-noise-channels

### Repetition codes (classical 3-bit code and its quantum analogue)
- Classical 3-bit repetition code b -> bbb, decoded by majority vote (§21.2, p. 195 [183]) → repetition-codes
- With independent bit flips of probability p, the encoded bit fails when 2 or 3 bits flip, which has probability 3p²(1-p) + p³ (< 3p²); so the error rate drops when p < 1/3 (and, with more work, when p < 1/2, Ex. 9) (§21.2, pp. 195-196 [183-184], footnote 1) → repetition-codes
- Concatenating the 3-bit code k times (3^k bits per logical bit) gives error roughly (1/3)(3p)^(2^k): exponentially more bits but double-exponentially smaller error (§21.2, p. 196 [184]) → repetition-codes
- Quantum repetition is not available in the naive form because of no-cloning (§21.3, p. 196 [184]). ⚠️ The notes do not present a stand-alone 3-qubit bit-flip or phase-flip code. They only show the repetition idea inside the 9-qubit code, where within each 3-qubit block |000> ± |111> the minority qubit locates a bit flip (§21.4, p. 198 [186]) → repetition-codes
- Syndrome extraction without measuring the data: a unitary U writes the error location into fresh auxiliary |0> qubits; only these auxiliary qubits are measured, and they are discarded after each round (§21.4, pp. 198-199 [186-187]) → repetition-codes
- Ex. 4 gives a 4-qubit code that only detects a bit flip and/or phase flip (the result is either the original state or a known "an error occurred" flag), contrasting detection with correction (Ex. 4, p. 201 [189]) → repetition-codes

### Shor's 9-qubit code
- Shor's code encodes 1 logical qubit into 9 physical qubits and corrects an error on any one of them; |0_L> = (|000>+|111>)^(⊗3)/√8 and |1_L> = (|000>-|111>)^(⊗3)/√8; the codespace is the 2-dimensional subspace they span inside the 2^9-dimensional space (§21.4, p. 197 [185]) → shor-code
- Bit flips: within each 3-qubit block the minority position gives the flip location; a unitary writes a number e_b in {0,...,9} (0 = no flip) into 4 auxiliary qubits (§21.4, p. 198 [186]) → shor-code
- Phase flips: compare the relative sign of the three blocks; a number e_p in {0,1,2,3} (the block where the sign differs) goes into 2 auxiliary qubits. The exact qubit is not located, but it is not needed, since applying Z to any qubit of that block fixes it (§21.4, p. 198 [186]) → shor-code
- Combined unitary U acts on 9+4+2 = 15 qubits: X_i Z_j|0_L>|0^4>|0^2> -> X_i Z_j|0_L>|i>|j'> with j' the block of qubit j. Measure the 6 auxiliary qubits, then apply X to qubit e_b and Z to a qubit in block e_p; a Y error is the case i = j and is handled (§21.4, p. 198 [186]) → shor-code
- Hence any single Pauli error on the 9 qubits is corrected, and by the discretization argument so is any error acting on one qubit, e.g. E = (a0 I + Σ a_i X_i)(b0 I + Σ b_j Z_j) expands into a superposition of syndromes, and measuring the syndrome picks one X_i Z_j (§21.4, pp. 198-199 [186-187]) → shor-code
- The notes do not name degeneracy: the phase-flip syndrome is deliberately coarse (any of the 3 qubits in a block gets the same Z correction), which is the closest they come to it. ⚠️ No explicit statement about degenerate codes (§21.4, p. 198 [186]) → shor-code
- Cost caveat: encoding raises the number of places an error can occur (1 -> 9 qubits, 15 with auxiliaries, plus time-steps for syndrome and correction), so the code only helps when p is small enough that 2 or more errors are less likely than 1 error on the unencoded qubit (§21.4, p. 199 [187]) → shor-code
- Each correction round needs fresh auxiliary qubits; error correction is compared to a refrigerator that dumps noise into discarded auxiliary qubits (§21.4, p. 199 [187]) → shor-code
- Better codes exist: the shortest code for 1 logical qubit protecting against 1 error has 5 physical qubits; "asymptotically good" codes encode k logical qubits into O(k) physical qubits and correct errors on a constant fraction of qubits (§21.4, p. 199 [187]) → shor-code
- Exercises: build the encoding circuit of the 9-qubit code (Ex. 3); show no code can protect one logical qubit against errors on up to k qubits using only 2k physical qubits (Ex. 6); counting bound 2^k Σ_{i≤w} C(n,i) 3^i <= 2^n for codes correcting weight-w Pauli errors (Ex. 8) (pp. 201-203 [189-191]) → shor-code

### Stabilizer formalism
- Pauli group on n qubits: P_n = {±1, ±i} x {I,X,Y,Z}^(⊗n), of size 4^(n+1); the phases make it a group under multiplication. Non-identity single-qubit Paulis anticommute and have eigenvalues ±1 (§20.1, p. 185 [173]) → stabilizer-formalism
- A state |ψ> is stabilized by P in P_n (not ±I) if P|ψ> = |ψ>; each independent stabilizer halves the dimension of the subspace of states it fixes (2^n -> 2^(n-1) -> ...); n independent stabilizers pin down a single state up to global phase, called a stabilizer state (§20.1, p. 185 [173]) → stabilizer-formalism
- Examples: |0> is stabilized by Z, |1> by -Z, |+> by X, |−> by -X; |01> by Z⊗I and -I⊗Z; every basis state is a stabilizer state, and entangled states such as EPR pairs are too (§20.1, p. 185 [173]) → stabilizer-formalism
- The stabilizers must pairwise commute (anticommuting matrices cannot share a +1 eigenstate) and -I must not be in the generated group; any Pauli commuting with all n of them is a product of them up to phase (§20.1, pp. 185-186 [173-174]) → stabilizer-formalism
- Clifford circuits (Paulis, H, S, CNOT) are exactly the unitaries that map Paulis to Paulis under conjugation (e.g. HXH = Z; Figures 20.1-20.2 give the action of H and CNOT); hence they map stabilizer states to stabilizer states, with the new stabilizers U P U* (e.g. X⊗X, Z⊗Z under H⊗I become Z⊗X, X⊗Z) (§20.2, p. 186 [174]) → stabilizer-formalism
- Pauli measurement of M: if M commutes with all stabilizers, the outcome is deterministic and the stabilizers do not change; if it anticommutes with one, the outcome is a fair coin and that stabilizer is replaced by ±M (§20.3, pp. 187-188 [175-176]) → stabilizer-formalism
- Gottesman-Knill theorem: measurement-controlled Clifford circuits on a basis state can be simulated classically in polynomial time by tracking the n stabilizers (about n(2n+2) = O(n²) bits) instead of 2^n amplitudes (§20.3, p. 187 [175]) → stabilizer-formalism
- The chapter says stabilizer states appear in circuit design and quantum error-correction, but Ch. 21 does not use the stabilizer language: the Steane code is introduced via classical binary codes (Ex. 5: the span C of 0001111, 0110011, 1010101 and its complement set C'; |0_L> and |1_L> are uniform superpositions over C and C'; logical X = X on all 7 qubits) (§20.1, p. 185 [173]; Ex. 5, p. 202 [190]) → stabilizer-formalism
- ⚠️ Not in the source: the [[n,k,d]] notation, syndromes as commutation patterns with stabilizer generators, logical operators in general, CSS construction by name (§§20-21) → stabilizer-formalism

### Threshold theorem
- Motivation: after Shor (1994) physicists doubted a quantum computer was feasible; error-correction and fault-tolerance theory shows that if the error rate per operation is reasonably small (say 1%) and errors on different qubits are not very correlated, near-perfect computation for arbitrarily long is possible (§21.1, p. 195 [183]) → threshold-theorem
- Concatenation: a code encoding 1 qubit into C qubits, correcting one error, with D time-steps per correction round has CD error locations; failure probability p' ≈ (CD)²p², so if p is below roughly 1/(CD)² then p' < p and each level reduces the error (§21.6, p. 200 [188]) → threshold-theorem
- Concatenating k times uses C^k qubits per logical qubit and gives error O((CDp)^(2^k)); to survive T = poly(n) steps it suffices to take k = O(log log T) levels, a polylogarithmic overhead in qubits and time (2^(O(log log T)) = (log T)^O(1)); the overhead can even be made O(1) by a result cited as [115] (§21.6, p. 200 [188] and footnote 4) → threshold-theorem
- Threshold theorem (Aharonov-Ben-Or, Knill et al., cited as [8, 168]): if the hardware error rate is below some constant, error-correction plus fault-tolerant computing allows long computations without serious errors. The "threshold" is not one number; it depends on noise model, allowed gate set and code (§21.6, pp. 200-201 [188-189]) → threshold-theorem
- Threshold values (as stated): best rigorous lower bounds about 0.1% under reasonable noise assumptions; numerical evidence that a few percent might be tolerable; larger upper bounds known; more efficient codes (smaller C and D) give a higher threshold (§21.6, p. 201 [189]) → threshold-theorem
- Assumptions: independent noise per physical qubit, operations done "in a scalable way". With devious correlated noise protection is much harder, though often still possible (footnote 5, p. 201 [189]; §21.3, p. 196 [184]). Classical processing for syndromes/corrections is implicitly assumed (§21.4, p. 198 [186]) → threshold-theorem
- Significance: the existence of a constant threshold is called one of the most important results in the field and the main answer to skeptics; experiments below threshold are said to be "starting to happen" (cited as [128]), with no known fundamental obstacle but an extremely hard engineering problem (§21.6, p. 201 [189]) → threshold-theorem
- Error-correction steps can themselves introduce more errors than they fix (leaky-boat analogy), which is why the fault-tolerant design below is needed (§21.5, p. 199 [187]) → threshold-theorem

### Fault-tolerant gates
- Operations on encoded qubits must be done without decoding: decoding, acting and re-encoding leaves an unprotected window (§21.5, p. 199 [187]) → fault-tolerant-gates
- Steane's 7-qubit code: logical Hadamard is H⊗7 and logical CNOT is CNOTs between corresponding qubits of the two 7-qubit blocks; such implementations are called transversal (§21.5, p. 200 [188]) → fault-tolerant-gates
- Errors must not spread: for a code correcting one error per block, a transversal CNOT turns two blocks with one error in total into at most one error per block, which is still correctable, whereas one block with two errors would not be (§21.5, p. 200 [188]) → fault-tolerant-gates
- H and CNOT plus the T gate (|b> -> e^{ibπ/4}|b>) give universality, but T is not easily done fault-tolerantly and the notes defer to magic states (Ex. 20.6) (§21.5, p. 200 [188]) → fault-tolerant-gates
- Fault-tolerant state preparation and logical computational-basis measurement are also needed; further details are skipped (§21.5, p. 200 [188]) → fault-tolerant-gates
- Clifford gates (H, S, CNOT) are not universal and, on stabilizer inputs, classically simulable (Gottesman-Knill); adding the single non-Clifford T gate = diag(1, e^{iπ/4}) gives universality ("Clifford+T"); T is non-Clifford since T X T* is not a Pauli (§20.4, p. 188 [176]) → fault-tolerant-gates
- In the error-correction setting Clifford gates are usually easy and non-Clifford gates like T are often hard (§20.4, pp. 188-189 [176-177]) → fault-tolerant-gates
- Magic states: a T gate can be replaced by a measurement-controlled Clifford circuit consuming one copy of |T> = T|+> = (|0>+e^{iπ/4}|1>)/√2 (Ex. 6). The cost of T gates moves to producing many |T> copies, up front or by a "magic-state factory" (§20.4, p. 189 [177]) → fault-tolerant-gates
- Simulation cost grows with the number t of T gates: stabilizer rank of |T>^(⊗t) has upper bound 3^(t/4) ≈ 2^(0.3963 t), and approximate rank around 2^(0.228 t), so circuits with O(log n) T gates are classically simulable (§20.5, p. 190 [178]). This supports the idea that T-count measures how non-classical a circuit is → fault-tolerant-gates
- ⚠️ Not in the source: the Eastin-Knill theorem, lattice surgery/braiding, magic-state distillation by name, or an explicit statement that T-count dominates fault-tolerant cost (§§20-21) → fault-tolerant-gates
