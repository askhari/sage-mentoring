## Shor algorithm: problem, reduction, modular exponentiation

- The paper gives randomized quantum algorithms for integer factoring and discrete logarithms, taking a number of steps polynomial in the input size (number of digits). Both problems are described as generally thought hard classically (Abstract, p. 1) → shor-algorithm
- Rather than factoring n directly, the paper builds a quantum algorithm for the order of x in the multiplicative group mod n (the least r with x^r ≡ 1 mod n), and uses the known randomized reduction from factoring to order finding, attributed to Miller 1976 (§5, p. 15) → shor-algorithm
- Reduction: for odd n, pick a random x mod n, find its order r, compute gcd(x^r/2 − 1, n). It works because (x^r/2 − 1)(x^r/2 + 1) ≡ 0 mod n. The gcd is a trivial divisor only if r is odd or x^r/2 ≡ −1 mod n (§5, pp. 15-16) → shor-algorithm
- A random x yields a factor with probability at least 1 − 1/2^(k−1), where k is the number of distinct odd prime factors of n. The sketch of proof uses the Chinese remainder theorem and the cyclicity of the group mod an odd prime power. The scheme needs n odd and not a prime power; prime powers are factored efficiently by classical methods (§5, p. 16) → shor-algorithm
- Modular exponentiation (x^a mod n) is the bottleneck of the algorithm, the part that uses the most time and space (§3, p. 10) → shor-algorithm
- Quantum computation is reversible, so a classical computation has to be made reversible (Toffoli/Fredkin gates, Bennett's method of keeping a record and then uncomputing). This costs only a constant factor in time but as much space as time (§3, pp. 8-10) → shor-algorithm
- Modular exponentiation is done by repeated squaring (the powers x^(2^i) mod n are precomputed classically and built into the gate array). Controlled multiplications are done by repeated modular addition. The input a is kept unchanged, so (a, 1) goes to (a, x^a mod n) (§3, pp. 10-11) → shor-algorithm
- Multiplication by c is reversible only if gcd(c, n) = 1. The multiplicand b is erased using the modular inverse c^−1 (§3, pp. 11-12) → shor-algorithm
- Cost of modular exponentiation: with Schönhage–Strassen multiplication, O(l² log l log log l) time and O(l log l log log l) space for l-bit numbers. With schoolbook multiplication, O(l³) time and O(l) space (§3, p. 10) → shor-algorithm
- Remark: measuring b after the erase step should give 0. Such partial measurements might stabilize the computation (the quantum Zeno effect). The author says the cost-benefit has not been analyzed (§3, p. 12) → shor-algorithm

## Shor algorithm: the quantum steps of order finding

- Choose q, a power of 2, with n² ≤ q < 2n². Put the first register in the uniform superposition over a mod q, using a Hadamard-like superposition on each bit. The state is (1/√q) Σ|a⟩|0⟩ (§5, p. 16, eq. 5.1) → shor-algorithm
- Compute x^a mod n in the second register, giving (1/√q) Σ|a⟩|x^a mod n⟩ (§5, p. 16, eq. 5.2) → shor-algorithm
- Apply the Fourier transform A_q to the first register. Then observe the machine (observing only the first register would suffice) (§5, pp. 16-17, eqs. 5.3-5.4) → shor-algorithm
- Probability of observing the state |c, x^k mod n⟩: the sum is over all a ≡ k mod r, i.e. a = br + k. It can be rewritten in terms of the residue {rc}_q, the representative of rc mod q in (−q/2, q/2] (§5, p. 17, eqs. 5.5-5.7) → shor-algorithm
- If |{rc}_q| ≤ r/2, the amplitudes add nearly in phase. Each such state is observed with probability at least about 4/(π²r²), and so at least 1/(3r²) for large n (§5, pp. 17-18) → shor-algorithm
- The condition |{rc}_q| ≤ r/2 is equivalent to |c/q − d/r| ≤ 1/(2q) for some d. Since q > n², at most one fraction d/r with r < n satisfies it. So d/r is obtained in lowest terms by a continued-fraction expansion of c/q, which is polynomial time (§5, pp. 18-19, eqs. 5.11-5.13) → shor-algorithm
- Fig. 5.1 shows, for q = 256 and r = 10 (as when factoring 33 with x = 5), that the measured c is with high probability near an integer multiple of q/r (§5, pp. 18-19) → shor-algorithm
- If d and r are coprime, d/r in lowest terms gives r. There are r values of x^k and φ(r) good values of d, so r is found with probability at least φ(r)/(3r). Since φ(r)/r > δ/log log r, repeating O(log log r) times gives a high probability of success (§5, p. 19) → shor-algorithm
- Practical improvements. (1) Try values near c, such as c ± 1. (2) Try small multiples of the candidate r′. Using multiples up to (log n)^(1+ε) cuts the expected number of trials for the hardest n from O(log log n) to O(1) (Odlyzko, personal communication). (3) Take the lcm of two candidate orders r1 and r2 (Knill, personal communication) (§5, p. 19) → shor-algorithm
- The order-finding algorithm uses few properties of multiplication mod n. It works for any permutation f whose k-th iterate is computable in time polynomial in log n and log k (§5, p. 19) → shor-algorithm
- Running time: O((log n)² (log log n)(log log log n)) steps on the quantum computer, plus polynomial classical post-processing that converts the output into factors. The paper says this post-processing could in principle be done on a quantum computer, but a classical one is fine (§5, p. 15) → shor-algorithm
- Speculation: since Schönhage–Strassen multiplication uses the FFT, a quantum speed-up of integer multiplication might make breaking RSA asymptotically faster than encrypting with RSA on a classical computer (§3, p. 13) → shor-algorithm

## Shor algorithm: discrete logarithm

- Definition: for a prime p and generator g, the discrete logarithm of x is the r with 0 ≤ r < p−1 and g^r ≡ x mod p (§6, pp. 19-20) → shor-algorithm
- The quantum algorithm uses three registers, two modular exponentiations and two quantum Fourier transforms. Take q a power of 2 with p < q < 2p. Put the first two registers in the uniform superposition over a, b mod p−1. Compute g^a x^−b mod p in the third register. Apply A_q to both of the first two registers and observe |c, d, y⟩ (§6, p. 20, eqs. 6.1-6.3) → shor-algorithm
- Outputs are classified as "good" by two conditions (6.10, 6.11). Each good state has probability at least about 1/(20q²). A good state is observed with probability at least about 1/480 per run (§6, pp. 21-22) → shor-algorithm
- r is recovered by rounding d/q to a multiple of 1/(p−1) and dividing mod p−1 by c′. The difficulty is that c′ may share factors with p−1. So the proof gathers t good c′ values for primes above 18 and tries all residues for small primes (under 18). The Chinese remainder theorem then yields r (§6, pp. 22-23) → shor-algorithm
- The discrete-log algorithm generalizes to other fields with a cyclic multiplicative group, e.g. Z_(p^α). Boneh and Lipton generalized it to abelian, non-cyclic groups (§6, pp. 23-24) → shor-algorithm
- The paper thanks J. Lagarias for finding and fixing a critical error in the first version of the discrete-log algorithm (Acknowledgements, p. 25) → shor-algorithm

## Quantum Fourier transform

- Definition: A_q takes |a⟩, 0 ≤ a < q, to (1/√q) Σ_c exp(2πi·ac/q)|c⟩. It is the unitary matrix with (a, c) entry exp(2πiac/q)/√q. The paper calls it "at the heart" of the algorithms (§4, p. 13, eq. 4.1) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Since q is exponentially large, a polynomial-time construction is needed. The paper gives one for q = 2^l, found independently by Coppersmith (1994) and Deutsch (via Ekert-Jozsa 1995). It is essentially the classical FFT adapted to a quantum computer (§4, p. 13) → quantum-fourier-transform
- The earlier (1994) version of the paper handled only smooth q with small prime-power factors. Cleve showed A_q can be built for all smooth q whose prime factors are at most O(log n) (§4, p. 13) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Only two gate types are needed. R_j is a one-qubit Hadamard-like gate with entries ±1/√2. S_{j,k} is a two-qubit diagonal gate that multiplies the |11⟩ amplitude by e^(iθ_{k−j}) with θ_{k−j} = π/2^(k−j) (§4, pp. 13-14, eqs. 4.2-4.3) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Order of the gates: R_{l−1}, S_{l−2,l−1}, R_{l−2}, S_{l−3,l−1}, S_{l−3,l−2}, R_{l−3}, ..., R_0. That is, R_j in decreasing j, with all S_{j,k} with k > j applied between R_{j+1} and R_j. The total is l(l−1)/2 quantum gates (as stated in the paper) (§4, p. 14, eq. 4.4) → quantum-fourier-transform
- The circuit produces the output with its bits in reversed order (b is the bit-reversal of c). The bits are either reversed with further computation or read in reverse order (§4, p. 14) → quantum-fourier-transform
- Proof of correctness: the 1/√2 factors multiply to 1/√q. The S gates only change phases. The phase on the path |a⟩ → |b⟩ is Σ π a_j b_j + Σ_{j<k} π a_j b_k/2^(k−j). After rewriting with c as the bit-reversal of b and dropping multiples of 2π, this equals 2πac/q (§4, pp. 14-15, eqs. 4.5-4.9) → quantum-fourier-transform
- When k − j is large, S_{j,k} applies a tiny phase, which is physically hard to do accurately. Coppersmith's approximate Fourier transform ignores these tiny phases and is still accurate enough for factoring. It drops most S_{j,k} gates and so greatly reduces the gate count (§4, p. 15) → quantum-fourier-transform
- In the factoring algorithm A_q is applied to the first register after modular exponentiation. The measurement outcome c/q then approximates d/r (§5, pp. 16-19) → quantum-fourier-transform

## Complexity and the Church-Turing thesis

- Quantitative Church's thesis (the "Strong Church's Thesis"): any physical computing device can be simulated by a Turing machine with at most polynomial overhead in the resources used. The paper says it escapes through the words "physical" and "resources". It suggests that quantum mechanics may break the thesis (Abstract p. 1; §1, p. 2) → quantum-complexity
- Church's thesis is not a theorem, because it would require a precise mathematical definition of "computing device" (§1, p. 2) → quantum-complexity
- P is the class of problems solvable in polynomial time. Efficiency is distinguished as polynomial versus superpolynomial scaling (§1, p. 2) → quantum-complexity
- A third resource for quantum computers is precision. With constant precision, nothing outside classical randomized polynomial time is known to be computable. With precision growing polynomially (logarithmically many bits), the quantum computer appears to be more powerful (§1, p. 3) → quantum-complexity
- Early history: Benioff showed that unitary evolution can realize a Turing machine. Feynman suggested that quantum systems may be expensive to simulate classically. Deutsch defined quantum Turing machines and quantum circuits. Deutsch-Jozsa and Berthiaume-Brassard gave problems solved exactly and quickly by a quantum computer that classical computers can only solve with randomness. Bernstein-Vazirani gave an oracle problem needing superpolynomial classical time. Simon's oracle problem takes exponential classical time. Simon's algorithm inspired the paper (§1, p. 4) → quantum-complexity
- BPP is the class solvable in polynomial time with a random number generator and a small error probability. It is called the widely viewed class of efficiently solvable problems (§1, p. 4) → quantum-complexity
- Quantum Turing machines and quantum gate arrays compute the same functions in polynomial time with small error probability (Yao 1993). The resulting class is robust and is named BQP by analogy with BPP (§2, p. 5) → quantum-complexity
- Uniformity of gate arrays: the circuit design must be produced by a polynomial-time classical computation. The entries of the unitary matrices must also be computable (the first log n bits computable in polynomial time, per Solovay), so non-computable information cannot be hidden in the gates (§2, p. 8) → quantum-complexity
- A universal gate set for quantum circuits is all one-bit gates plus the controlled-NOT. General n-bit unitaries often need exponentially many two-bit gates (§2, p. 6) → quantum-complexity
- Number field sieve: the best classical factoring algorithm known, at about exp(c (log n)^(1/3) (log log n)^(2/3)). Since the input is log n bits, this is exponential time. Compared with it, the quantum algorithm uses O((log n)² log log n log log log n) steps (§5, p. 15) → quantum-complexity
- Classical discrete log mod arbitrary primes: the fastest known is Gordon's adaptation of the number field sieve, with time exp(O((log p)^(1/3) (log log p)^(2/3))) (§6, p. 20) → quantum-complexity
- Any general method of simulating quantum mechanics with polynomial slowdown would give a classical polynomial-time factoring algorithm (§1, p. 5) → quantum-complexity
- Open problems: quantum computers will likely not be widely useful unless they can solve NP-complete problems, though the author does not rule this out. He notes only "weak indications" (Bennett et al. 1994) that they cannot. Short lattice vector is suggested as a candidate problem for quantum speed-up (§7, pp. 24-25) → quantum-complexity
- Error tolerance (§7, p. 24): gates need precision only O(1/t) for t steps, and decoherence needs to be only polynomially small in t (Bennett et al. 1994). The no-cloning theorem rules out simple redundancy, but this does not rule out more complicated error-reduction schemes. Progress on error reduction (Berthiaume et al., Shor 1995) and faithful transmission over noisy quantum channels (Bennett et al. 1995) gives hope → quantum-complexity

## Cryptographic implications

- Factoring and discrete log are widely believed hard. Several cryptosystems are based on them, notably the RSA public-key cryptosystem. The paper shows these problems are polynomial-time solvable on a quantum computer with small error probability (§1, p. 4) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Factoring and discrete log are not in themselves widely useful problems. They matter because they are crucial to public-key cryptography, which presumes their difficulty. If these remain the only uses of quantum computation, quantum computing may become a special-purpose technique whose only raison d'être is to thwart public-key cryptosystems (§7, p. 24) → post-quantum-cryptography
- The Boneh-Lipton generalization of the algorithm to abelian groups is also tied to the same cryptographic usefulness (§7, p. 24) → post-quantum-cryptography
- Practicality: nobody knows how to build a quantum computer. The main obstacles are decoherence and precision, and both worsen with machine size. Small machines may be possible while scaling up to interesting computations may face fundamental difficulties (§1, p. 5; §7, p. 24) → post-quantum-cryptography
- The paper does not discuss quantum-resistant cryptosystems or key sizes. Only the threat to RSA and discrete-log systems is stated (⚠️ no post-quantum alternatives are covered by this source) → post-quantum-cryptography
