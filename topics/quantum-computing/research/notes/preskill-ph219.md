## quantum-mechanics

### State vector postulate
- Closed-system QM rests on five axioms (states, observables, measurement, dynamics, composite systems); the chapter recalls them before studying open systems (ch.2 §2.1, p. 3) → state-vector-postulate
- Axiom 1: a state is a ray in a Hilbert space, i.e. a complex vector space with an inner product that is complete in its norm; completeness only matters in infinite dimensions (ch.2 §2.1, pp. 3-4) → state-vector-postulate
- A ray is a class of vectors differing by a nonzero complex scalar; by convention one picks a unit-norm representative, so the overall phase has no physical meaning (|psi> and e^{i alpha}|psi> are the same state) (ch.2 §2.1, p. 4) → state-vector-postulate
- Any superposition a|phi>+b|psi> is also a state, and the RELATIVE phase between terms is physically significant, unlike the global phase (ch.2 §2.1, p. 4) → state-vector-postulate
- A qubit is a system with a two-dimensional Hilbert space; the general state is a|0>+b|1> with |a|^2+|b|^2=1 and global phase irrelevant (ch.2 §2.2, p. 8) → state-vector-postulate
- Composite systems: the Hilbert space is the tensor product H_A (x) H_B, and independently prepared parts give a product state (Axiom 5) (ch.2 §2.1, pp. 6-7) → state-vector-postulate
- Wigner's theorem: a symmetry preserves |<phi|psi>|, so it can be represented (with phase choices) by a unitary or antiunitary map; symmetry representations need only hold up to a phase because states are rays (ch.2 §2.2.1, p. 9) → state-vector-postulate
- Because states are rays, rotations of spin-1/2 are represented only up to sign (2 pi rotation gives -I), yet this is observable via relative phase: a controlled 2 pi rotation flips a superposed control spin to an orthogonal state (ch.2 §2.2.1, p. 12, eq. 2.34) → state-vector-postulate

### Born rule and measurement
- Axiom 3: measuring an observable A yields an eigenvalue a_n with probability ||E_n|psi>||^2 = <psi|E_n|psi> (E_n = projector onto that eigenspace), and the post-measurement state is the normalized E_n|psi> (ch.2 §2.1, p. 5, eqs. 2.7-2.8) → born-rule-measurement
- A repeated measurement immediately afterwards gives the same outcome with probability one (ch.2 §2.1, p. 6) → born-rule-measurement
- For a qubit measured in {|0>,|1>}, outcomes have probability |a|^2 and |b|^2; except when a or b is zero the measurement irrevocably disturbs the state, and a and b cannot be determined from a single measurement (ch.2 §2.2, p. 8) → born-rule-measurement
- Two ways a state changes: unitary evolution (deterministic) and measurement (probabilistic); the unexplained difference is called the measurement problem (ch.2 §2.1, p. 7) → born-rule-measurement
- Von Neumann model: measure observable M by coupling it to a pointer (H ~ lambda M (x) P); the pointer position becomes correlated with the eigenvalue, and reading it projects the system onto an eigenstate with probability |<a|phi>|^2. Stern-Gerlach is the example for sigma_3 (ch.3 §3.1.1, pp. 5-7) → born-rule-measurement
- Orthogonal measurement with projectors {E_a} can be implemented by a unitary that shifts a pointer by a when the system is in E_a; if the outcome is unrecorded the state becomes sum_a E_a rho E_a (ch.3 §3.1.1, pp. 7-8, eqs. 3.12-3.18) → born-rule-measurement
- Measuring the pointer in a different basis gives a generalized measurement with operators M_a satisfying sum M_a^dagger M_a = I; outcomes have probability ||M_a|psi>||^2, post-measurement states need not be orthogonal and the measurement is not repeatable; the operators E_a = M_a^dagger M_a form a POVM (ch.3 §3.1.2, pp. 8-10) → born-rule-measurement
- Any POVM can be realized as an orthogonal measurement on system plus ancilla; the POVM gives probabilities, p(a) = tr(rho E_a), but not the post-measurement state (ch.3 §3.1.2, pp. 10-11) → born-rule-measurement

### Observables
- Axiom 2: an observable is a self-adjoint operator (A = A^dagger, i.e. <phi|A|psi> = <psi|A|phi>^*) (ch.2 §2.1, p. 4) → observables
- Sum of self-adjoint operators is self-adjoint, but a product AB is self-adjoint only if A and B commute; AB+BA and i(AB-BA) always are (ch.2 §2.1, pp. 4-5) → observables
- Spectral representation: a self-adjoint operator has an orthonormal eigenbasis, A = sum_n a_n E_n = sum_n |n> a_n <n|, with E_n orthogonal projectors (E_n E_m = delta_nm E_n); the projector onto |psi> is |psi><psi| (ch.2 §2.1, p. 5, eqs. 2.4-2.6) → observables
- Expectation value of A in |psi> is sum_n a_n Prob(a_n) = <psi|A|psi> (ch.2 §2.1, p. 6, eq. 2.9) → observables
- An observable on subsystem A alone is M_A (x) I_B (ch.2 §2.3.1, p. 18) → observables
- For a qubit, <n.sigma> in a pure state gives the spin component along n; measuring only along z gives |a|,|b| but not the relative phase, so three axes are needed to determine the state from many copies (ch.2 §2.2.1, p. 13) → observables
- Measuring along axis n equals rotating n to z and then measuring along z (ch.2 §2.2.1, p. 13) → observables

### Unitary time evolution
- Axiom 4: closed-system evolution is a unitary operator, |psi(t')> = U(t',t)|psi(t)> (Schrodinger picture) (ch.2 §2.1, p. 6) → unitary-time-evolution
- Infinitesimal evolution obeys the Schrodinger equation d/dt|psi> = -iH|psi> with H a self-adjoint Hamiltonian (units of energy; hbar = 1); equivalently |psi(t+dt)> = (I - iH dt)|psi(t)>, which is unitary to first order, and finite evolution is a product of such steps (ch.2 §2.1, p. 6) → unitary-time-evolution
- For a time-independent H, U(t',t) = e^{-i(t'-t)H} (ch.2 §2.1, p. 6) → unitary-time-evolution
- The Schrodinger equation is linear, in contrast to typical classical dynamical equations; the text says this property "seems to beg for an explanation" (ch.2 §2.1, p. 7) → unitary-time-evolution
- A symmetry must commute with time evolution; infinitesimally this gives [Q,H]=0 for its generator Q, so symmetries imply conservation laws and vice versa (ch.2 §2.2.1, p. 10) → unitary-time-evolution
- A device that "turns on" a Hamiltonian whose eigenstates are circular (or linear) polarization states rotates (or phase-shifts) a photon polarization; the two together give any 2x2 unitary of determinant 1 (ch.2 §2.2.2, p. 16) → unitary-time-evolution
- For density operators, closed evolution reads rho(t+dt) = rho(t) - i dt [H, rho(t)] (ch.3 §3.5.2, p. 35) → unitary-time-evolution

### Two-level systems
- A qubit is the smallest nontrivial quantum system; unlike a probabilistic classical bit there is "more than one way to look at" it (measurement in different bases) (ch.2 §2.2, p. 8) → two-level-systems
- Spin-1/2 realisation: |0>,|1> are spin up/down along z; the two real parameters of the state (after normalisation and global phase) give the spin direction (polar theta, azimuth phi) (ch.2 §2.2.1, pp. 8-9) → two-level-systems
- The spin-1/2 rotation generators are J_k = sigma_k/2 (Pauli matrices, which anticommute and square to I); a rotation by theta about unit vector n is U = cos(theta/2) I - i sin(theta/2) n.sigma, and every 2x2 unitary of determinant 1 has this form, so any qubit gate is a spin rotation up to global phase (ch.2 §2.2.1, pp. 11-12) → two-level-systems
- The state pointing along (theta, phi) is (e^{-i phi/2} cos(theta/2), e^{i phi/2} sin(theta/2)), an eigenstate of n.sigma with eigenvalue 1 (ch.2 §2.2.1, p. 13, eqs. 2.39-2.40) → two-level-systems
- Interference example: |up_x> = (|up_z>+|down_z>)/sqrt2 and |down_x> are each 50/50 in z, but the superposition (|up_x>+|down_x>)/sqrt2 is |up_z> and always gives up in z, unlike a probabilistic classical bit (ch.2 §2.2.1, p. 14) → two-level-systems
- Formal equivalence with spin-1/2 holds for any two-level system, but not every two-level system transforms as a spinor under spatial rotations (ch.2 §2.2.1, p. 14) → two-level-systems
- Photon polarization is another qubit: massless, spin-1 (not a spinor); linear states |x>,|y> rotate by the real rotation matrix, with circular states |R>,|L> as eigenstates (ch.2 §2.2.2, pp. 14-15) → two-level-systems
- Polarizer demo: x then y analyzer passes nothing, but inserting a 45-degree analyzer between them lets about 1/8 of photons through; this rules out each photon carrying a classical polarization bit (ch.2 §2.2.2, p. 16) → two-level-systems
- ⚠️ Spin precession (a magnetic field driving rotation of the spin) is not discussed explicitly in these chapters; only the general statement that a Hamiltonian generates a rotation (see unitary-time-evolution bullets). → two-level-systems

### Density matrices
- When we observe only part of a larger system (open system), states are not rays, measurements are not orthogonal projections, and evolution is not unitary (ch.2 §2.3.1, p. 17) → density-matrices
- For |psi>_AB = a|00>+b|11>, expectation values of observables on A alone equal tr(M_A rho_A) with rho_A = |a|^2|0><0| + |b|^2|1><1|; rho_A is self-adjoint, positive, with unit trace (ch.2 §2.3.1, p. 18) → density-matrices
- General bipartite pure state: rho_A = tr_B(|psi><psi|), the partial trace; it is self-adjoint, positive (nonnegative eigenvalues), unit trace, hence diagonalisable with real eigenvalues summing to 1 (ch.2 §2.3.1, pp. 19-20, eqs. 2.62-2.67) → density-matrices
- Pure state: rho = |psi><psi| with rho^2 = rho. Mixed: rho = sum p_a |a><a| with two or more terms, tr rho^2 = sum p_a^2 < 1; this is an incoherent mixture, meaning relative phases are inaccessible (ch.2 §2.3.1, pp. 20-21) → density-matrices
- Even if the whole system is in a ray, a subsystem generally is not; entangling A with B makes A's phases inaccessible, so "collapse" appears as A being in one of several states with assigned probabilities (ch.2 §2.3.1, p. 21) → density-matrices
- Example: (|up_z>+|down_z>)/sqrt2 gives sigma_1 = +1 with certainty, while the ensemble of up_z/down_z each with 1/2 is rho = I/2 and gives 1/2 along every axis; the half of an equal-weight entangled pair behaves incoherently (ch.2 §2.3.1, pp. 18-19) → density-matrices
- Qubit: rho(P) = (I + P.sigma)/2; rho is valid iff |P| <= 1, so single-qubit density matrices fill a unit ball (the "Bloch sphere" is really a ball); the surface |P|=1 are pure states; <n.sigma> = n.P, and P can be measured from three independent axes (ch.2 §2.3.2, pp. 21-22) → density-matrices
- Density operators form a convex set; pure states are exactly its extremal points; for d>2 the boundary of the set contains non-pure states, unlike the qubit ball (ch.2 §2.5.1, pp. 26-28) → density-matrices
- A mixed state has infinitely many decompositions into pure-state ensembles (e.g. I/2 = equal mix of up_z/down_z = equal mix of up_x/down_x = any antipodal pair), yet they are observationally indistinguishable; a pure state has only one preparation. A classical distribution, in contrast, is a simplex with unique decomposition (ch.2 §2.5.2, pp. 28-30) → density-matrices
- Ambiguity does not allow faster-than-light signalling: Bob's choice of measuring B in different bases prepares different ensembles for Alice but the same rho_A, so she cannot tell them apart without his classical message (ch.2 §2.5.3, pp. 30-31) → density-matrices
- Schmidt decomposition: any bipartite pure state is sum_i sqrt(p_i)|i>_A|i'>_B, with rho_A, rho_B having the same nonzero eigenvalues; Schmidt number > 1 means entangled, in which case the reduced states are mixed (ch.2 §2.4, pp. 23-26) → density-matrices
- HJW theorem: any two ensemble decompositions of rho_A come from purifications related by a unitary on B alone, so measuring B in a suitable basis realises any chosen ensemble; this is the general "quantum eraser" (ch.2 §2.5.4-2.5.5, pp. 31-36) → density-matrices
- Layer 3: in the open-system formulation, a state is a density operator, a measurement a POVM (probability tr(E_a rho)) and dynamics a trace-preserving completely positive map; the closed axioms are recovered via purification and dilations ("church of the larger Hilbert space") (ch.3 §3.3.3, p. 23) → density-matrices

### Decoherence
- The motivation for open systems: all realistic systems are imperfectly isolated and exchange energy and information with an environment; treat S+E as closed and look only at S (ch.2 §2.1, p. 3) → decoherence
- Entanglement with an inaccessible system destroys interference because the which-state information is in principle available in B; it is "decoherence", but it can be undone partly if B's information is erased by measuring suitably and relaying results (quantum eraser) (ch.2 §2.5.4, pp. 31-33) → decoherence
- Unitary on A+B then tracing out B gives a quantum channel E(rho) = sum_a M_a rho M_a^dagger with sum M_a^dagger M_a = I (Kraus/operator-sum representation); equivalently the trace-preserving completely positive (TPCP) map; any channel arises this way from a joint unitary, and Kraus operators are unique up to a unitary mixing (ch.3 §3.2.1, pp. 11-13) → decoherence
- Unitary evolution is the one-Kraus-operator case; two or more Kraus operators can turn pure states into mixed ones, which is decoherence (ch.3 §3.2.1, p. 13) → decoherence
- Depolarizing channel: with prob 1-p nothing, with prob p one of sigma_1,sigma_2,sigma_3 applied (each p/3). The Bloch ball shrinks uniformly, P' = (1-4p/3)P; at p=3/4 every state maps to I/2. It cannot be inverted since expanding the ball is not positive (ch.3 §3.4.1, pp. 24-27) → decoherence
- Dephasing channel: rho -> (1-p/2) rho + (p/2) sigma_3 rho sigma_3; diagonals unchanged, off-diagonals multiplied by (1-p); with scattering rate Gamma, coherences decay as e^{-Gamma t} and the state decoheres in a preferred basis (pointer basis) while populations stay fixed; the Bloch ball squashes towards the z axis (ch.3 §3.4.2, pp. 27-29) → decoherence
- No-pancake theorem: no channel can shrink only one Bloch component (it would amount to a transpose, which is not completely positive) (ch.3 §3.4.2, p. 29) → decoherence
- Why macroscopic objects look classical: for a heavy object (dust grain), superpositions of distinguishable positions lose coherence at the scattering rate, far faster than its damping time; locality of interactions picks position as the preferred basis; the same applies to Schrodinger's cat. For microscopic systems decoherence need not be fast (ch.3 §3.4.2, pp. 29-30) → decoherence
- If the environment states scattered by the two branches overlap by 1-epsilon, the decoherence rate is epsilon times the scattering rate (ch.3 §3.4.2, p. 30) → decoherence
- Dephasing shows up as decaying visibility of oscillations: for an atom in (|0>+e^{-i omega t}|1>)/sqrt2 with dephasing rate Gamma, Prob(+,t) = (1+e^{-Gamma t} cos omega t)/2, so Gamma can be measured from the decay of visibility (ch.3 §3.4.2, pp. 30-31) → decoherence
- Amplitude damping (spontaneous emission): Kraus operators M_0 = diag(1, sqrt(1-p)), M_1 = sqrt(p)|0><1|; excited population decays as e^{-Gamma t} (T1) while off-diagonals decay as e^{-Gamma t/2} (T2), so T2 = 2 T1 for pure amplitude damping; at long times the atom ends in the ground state (ch.3 §3.4.3, pp. 31-32) → decoherence
- Watching the environment (photon detectors) turns the channel into a POVM; detecting no photon still updates the state (M_0 reduces the excited amplitude by e^{-Gamma t/2}), so one can project onto |0> "by not detecting anything" (ch.3 §3.4.3, pp. 33-34) → decoherence
- Master equation: if evolution is Markovian (environment forgets quickly relative to coarse-graining and damping times, (dt)_damp >> (dt)_coarse >> (dt)_env), then rho_dot = L(rho) = -i[H,rho] + sum_a (L_a rho L_a^dagger - 1/2 L_a^dagger L_a rho - 1/2 rho L_a^dagger L_a) (Lindblad equation); the L_a are jump operators; an exactly Markovian description is impossible because information can flow back from the environment (ch.3 §3.5.1-3.5.2, pp. 34-37) → decoherence
- If the environment is monitored, each jump is detected and a pure state stays pure (stochastic Schrodinger equation / quantum trajectories); the average over trajectories reproduces the master equation (ch.3 §3.5.2, p. 38) → decoherence
- Measurement as decoherence: an unrecorded orthogonal measurement turns rho into sum E_a rho E_a, and the system is described by this mixture once entangled with the pointer, whether or not the pointer is observed (ch.3 §3.1.1, p. 8) → decoherence

### Uncertainty and noncommutativity (marginal coverage only)
- Observables A and B give a self-adjoint product only if they commute (ch.2 §2.1, pp. 4-5) → uncertainty-and-noncommutativity
- Rotations about different axes do not commute, giving [J_k, J_l] = i eps_klm J_m, and the Pauli matrices anticommute (ch.2 §2.2.1, p. 11) → uncertainty-and-noncommutativity
- A narrow pointer wavepacket (width Delta x) has velocity spread ~ hbar/(m Delta x), giving a "standard quantum limit" on resolution ~ sqrt(hbar t / m); a heavy pointer makes it negligible (ch.3 §3.1.1, p. 5) → uncertainty-and-noncommutativity
- ⚠️ The Heisenberg uncertainty relation itself is not stated or derived in these two chapters.

### Entanglement
- A bipartite pure state is entangled iff its Schmidt number exceeds one; the chapter's main themes are the properties, implications and uses of such states, which show correlations with no classical analogue (ch.4 §4.1.1, p. 4) → entanglement
- The EPR pair |phi+> = (|00>+|11>)/sqrt2 is "maximally entangled": tracing out one qubit leaves I/2, so any local measurement along any axis gives a random bit and reveals nothing about which state was prepared (ch.4 §4.1.1, p. 4) → entanglement
- The four Bell states |phi+-> = (|00>+-|11>)/sqrt2 and |psi+-> = (|01>+-|10>)/sqrt2 form an orthogonal basis of two qubits, all maximally entangled; they encode a parity bit (phi vs psi) and a phase bit (+ vs -) that neither party can read locally (ch.4 §4.1.1, p. 5) → entanglement
- The Bell basis states are the joint eigenstates of the commuting observables sigma_1(x)sigma_1 (phase bit) and sigma_3(x)sigma_3 (parity bit). Local z-measurements reveal parity but disturb phase; local x-measurements do the reverse; learning both needs a joint (nonlocal) operation (ch.4 §4.1.1, p. 6) → entanglement
- Local unitaries on A or B can turn one maximally entangled state into any other, but cannot change rho_A = rho_B = I/2; maximally entangled states are not a subspace (a superposition of them is typically not maximally entangled) (ch.4 §4.1.1, pp. 5-6) → entanglement
- Entanglement cannot be created locally: with local operations and classical communication (LOCC) no step can raise the Schmidt number of a pure state above one, so a separable state stays separable; LOCC can prepare any separable state (ch.4 §4.6, p. 42) → entanglement
- A mixed state is separable iff it is an ensemble (convex mixture) of product states, i.e. its correlations are purely classical; otherwise it is entangled. A separable mixed state can also have a decomposition into entangled pure states (e.g. I(x)I/4 is both an ensemble of Bell states and of product states) (ch.4 §4.6, pp. 41-42) → entanglement
- For bipartite pure states, "entangled" is equivalent to "violates some Bell inequality"; for mixed states the relation is more subtle (ch.4 §4.3.4, p. 24) → entanglement
- Entanglement is presented as a resource, quantified in "ebits" (shared entangled pairs); resource inequalities 1 ebit + 2 cbits >= 1 qubit and 1 ebit + 1 qubit >= 2 cbits summarise teleportation and dense coding (ch.4 §4.4.2, p. 32, eq. 4.85) → entanglement

### Bell inequalities
- Einstein locality (local realism): in a complete description, an action on system A must not change the description of a spacelike-separated system B. Entanglement conflicts with this because the density matrix of B changes when A is measured; Einstein thus judged QM incomplete (ch.4 §4.1.2, pp. 8-9) → bell-inequalities
- A local hidden-variable theory makes measurement deterministic given hidden variables lambda; randomness is only ignorance, and measuring A just reveals information about lambda, sharpening predictions for B (ch.4 §4.1.2, p. 9) → bell-inequalities
- Three-coins argument: perfect correlation when Alice and Bob uncover coins with the same label, plus locality, forces a joint distribution P(x,y,z) for three coins, and then at least two of three coins agree, giving P_same(1,2)+P_same(2,3)+P_same(1,3) >= 1 (ch.4 §4.2.1, pp. 11-13, eq. 4.18) → bell-inequalities
- Quantum prediction for |psi-> with axes a, b at angle theta: <(sigma.a)(sigma.b)> = -cos theta and P_same = (1-cos theta)/2. For three axes 120 degrees apart (Bob opposite to Alice's) all different-label pairs have P_same = 1/4, so the sum is 3/4 < 1 and the Bell inequality is violated (ch.4 §4.2.2, pp. 14-15, eqs. 4.24-4.29) → bell-inequalities
- The Bell derivation assumes (1) a joint probability distribution for outcomes of all measurements, including mutually exclusive ones (hidden variables) and (2) that Bob's choice does not affect the hidden variables governing Alice (locality). QM correlations are incompatible with the pair. Options: reject counterfactual reasoning (Bohr complementarity), or accept "quantum nonlocality"; if locality is kept, randomness is intrinsic (ch.4 §4.2.2, pp. 16-17) → bell-inequalities
- CHSH inequality: if a, a', b, b' take values +-1 under a joint distribution, C = (a+a')b + (a-a')b' equals +-2 for every assignment, hence |<ab>+<a'b>+<ab'>-<a'b'>| <= 2 (ch.4 §4.3.1, pp. 17-18, eqs. 4.31-4.33) → bell-inequalities
- With |psi->, coplanar axes spaced by successive 45 degrees give <ab>=<a'b>=<ab'>=-1/sqrt2 and <a'b'>=+1/sqrt2, so the CHSH combination has magnitude 2 sqrt2 > 2 (ch.4 §4.3.1, p. 18, eqs. 4.36-4.38) → bell-inequalities
- Tsirelson ("Cirel'son") bound: for Hermitian +-1-valued a, a' commuting with b, b', C^2 = 4I - [a,a'][b,b'], so ||C||_sup <= 2 sqrt2 by the sup-norm submultiplicativity/triangle inequality; the 45-degree configuration saturates it, so no quantum state can violate CHSH by more than 2 sqrt2 (ch.4 §4.3.2, pp. 18-20, eqs. 4.43-4.49) → bell-inequalities
- CHSH game: Charlie sends bits x,y; Alice and Bob (no communication) output a,b and win iff a XOR b = x AND y. Classically (even with shared randomness) the best average win probability over uniform inputs is 3/4; with a shared maximally entangled pair and suitable measurements it is 1/2 + 1/(2 sqrt2) ~ 0.853, which is the Tsirelson maximum (ch.4 §4.3.3, pp. 20-22, eqs. 4.50-4.59) → bell-inequalities
- Separable states cannot violate Bell inequalities (a local hidden-variable model exists; for products <ab> = <a><b>), but every pure entangled two-qubit state alpha|00>+beta|11> (alpha beta > 0) does, as shown by a CHSH setting with violation to linear order in a small angle (ch.4 §4.3.4, pp. 22-24, eqs. 4.60-4.68) → bell-inequalities
- For photon polarization the correlation is cos 2(theta_B - theta_A) rather than cos theta (spin-1 vs spin-1/2); maximal CHSH violation occurs at successive 22.5 degree separations (ch.4 §4.3.5, pp. 24-26, eqs. 4.78-4.80) → bell-inequalities
- Loopholes: the locality loophole (Aspect 1982: analyzer choice made while photons in flight, detectors ~12 m apart, ~40 ns; violated CHSH by 5 standard deviations; later experiments at km separation); the detection loophole (needs detection efficiency above 82.84%; ion-trap experiments have ~100% efficiency but do not address locality); the free-will loophole (settings correlated with hidden variables). As of this 2001 text no experiment closed locality and detection loopholes simultaneously (ch.4 §4.3.6, pp. 26-27) → bell-inequalities
- ⚠️ The "no experiment closes both loopholes" statement reflects the 2001 version of the chapter and may be outdated; the source does not say what happened later. → bell-inequalities

### Quantum teleportation
- Setting: Alice holds an unknown qubit |psi> and shares |phi+>_AB with Bob; only a classical channel is available. Measure-and-send-classical strategies reach average fidelity at most 2/3 (shown for one-axis measurement; the text claims no measure-and-send protocol beats 2/3, proof deferred to ch.5) (ch.4 §4.4.2, p. 30, eq. 4.81) → quantum-teleportation
- Protocol: Alice does a Bell measurement on her unknown qubit C and her half A (measuring sigma_1(x)sigma_1 and sigma_3(x)sigma_3), sends the 2-bit outcome; Bob applies I, sigma_1, sigma_2 or sigma_3 (for outcomes phi+, psi+, psi-, phi-) and obtains a perfect copy, fidelity 1 (ch.4 §4.4.2, pp. 30-31, eq. 4.83) → quantum-teleportation
- Why it works: expanding |psi>_C|phi+>_AB in the Bell basis of CA gives four terms of weight 1/2, each with Bob's qubit in sigma|psi> for a known Pauli sigma; all four outcomes are equally likely (ch.4 §4.4.2, p. 31, eq. 4.84) → quantum-teleportation
- The Bell outcome is completely random, so neither Alice nor Bob learns anything about |psi>, and the two classical bits do not themselves carry the state; this is consistent because acquiring information would disturb it. The text calls this "puzzling", since the entangled pair was prepared long before (ch.4 §4.4.2, pp. 31-32) → quantum-teleportation
- Consistency with no-cloning: a copy appears at Bob's side, but the original is destroyed by Alice's measurement before the copy exists (ch.4 §4.4.2, p. 32) → quantum-teleportation
- Resource accounting: teleporting one qubit consumes one ebit and two classical bits (1 ebit + 2 cbits >= 1 qubit); without ebits fidelity is at most 2/3 (ch.4 §4.4.2, p. 32, eq. 4.85) → quantum-teleportation
- Why relativity is not violated: Bob's state is a Pauli-rotated version of |psi> and he cannot undo it until Alice's two classical bits arrive; the outcome is random, so before the message Bob's qubit carries no information (ch.4 §4.4.2, pp. 31-32). ⚠️ The chapter does not state the relativity point in these words; this is assembled from the random outcome, the need for the classical message, and the no-signalling discussion at ch.4 §4.1.2, p. 8 / ch.2 §2.5.3 → quantum-teleportation
- General form: for dimension N, with |Phi> = sum_i |i>|i>/sqrt N, <Phi|_CA |Phi>_AB = T_BC / N where T is a "transfer operator" mapping a state in C to the identical state in B (basis dependent). Any maximally entangled state is (U (x) I)|Phi>, so Bob corrects with the unitary determined by Alice's outcome; teleportation with F = 1 requires a maximally entangled resource (ch.4 §4.4.3, pp. 32-34, eqs. 4.86-4.95) → quantum-teleportation
- Alice's measurement need not be orthogonal: any POVM whose elements are proportional to projectors on maximally entangled states |Phi(U_a)> works. If the shared state is twisted, |Phi(V^T)>, Bob's state after outcome a is V U_a^{-1}|psi> and he applies U_a V^{-1} (ch.4 §4.4.3, pp. 34-35, eqs. 4.96-4.98) → quantum-teleportation
- Quantum software: if the shared state is the certified "software" |Phi(V^T)> = (I (x) V)|Phi>, teleportation applies the gate V to |psi> up to the correction V U_a V^{-1}; this is useful in fault-tolerant computation (ch.8) because preparing and verifying a known state is easier than applying a gate to an unknown state, and each use consumes one copy (ch.4 §4.4.4, pp. 35-36) → quantum-teleportation

### Superdense coding
- Holevo bound (special case, derivation deferred to ch.5): sending qubits one at a time without pre-shared entanglement carries at most one classical bit per qubit, even if qubits are entangled with each other (ch.4 §4.4.1, p. 28) → superdense-coding
- Protocol: Alice and Bob share |phi+>_AB. Alice applies I, sigma_1, sigma_2 or sigma_3 to her half, producing four mutually orthogonal states (phi+, psi+, psi- up to phase, phi-), sends her qubit to Bob, who does a Bell-basis measurement on the pair and reads 2 classical bits from one transmitted qubit (ch.4 §4.4.1, pp. 28-29) → superdense-coding
- Confidentiality: the transmitted qubit alone has density matrix I/2 and carries no information; all information is in the correlations between A and B, so an eavesdropper must capture both qubits (jamming is still possible) (ch.4 §4.4.1, p. 29) → superdense-coding
- Fairness caveat: one can regard it as two qubit transmissions overall (half the pair was sent earlier), consistent with the Holevo bound of 2 bits for two qubits; the odd features are that the first qubit was sent before the message was known and that each qubit alone carries nothing (ch.4 §4.4.1, p. 29) → superdense-coding
- Resource inequality: 1 ebit + 1 qubit >= 2 cbits; entanglement is essential because without ebits a qubit is worth only one cbit (ch.4 §4.4.2, p. 32, eq. 4.85) → superdense-coding
- Dense coding and teleportation are presented as converses (qubit gives two cbits vs two cbits give a qubit), both consuming one ebit (ch.4 §4.4.2, p. 30) → superdense-coding

### Two-qubit gates
- CNOT (reversible XOR): |a,b> -> |a, a XOR b>; control a, target b; it flips the target iff control is 1, and CNOT^2 = identity (ch.4 §4.1.1, p. 7, eqs. 4.11-4.12; ch.5 §5.2.2, p. 15, eq. 5.28) → two-qubit-gates
- General notation Lambda(G) = |0><0| (x) I + |1><1| (x) G is the two-qubit gate applying a single-qubit G to the target when the control is |1>; CNOT = Lambda(X) (ch.5 §5.4.3 item (3), p. 35, eq. 5.85) → two-qubit-gates
- Hadamard on the first qubit followed by CNOT maps the computational basis to the Bell basis (|00> -> phi+, |01> -> psi+, |10> -> phi-, |11> -> psi-); running it backwards then measuring both bits identifies the Bell state. CNOT is the "nonlocal" part that creates or removes entanglement (ch.4 §4.1.1, pp. 6-8, eq. 4.13) → two-qubit-gates
- Three CNOTs implement a SWAP of two (qu)bits, (x,y) -> (y,x) (ch.5 §5.2.2, p. 16, eq. 5.30). SWAP lets one bring distant qubits together and return them, so geometric locality constraints do not change complexity much (ch.5 §5.4.1, pp. 33-34) → two-qubit-gates
- An entangling two-qubit gate maps some product state to a non-product state. Local gates A(x)B, SWAP, and gates locally equivalent to SWAP, (A(x)B) SWAP (C(x)D), are the only non-entangling ones; every other two-qubit unitary is entangling and, with arbitrary single-qubit gates, universal (ch.5 §5.4.1 item (1), p. 34, eq. 5.82) → two-qubit-gates
- Lambda^2(V) for any single-qubit V (with V = U^2) can be built from Lambda(U), Lambda(U-dagger) and CNOT gates (the exponent of U applied is y - (x XOR y) + x = 2xy); this is the key step for building n-qubit controlled gates from two-qubit gates (ch.5 §5.4.2, p. 38, eqs. 5.94-5.95) → two-qubit-gates
- A controlled-phase gate Lambda(S) = diag(1,1,1,i) acts symmetrically on the two qubits despite the asymmetric circuit symbol (ch.5 exercise 5.3, p. 48) → two-qubit-gates

### Single-qubit gates (only the new points)
- Hadamard H = (sigma_1+sigma_3)/sqrt2, H^2 = I, with H sigma_1 H = sigma_3 and H sigma_3 H = sigma_1; up to phase it is a pi rotation about the axis (x+z)/sqrt2 that swaps x and z (ch.4 §4.1.1, p. 7, eqs. 4.7-4.10) → single-qubit-gates
- S and T are z-axis rotations on the Bloch sphere: S = exp(-i pi Z/4) (rotation by pi/2) and T = exp(-i pi Z/8) (rotation by pi/4). ⚠️ In the exercise (ch.5 ex. 5.3, p. 47) S is instead written as diag(1,i), which differs from the main-text matrix by a global phase and a Z-rotation convention; do not mix the two forms (ch.5 §5.4.3, p. 35, eq. 5.84) → single-qubit-gates

### Quantum circuits
- Model: n qubits start in |00...0>, a circuit of finitely many gates (each a unitary on a fixed small number of qubits) is applied, then some or all qubits are measured in the {|0>,|1>} basis; the outcome is the result (ch.5 §5.3, pp. 22-23) → quantum-circuits
- Design assumptions: a preferred tensor-product decomposition into subsystems with gates acting on few at a time (rooted in locality); a finite gate set (needed later because only discrete gates can be protected by fault tolerance); unitary gates and orthogonal measurements suffice since TPCP maps and POVMs can be simulated on extended systems (ch.5 §5.3 items (1)-(3), p. 23) → quantum-circuits
- Inputs and measurements must be easy: a product input state is cheap to prepare, while a complicated entangled input could hide the difficulty of the algorithm; any collective measurement reduces to a unitary followed by a standard-basis measurement, which costs gates; measurements can always be postponed to the end (ch.5 §5.3 items (4)-(6), pp. 23-24) → quantum-circuits
- A classical reversible gate (a permutation of bit strings) is a unitary mapping one product basis to another, so classical reversible computing is a special case of quantum circuits and the final measurement is then deterministic (ch.5 §5.3, p. 24, eqs. 5.48-5.50) → quantum-circuits
- Toffoli (Lambda^2(X), (x,y,z) -> (x,y,z XOR xy)) is nonlinear; with fixed ancilla inputs it gives NOT, AND (z=0) and COPY, so it is universal for Boolean logic and for any reversible function (with NOT and one scratch bit). One- and two-bit reversible gates are all affine (x -> Mx + a) so cannot be universal (ch.5 §5.2.2, pp. 15-19, eqs. 5.31-5.34) → quantum-circuits
- Lambda^3(X) cannot be built from Toffolis on only four bits because it is an odd permutation, while Toffoli on any three of four bits is even, so one scratch bit is needed (ch.5 §5.2.2, p. 18) → quantum-circuits
- Computation can be made garbage-free by saving junk bits, copying the output, then running the circuit backward (uncomputing); this roughly doubles gate count and needs memory ~ gate count, and the pebble game trades time and space (about 3^n steps and n+1 pebbles for 2^n steps) (ch.5 §5.2.3, pp. 20-22) → quantum-circuits
- Accuracy: if each real gate differs from the ideal by at most epsilon in the sup norm, the state error after T gates is at most T*epsilon (worst case errors add coherently); the probability distribution error (L1/2) is bounded by the same T*epsilon (ch.5 §5.3.1, pp. 26-28, eqs. 5.57-5.68) → quantum-circuits
- To keep success probability 1/2 + delta' positive for an ideal circuit with success 1/2 + delta, per-gate error must satisfy epsilon < delta/T, i.e. accuracy must improve linearly with circuit size; fault tolerance is claimed to deliver logical gates with accuracy ~1/T from constant-accuracy physical gates (ch.5 §5.3.1, p. 29) → quantum-circuits
- BQP is defined with polynomial-size uniform quantum circuit families; it contains BPP (a Hadamard plus measurement yields a random bit), is believed larger, but BPP != BQP is unproven, since proving it would imply BPP != PSPACE (ch.5 §5.3, pp. 24-26) → quantum-circuits
- BQP is in PSPACE: p(a) = |<a|U|0>|^2 is a sum over 2^{n(T-1)} paths ("path integral") computed term by term with memory ~ nT log T bits, but exponential time (ch.5 §5.3.2, pp. 29-31, eqs. 5.69-5.72) → quantum-circuits
- Counting argument: reaching all of U(2^n) to accuracy delta needs number of circuits >= (C/delta)^{N^2} (N=2^n) while circuits of T gates number at most poly(n)^T, so T must be exponential (about 2^{2n} log(C/delta)/log poly(n)); most unitaries, and most n-qubit states, are out of reach of polynomial-size circuits (ch.5 §5.3.3, pp. 31-33, eqs. 5.76-5.79) → quantum-circuits
- Because circuits are countable and unitaries a continuum, finite circuits can only approximate arbitrary unitaries (ch.5 §5.3.3, pp. 31-32) → quantum-circuits

### Universal gate sets
- A finite gate set G (each gate on at most k qubits) is universal if circuits over G are dense in U(2^n) up to a global phase: for every V and delta there is a circuit with ||V~ - e^{i phi} V||_sup <= delta (ch.5 §5.4.1, p. 34, eq. 5.81) → universal-gate-sets
- Variants: encoded universality (dense only in a subgroup U(N) with N large); exact universality (uncountable gate sets reach any unitary exactly); generic universality (almost every two-qubit gate is universal, excluding a measure-zero set of U(4)) (ch.5 §5.4.1, pp. 34-35) → universal-gate-sets
- Particular finite universal sets stated (proved in exercises): {H, Lambda(S)}, {H, T, CNOT}, {H, S, Toffoli} (ch.5 §5.4.1 item (3), p. 35, eq. 5.83). ⚠️ The later text p. 42 writes the second as {CNOT, H, T}; same set. → universal-gate-sets
- Two-qubit gates are exactly universal, in outline: (i) any U in U(N) factors into N(N-1)/2 "2x2" unitaries acting in the span of two basis states, by successively mapping |0>, |1>, ... (ch.5 §5.4.2, pp. 36-38, eqs. 5.90-5.93); (ii) a 2x2 unitary is Sigma^{-1} Lambda^{n-1}(V) Sigma, with Sigma a permutation built from Lambda^{n-1}(X) and NOT, and Lambda^{n-1}(V) from two-qubit gates recursively (ch.5 §5.4.2, pp. 38-39, eq. 5.98) → universal-gate-sets
- A stronger result obtained: the set {Lambda(U) : U any single-qubit gate} is exactly universal; consequently CNOT plus arbitrary single-qubit gates is exactly universal (exercise 5.2) (ch.5 §5.4.2, p. 39; §5.4.1, p. 34) → universal-gate-sets
- Dense circle argument: powers of a rotation by an irrational fraction alpha of the circle are dense (pigeonhole: two points within epsilon, so a multiple r*alpha is within epsilon, and its multiples fill the interval); for a generic U(N) element, powers fill the torus U(1)^N, but the required power k can be huge, so commuting gates are inefficient (ch.5 §5.4.3, pp. 39-40, eqs. 5.99-5.101) → universal-gate-sets
- Two noncommuting generic gates reach the whole Lie algebra generated: limits give e^{i(alpha A + beta B)} (Trotter) and e^{-[A,B]} (group commutator), so two generic elements of SU(2) reach all of SU(2) (since [Z, X~] ~ iY) and two generic two-qubit gates reach SU(4); a single generic two-qubit gate suffices if combined with its SWAP-conjugate (apply it to either qubit order) (ch.5 §5.4.3, pp. 41-42, eqs. 5.102-5.104) → universal-gate-sets
- Nongeneric gates can be universal: {CNOT, H, T} with T a pi/4 Bloch rotation is universal, but replacing T by T^2 (pi/2 rotation) leaves only a finite subgroup of SU(2) (the cube's symmetry group), so it is not universal; the only finite nonabelian subgroups of SO(3) are polygon and platonic-solid symmetry groups (ch.5 §5.4.3, p. 42) → universal-gate-sets
- Solovay-Kitaev: for a gate set closed under inverses, any k-qubit unitary (k fixed) can be approximated to accuracy epsilon with polylog(1/epsilon) gates, and the circuit can be found classically in polylog(1/epsilon) time. Hence one universal set simulates another with polylog overhead and BQP is gate-set independent (ch.5 §5.4.1 item (4), pp. 35-36; §5.4.4, pp. 42-43) → universal-gate-sets
- Solovay-Kitaev proof sketch: if R is an epsilon-net of U(N) closed under inverse, build W as a group commutator e^{iB~}e^{iC~}e^{-iB~}e^{-iC~} of four elements with [B,C] = -iA to approximate U U~^{-1} = e^{iA}; then W U~ (5 gates) is an epsilon'-net with epsilon' = C epsilon^{3/2}. The O(epsilon) errors cancel in the commutator, which needs inverses to be exact (ch.5 §5.4.4, pp. 43-45, eqs. 5.105-5.111) → universal-gate-sets
- Solovay-Kitaev scaling from the recursion: circuit size grows as [log(1/epsilon)]^3.97 and classical cost as [log(1/epsilon)]^2.71; for the specific set {H, T} one can do better, O(log(1/epsilon)) gates for any single-qubit unitary (ch.5 §5.4.4, pp. 44-45, eqs. 5.109-5.112) → universal-gate-sets
- Consequence for efficiency: approximating T ideal gates from set G1 to total constant accuracy using set G2 needs each gate to accuracy epsilon/T and totals O(T polylog T) gates; the huge size of U(2^n) comes from being able to act on only a constant number of qubits at a time, not from a discrete gate set (ch.5 §5.4.1, p. 36, eq. 5.87) → universal-gate-sets

### No-cloning
- Nonorthogonal states cannot be distinguished without disturbing them; this is the basis of QKD security (ch.4 §4.5.2, p. 39) → no-cloning
- Disturbance-free probe argument: if a unitary U on H (x) H_E leaves two nonorthogonal states undisturbed, |psi>|0> -> |psi>|e> and |phi>|0> -> |phi>|f>, then unitarity forces <psi|phi> = <psi|phi><e|f>, so |e> = |f> and Eve's system carries no information distinguishing them (ch.4 §4.5.2, pp. 39-40, eqs. 4.104-4.105) → no-cloning
- Perfect copying of a qubit would let one make many copies and measure <sigma_1>, <sigma_2>, <sigma_3> to find the density matrix, contradicting the above; so a perfect "quantum Xerox machine" cannot exist (ch.4 §4.5.2, p. 40) → no-cloning
- Orthogonal states can be copied, e.g. the CNOT-like map |0>|0> -> |0>|0>, |1>|0> -> |1>|1> (classical-style copying), but applied to a|0>+b|1> it produces the entangled state a|00>+b|11>, not |psi>|psi> (ch.4 §4.5.2, pp. 40-41, eqs. 4.106-4.107) → no-cloning
- General no-cloning theorem: for the most general cloning unitary |psi>|0>|0> -> |psi>|psi>|e>, |phi>|0>|0> -> |phi>|phi>|f> (with an extra ancilla F), unitarity gives <psi|phi> = <psi|phi>^2 <e|f>, hence |<psi|phi>| = 1 whenever <psi|phi> != 0: no unitary machine can copy two distinct nonorthogonal states (ch.4 §4.5.2, p. 41, eqs. 4.108-4.111) → no-cloning
- The proof relies only on unitarity (linearity plus inner-product preservation); it covers any larger Hilbert space with ancillas, since the extra system F is allowed (ch.4 §4.5.2, p. 41) → no-cloning
- Link to teleportation: the teleported copy appears only after the original is destroyed by Alice's Bell measurement, so teleportation does not clone (ch.4 §4.4.2, p. 32) → no-cloning
- Application: in BB84 (Alice sends one of |up_z>,|down_z>,|up_x>,|down_x>) any eavesdropping that leaves the states undisturbed yields identical states in Eve's system and hence no key information; copies of the orthogonal pair alone would be allowed (ch.4 §4.5.1-4.5.2, pp. 38-40) → no-cloning

### Multi-qubit states (only the new points)
- An n-qubit Hilbert space has dimension 2^n; classical simulation difficulty comes from the huge dimension, and typical states in it can never be prepared with reasonable resources (ch.5 §5.3, pp. 25-26, 33) → multi-qubit-states
- A general two-qubit pure state can be written alpha|00>+beta|11> in the Schmidt basis, with real nonnegative alpha, beta after phase conventions; <sigma_1(x)sigma_1> = 2 alpha beta, <sigma_3(x)sigma_3> = 1 (ch.4 §4.3.4, p. 23, eqs. 4.61-4.64) → multi-qubit-states
