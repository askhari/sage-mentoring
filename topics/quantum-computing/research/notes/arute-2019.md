## Quantum advantage experiment (task, XEB, verification, classical cost)

- The authors define a "supremacy" demonstration as needing a well-defined computational task, a programmable device, and a runtime gap that grows with problem size so that it is impractical for a supercomputer using any known classical algorithm. They accept that the classical benchmark is a moving target (XI, pp. 59-60) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- The task is random circuit sampling (RCS): run a random circuit on n qubits for m cycles and sample output bitstrings. The supremacy circuits use n = 53 qubits and m = 20 cycles (VII A, p. 27; Table IV, p. 32) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Each cycle = one random single-qubit gate per qubit (chosen from sqrt(X), sqrt(Y), sqrt(W), never repeating the previous gate on that qubit) followed by a layer of two-qubit gates; two-qubit gates are not random but fixed by pair and cycle. The 53-qubit, 20-cycle circuit has 1113 single-qubit and 430 two-qubit gates, 35 of which cross the main cut used by the classical simulator (VII C, E, p. 27-28; Table IV, p. 32) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- The two-qubit gate used is fSim(theta ~ pi/2, phi ~ pi/6), a partial iSWAP with a conditional phase. It is chosen because it arises naturally from the transmon interaction and is harder to simulate than CZ (a factor of 2 per the authors) (VI C 1, p. 15-16; VII E, p. 28) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Coupler activation patterns for supremacy circuits (sequence ABCDCDAB) are designed to prevent "wedges", which would let the classical Schrödinger-Feynman simulator cut cost by a factor of 4 each; the easier "verifiable" circuits use pattern EFGH, which allows wedges (VII G 2, p. 31; X D 3, p. 54) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- XEB estimates the fidelity F of a random circuit from the sampled bitstrings alone: simulate classically the ideal probability p_s(q) of each measured bitstring and average a function of it. Linear XEB uses D*p_s(q) - 1 (D = 2^n), and the log version uses log p_s(q); no need to reconstruct the whole output distribution (IV, p. 5-7) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Basis of XEB: output probabilities of a random circuit follow the Porter-Thomas ("speckle") distribution, and errors flatten this speckle. For a depolarizing model, linear XEB gives F = <D p_s(q)> - 1; the log form gives F = <log(D p_s)> + Euler's gamma (IV B, Eqs. 26-28, p. 8) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Sanity limits: uniformly random bitstrings give F_XEB = 0; sampling from the ideal distribution gives F_XEB = 1 (IV C, p. 8-9) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Linear XEB has lower variance than log XEB when F < 0.32 (the regime of the experiment); the "heavy output generation" (HOG) estimator always has higher variance than XEB (IV B, p. 8) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Why XEB is limited: it needs classical simulation of the circuit, so it cannot be applied directly to the hardest circuits. The authors therefore estimate the fidelity of hard circuits from easier variants: "patch" circuits (remove all gates along a cut, fidelity ~ product of two halves) and "elided" circuits (remove only some cut gates), plus "full" circuits up to ~43 qubits (VII G, p. 30-31; VIII A-C, p. 32-34) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Validation of the variants: patch and full circuits agree to about 5% typically (max 10%) up to 38 qubits at 14 cycles; elided/full fidelity ratio averages 1.01 with a 5% standard deviation (VIII B-C, p. 33-34) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Full-circuit XEB was classically computed with a Schrödinger simulator (qsim) for 12-37 qubits, in Google Cloud at 38, and up to 43 qubits on the Jülich supercomputers (VIII A, p. 32-33; X C, p. 49-50). Full XEB on the verifiable circuits (n up to 53) was done at depth 14 with a hybrid Schrödinger-Feynman approach (X D 3, p. 54) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Headline result: the mean linear-XEB fidelity of ten 53-qubit, 20-cycle circuits is (2.24 +/- 0.18 stat. +/- 0.10 syst.) x 10^-3 (about 0.2%); the log-XEB estimate is 2.34 x 10^-3 (VIII G-I, p. 39-41). Roughly 3 million bitstrings were sampled per circuit (VIII F, p. 38) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Null hypothesis: F <= 10^-3 (fidelity at which classical noisy sampling is estimated to need ~5000 years on 10^6 cores while a quantum computer needs under an hour). It is rejected at 6 sigma. The authors argue systematic drift (multiplicative) cannot create a false positive, so 6 sigma is conservative (VIII I, p. 41-42) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Goodness-of-fit: a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test of the 53-qubit data rejects F = 0 (p < 2.2e-16 on the combined 30 M bitstrings) and is consistent with the estimated F (p = 0.66 linear, 0.95 log) (VIII F, Table V, p. 37-39) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Estimated classical cost for sampling at the experiment's fidelity (qsimh, Schrödinger-Feynman, extrapolated to 10^6 cores from 1000 sample paths; avg 246 s per path): 53 qubits, 12 cycles: 2 hours (F 1.4%); 14: 2 weeks; 16: ~4 years; 18: ~175 years; 20 cycles: ~10,000 years (F 0.2%) (X F, Table XI, p. 56-57; ⚠️ the 10,000-year figure is an extrapolation by the authors, not a measured run) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Quantum side: acquiring 1M samples took about 200 s; energy ~5x10^6 J (~1 kWh) at ~26 kW worst-case total apparatus power. The authors claim ~7 orders of magnitude less energy than the qFlex simulation on the Summit supercomputer (X H, p. 59). The duty cycle was as low as 2% (control communication dominated) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- The classical cost scales roughly linearly with target fidelity F (cost reduced by ~1/F), because one sums only a fraction F of Feynman paths; speedup from unbalanced Schmidt coefficients is bounded to well under an order of magnitude here (X D, p. 50-53; X F). Theorem 1 argues, under complexity assumptions (global white noise, polynomial hierarchy), that the linear scaling in F is essentially optimal (XI, p. 61-62) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Alternative classical methods tested: qFlex tensor network on Summit (12 cycles, 1M bitstrings at F 0.5%: 1.29 hours; 14 cycles: estimated 68 days for F 0.5%, 1.1 years for 3M samples at F 1%; 16+ cycles impractical because of memory); variable elimination with projected variables (Ref. 72) judged out of reach at m >= 16 (X B, Table VII, p. 45-46; X E, p. 54-56) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- The authors address low-fidelity spoofing proposals based on Fourier-component truncation: they say these reduce to uniform random sampling, which yields F_XEB = 0, while the experiment obtained F_XEB >= 0.1% (X D, p. 50) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Interpretation of the result: it is a quantum-supremacy claim for b-Heavy Output Generation with b = 1 + F. The sampling itself cannot be verified directly in practice for distributions over > 2^50 outcomes, so XEB serves as the proxy (XI B, p. 61-62) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- Reproducibility and stability: patch fidelity at 53 qubits/16 cycles drifted over 17.4 h without recalibration (linear fit slope about -6.9e-5 per hour on a base of 5.5e-3). The authors attribute fluctuations mostly to two-level-system (TLS) defects (VIII H, p. 40-42) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- The authors state that the classical benchmark is a moving target and that the formal complexity results are asymptotic; near-term claims therefore rest on comparisons with optimized classical algorithms (XI, p. 60) → quantum-advantage-experiments
- ⚠️ Later classical-simulation work (not in this source) challenged the 10,000-year estimate; this source's estimates are from late 2019 and should be paired with a rebuttal source.

## Sycamore hardware (superconducting qubits)

- The Sycamore chip has 142 transmons: 54 qubits with individual microwave drive, flux control and readout, and 88 transmons used as tunable couplers kept in the ground state. One qubit was broken, so 53 are used (II, p. 2; VII A) → superconducting-qubits
- Qubits are DC SQUIDs between two metal islands (transmon regime), tuned by on-chip flux bias lines. Typical tuning range is < 100 MHz, operating at 5-7 GHz (II, p. 2; VI B, p. 12) → superconducting-qubits
- Fabrication: aluminium on high-resistivity silicon, two dies joined with indium bump bonding, 14 lithography layers (optical and e-beam); packaged with a superconducting Al cap and a mu-metal magnetic shield (II, p. 2-3) → superconducting-qubits
- Tunable coupler architecture: each neighbouring pair is coupled by a direct capacitance and by a coupler (second-order virtual process of opposite sign), so the net coupling can be tuned between +5 and -40 MHz and set to zero when idle; "on" coupling is about -20 MHz in the experiment. Reasons given: strong coupling when the gate is on (fast), near-zero residual coupling otherwise (fewer control errors), less flux sensitivity (qubits near max frequency), reduced measurement crosstalk. Cost: more wiring and control signals (I, p. 2; II, p. 2) → superconducting-qubits
- Entangling gate physics: resonant qubits swap |01> and |10> (XX+YY interaction); interaction of |11> with higher levels |02>/|20> yields a conditional phase (ZZ). Gate time 12 ns, chosen as a trade-off between decoherence (too slow) and leakage to higher levels (too fast) (I, p. 2; VI C 1, p. 15) → superconducting-qubits
- Typical device parameters (Table II, p. 22; median over 53 qubits, 86 pairs): max frequency 6.924 GHz, idle 6.661 GHz, anharmonicity -208 MHz, T1 at idle 15.54 microseconds (mean 16.04), resonator linewidth 0.64 MHz, qubit-resonator coupling 72.3 MHz → superconducting-qubits
- Readout: dispersive. The qubit state shifts a far-detuned resonator frequency; a reflected probe's phase is read out through a Josephson parametric amplifier (IMPA) at the mixing chamber, a 3 K HEMT, and room-temperature electronics (~100 dB total gain). Probe populates the resonator with only a few photons to avoid induced transitions. Nine multiplexed groups of 6 qubits with shared Purcell filters (III B, p. 3-5) → superconducting-qubits
- Control stack: 54 microwave XY lines, 54 qubit flux lines, 88 coupler flux lines, and 9 readout lines; custom 14-bit 1 GS/s DAC modules, with 4 chassis (each ~15 qubits) driving the chip. Wiring and electronics are a scaling burden (III A, p. 3; Figs. S2-S3) → superconducting-qubits
- The chip sits at the mixing-chamber stage of a dilution refrigerator (~10 mK in Fig. S3); power ~10 kW direct (plus ~10 kW cooling water) and ~3 kW electronics, ~26 kW total worst case (X H, p. 59; Fig. S3) → superconducting-qubits
- Calibration is a heavy burden: >100 parameters per qubit, automated with a graph ("Optimus"); bring-up ~36 hours after first cooldown and ~4 hours per day to maintain. Two-level-system (TLS) defects make T1 vary strongly with frequency, so operating frequencies are chosen by an optimizer ("Snake") balancing relaxation, dephasing, leakage, crosstalk (VI A-B, p. 11-14) → superconducting-qubits
- Design targets: 0.1% two-qubit error for error correction; supremacy needs only 0.3-0.6%. For decoherence-limited errors, 0.1% needs coherence/gate time ratio ~1000 (example 25 microseconds coherence with 25 ns gate). The architecture is also meant for surface-code experiments (I, p. 2) → superconducting-qubits
- Mixing in a flux-tunable design: operating the qubit near its max frequency reduces 1/f flux-noise dephasing (I, p. 2; VI B 4, p. 14) → superconducting-qubits

## Error rates, benchmarking, coherence and fidelity

- Reported error numbers (Table II, p. 22; medians, Pauli errors; Sycamore, 2019): single-qubit gate (XEB) 0.13% isolated / 0.14% simultaneous; single-qubit RB 0.19% (0.15% with pi/2-only gate set; RB taken later); two-qubit gate (XEB, per cycle with single-qubit gates) e2c 0.64% isolated / 0.89% simultaneous; two-qubit gate alone e2 0.30% isolated / 0.60% simultaneous; readout error 2.83% isolated / 3.50% simultaneous (mean over both states) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Two-qubit gates are therefore more error-prone than single-qubit gates in this device: median 0.30-0.60% (two-qubit gate) versus 0.13-0.14% (single-qubit gate), about 2-4x worse; readout (~3-3.5%) is worse still. Two-qubit gates are also slower: 12 ns (VI C 1) – ⚠️ the single-qubit gate duration is not given in this text → coherence-and-fidelity
- Readout identification error per qubit: state |0> 0.97% isolated, 1.8% simultaneous; state |1> 4.5% isolated, 5.1% simultaneous. The authors conjecture thermal excitation causes the |0> error and T1 decay during readout the |1> error; the increase under simultaneous readout is not understood (VI D 2-3, p. 20-21) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Reading 53 qubits at once of 150 random bitstrings (3000 trials each): only 13.6% of trials recovered the exact prepared string; the most common error is one lost excitation (VI D 3, p. 20) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Running gates in parallel degrades them: two-qubit XEB error rose by about 0.003 (0.3 percentage points), mostly from purity (incoherent) error, attributed to stray interactions and control crosstalk (VI C 5, p. 20; Table II) → coherence-and-fidelity
- XEB for gates: a two-qubit XEB cycle (two random single-qubit gates + the two-qubit gate) is repeated for m cycles; fidelity decays exponentially as p_c^m, like randomized benchmarking (RB), and so is independent of state-preparation and measurement (SPAM) errors (IV A, p. 7; VI C 2, p. 16) → coherence-and-fidelity
- RB cross-check: the sum of single-qubit RB errors (0.09% and 0.07%) and CZ interleaved RB error (0.41%) predicts a two-qubit XEB cycle error of 0.57%; measured XEB 0.59% (VI C 3, Fig. S17, p. 16-18) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Metric definitions: RB signal = p(1-1/D)+1/D, XEB signal = p; Pauli error e_P = (1-p)(1-1/D^2); the paper recommends reporting Pauli error (dimension-independent) rather than the common RB average fidelity, whose D-dependent factor flatters the value (V, p. 10-11, Table I). For 0.1% Pauli error, average error is 0.067% (1 qubit) and 0.08% (2 qubits) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Decoherence-limited estimate: for a gate of length t_g with energy relaxation time T1, the average error is ~t_g/(3 T1) and the Pauli error ~t_g/(2 T1) (V, p. 10) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Decoherence vs control error: speckle purity benchmarking (SPB) extracts state purity from raw XEB data (variance of probabilities vs the Porter-Thomas variance), so XEB error minus purity loss gives control (coherent) error. Initial two-qubit gate error split about half control, half decoherence; after optimizing the unitary model the XEB error (0.62%) is near the purity (decoherence) limit (0.54%) (VI C 2, 4, p. 16-19, Figs. S15, S18) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Single-qubit pi pulses are slightly worse than pi/2 pulses, which the authors attribute to reflections in the microwave environment (VI C 3, p. 17-18) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Digital error model test: the fidelity of a whole circuit is predicted well (within 10-20%) by multiplying the (1 - Pauli error) of each gate and each qubit's readout, for tens of qubits and ~1000 gates. The authors say this supports the independent-error assumption behind error correction (VIII E, Eq. 77, p. 35-36) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Crosstalk, TLS and leakage as error sources: flux/control crosstalk, readout-resonator cross-coupling (needing correlated discrimination on 8 qubits), and TLS defects dominate fluctuations; gate time is a compromise between decoherence and leakage (VI D 1, p. 20; VIII H, p. 40-41; VI C 1, p. 15) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Total circuit fidelity is the product of component fidelities, which is why it falls exponentially with qubit and gate count: F_XEB goes down to ~0.2% at 53 qubits, 20 cycles (VIII I; Fig. S31) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Unitary calibration precision degraded by less than a factor of 2 going from 2 to 50 qubits (fidelity 9e-3 to 5e-3 at 50 qubits for generic vs fitted unitaries) (VIII D, p. 35) → coherence-and-fidelity
- Quantum volume is only mentioned in passing: the HOG test is related to a definition of quantum volume (IV B, p. 8) → coherence-and-fidelity
